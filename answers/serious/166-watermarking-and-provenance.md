# Watermarking and provenance

Two different questions get asked as one:

* **Detection**: given this image, was it generated? (No reliable general answer.)
* **Provenance**: given this image, what is its documented history? (Tractable, and the one worth
  building.)

Getting these confused produces the recurring cycle of "AI detector" tools that are wrong often
enough to ruin someone's career.

## Why post-hoc detection does not work

A classifier trained to spot generated images works on the generators it saw, degrades on new ones,
and fails on the ones that matter — a generated image that has been cropped, re-encoded, printed
and re-photographed, or lightly edited. Meanwhile the **false positive rate is the whole problem**:
at 99% accuracy on a corpus that is 1% generated, most positives are wrong. Deployed against
students or journalists, that is not a metric, it is an accusation.

Treat any general-purpose "is this AI" detector as unreliable, and say so when asked.

## Watermarking: the three-way trade

An embedded signal in the output, ideally invisible and detectable by whoever holds the key.

```
                    ROBUSTNESS
                   (survives crop, re-encode, screenshot, filter)
                        /\
                       /  \
                      /    \   pick two, roughly
                     /______\
       IMPERCEPTIBILITY      CAPACITY
    (no visible artefact)   (how many bits: "generated" vs
                             "generated by X on date Y")
```

* **Image watermarking** (SynthID-style) embeds in the latent or pixel space; survives mild edits,
  degrades under aggressive transformation, and can be removed by a determined adversary.
* **Audio watermarking** is comparatively robust — audio has more redundancy to hide in — and
  matters most, because voice cloning (questions 138, 153) is the highest-harm case.
* **Text watermarking** biases token sampling toward a keyed subset. It works statistically over
  enough tokens and is defeated by paraphrase, which is cheap.

Watermarking is a **deterrent and an audit trail**, not a defence against an adversary. Designing as
if it were the latter is the error.

## Provenance: C2PA and content credentials

Instead of asking what the pixels look like, attach a **signed manifest** recording how the asset
was made and edited, cryptographically bound to the content and chained across edits.

Strengths: verifiable, expressive (camera capture, edit history, generation claims), and it works
for *authentic* media too — which is the underrated half. The scarce thing in five years is not
proof that something was generated; it is proof that something was **not**.

Weaknesses: metadata is stripped by nearly every platform upload path; it requires ecosystem
adoption from capture device to display surface; and absence of a manifest proves nothing, so it
cannot be used as evidence of fakery.

## What to actually do

* **Watermark and sign everything you generate**, both, and from launch — retrofitting fails
  because untagged output is already circulating (question 158).
* **Preserve manifests** on ingest and re-sign on edit rather than stripping them.
* **Disclose in the interface**, not only in metadata. A visible label survives what metadata does
  not.
* **Never present a detector's output as proof.** Report a likelihood with an explicit false
  positive rate, or do not report it.

## What an interviewer digs into next

* Why is the false positive rate the deciding factor for detectors?
* Explain the robustness/imperceptibility/capacity trade.
* Why does absence of provenance prove nothing?
* Why is proving media is *authentic* the more valuable capability?
