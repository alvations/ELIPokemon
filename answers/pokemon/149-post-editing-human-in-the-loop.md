# Fixing up a Pokémon somebody else raised

You did not catch this one. It arrived in a trade, already levelled, already holding something,
already knowing four moves — and it is **nearly** right.

So you fix it. **Hyper Training** with a **Bottle Cap** to paper over the stats it was born with. A
**Mint** to change which stat its nature favours. An **Ability Capsule** to swap the ability. The
**Move Deleter** in Fuchsia City for the move it should never have learned, and a **Heart Scale**
to the **Move Reminder** for one it should have. A **Kelpsy Berry** to walk back the Attack
training somebody wasted on a special attacker.

That is post-editing. **Repairing** rather than raising, and it is how almost all professional
translation now happens.

## The three lanes 🛣️

```
   the source ─► the machine ─► Nurse Joy looks it over (question 132)
                                        │
        ┌───────────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────┐
     ✅ fine                       ⚠️ needs work                   🚨 start again
        │                               │                               │
   send it out              fix it up and send it            raise one from scratch
                                        │
                                        └──► every fix is written down ──► the archive
```

📌 And there are **two different jobs** in that middle lane, which get confused constantly.

**Patching what is broken** — the wrong move, the wrong item, a genuine mistake — and living with a
Pokémon that is a bit rough around the edges.

**Making it properly yours** — right down to the nature, the spread, the nickname — so nobody could
tell it was not raised from an egg.

Those are different amounts of work and they are priced differently. Briefing somebody for the
first and expecting the second is the single commonest argument in this whole business.

## Counting the work 🧮

The usual measure is **how many things had to be changed** — count the edits, divide by the length.
It is a proxy, and it is worth knowing exactly how it lies: **it counts moves, not thought.**

Swapping one move because you finally worked out what the whole team was for counts as **one**.
Rearranging a sentence counts as **many**. The first took an hour and the second took a minute.

So also measure the thing you are actually paying for: **time**. And expect the savings to be
real — often a third to a half — and to **vary wildly** by language, by subject, by how good the
machine was. ⚠️ One number averaged across a whole programme hides the pairs where fixing up is
**slower** than raising one yourself.

## Two things nobody warns you about 🎭

**🏷️ It keeps its Original Trainer.** A traded Pokémon carries the mark of whoever raised it,
permanently. You can max its stats, change its nature, rebuild its moveset — and it is still
*from somewhere else*, and if you have not earned enough badges it will not even obey you.

Repaired translations are the same. They stay more literal, more simply built, and shaped more
closely around the original than anything a person would have written from scratch. You can
**measure** the difference. ⚠️ And it matters enormously when the repaired text becomes **training
material**, because you are now raising the next machine on Pokémon that all came from the last one.

**⚓ The draft anchors the person.** Show a Trainer a Pokémon that is *nearly* right and they will
tweak it. Show them an egg and they will build what the team actually needs. A fluent wrong
translation quietly stops the translator restructuring the sentence, because it already looks
finished.

📌 So *"just post-edit it"* is bad advice for anything creative. You are not getting a human
translation with fewer mistakes. You are getting a machine translation with the mistakes taken out.

## When to refuse and raise your own 🥚

* **✨ Anything where the writing *is* the product.** Advertising, slogans, flavour text. The right
  process is not translating at all — it is **remaking it** for the new audience, which may share
  no sentences with the original. Pokémon names themselves work this way: they are re-invented as
  puns in every language, not translated. Repairing a literal version gets you something that
  technically says the right thing and lands like nothing.
* **📉 When the machine's output is genuinely poor.** Below some line, fixing is slower than
  raising. Translators know exactly where that line is for their pair. **Ask them** — it is far
  cheaper than discovering it six months later in the throughput figures.
* **⚕️ When a dropped *not* is unacceptable.** Then you need a second person reading the **source**,
  not a person reading a draft that has already anchored them.

## Feeding it back 🔄

Every fix is the best training signal you will ever have: a real mistake, corrected by an expert,
in exactly your subject. Capture them (question 133).

⚠️ Two cautions. It is a **loop**, and loops amplify — the Original Trainer mark gets stronger with
every generation. And the fixes were made by people: using their work to train the machine that
competes with them is a matter of consent and payment, not a technical footnote.
