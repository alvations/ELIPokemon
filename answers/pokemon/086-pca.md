# PCA: what actually distinguishes one Pokémon from another?

Every Pokémon has six stats: HP, Attack, Defence, Special Attack, Special Defence, Speed. Six numbers
to keep track of.

But look at real Pokémon and you notice something. The six numbers **aren't independent.**

* High Attack usually comes with high Defence — that's Snorlax, Rhyperior, Aggron.
* High Special Attack usually comes with high Speed — that's Alakazam, Gengar, Jolteon.

So maybe you don't need six numbers. Maybe you need **two**:

```
   📊 AXIS 1: "physical bruiser  ←──────────→  special sweeper"
   📊 AXIS 2: "fragile           ←──────────→  bulky"

   Snorlax     → far physical, very bulky
   Alakazam    → far special, very fragile
   Gyarados    → physical, moderately bulky
```

Two numbers instead of six, and you've barely lost anything — because those two axes were **doing
most of the work all along.**

## What PCA actually does 🧭

It finds the axes **along which Pokémon differ most**, and ranks them.

```
   the raw stats                  rotated to the real axes         keep axis 1
        │      ●●                      axis 2                       │
        │    ●●●●●                       │  ●                        │
        │  ●●●●●                   ● ●●●●●●●● ● ── axis 1           │ ●●●●●●●●●●
        │●●●●                            │  ●                        │
        └──────────                                                  └──────────
   Attack and Defence          uncorrelated, biggest             one number,
   tangled together            spread first                       most of the info
```

* 📏 **Axis 1** is the direction Pokémon vary along the most.
* 📏 **Axis 2** is the biggest remaining variation, at right angles to the first.
* And so on, each one smaller than the last.

Keep the top two or three, throw away the rest. You've compressed six stats into two with most of the
distinguishing information intact.

## Two things you must get right ⚠️

**⚖️ Put everything on the same scale first.**

HP runs from Shedinja's 1 to Blissey's 255. Speed tops out at Regieleki's 200. But if you'd recorded HP in *hundredths* and Speed in ordinary
units, HP's numbers would be a hundred times bigger — and PCA would declare **"axis 1 is basically
HP"**, purely because of the units you happened to write it in.

Standardise first, or your answer is about your notation rather than your Pokémon.

**🔒 Work out the axes from your training footage ONLY.**

Compute them using all your footage, including the test set, and you've leaked. Your axes were
chosen with knowledge of the matches you're about to be tested on.

## When NOT to use it 🚫

**1. 🏷️ When someone needs to understand the answer.**

Your axis 1 turns out to be:

> `0.4×Attack − 0.3×Speed + 0.2×HP + 0.5×Defence − 0.1×SpA`

What *is* that? "Physical bulkiness," roughly. But you can't hand that to someone and expect them
to act on it — nobody EV-trains axis 1. You train Attack, and you hold a Choice Band.

If the answer has to be actionable, **just pick the stats that matter** and keep them as stats.

**2. 🌀 When the pattern isn't a straight line.**

PCA can only rotate and flatten. If your Pokémon are arranged in a **spiral** — where what matters is
position *along the curve* — no rotation will ever untangle that. You need a method that can bend.

**3. 🎯 When the important thing is subtle. ← the deep one**

PCA keeps whatever **varies most**, and quietly assumes that's the same as what **matters most.**

It often isn't:

```
   📊 Varies enormously:  total stat sum (Magikarp 200 → Arceus 720)
      → PCA keeps this. Obviously.

   🎯 Varies almost not at all:  a tiny Speed difference that decides
      who moves first — and therefore who wins.
      → PCA throws this away. It's a rounding error, by variance.
```

📌 **PCA has never seen who won.** It's looking only at how Pokémon differ, not at what makes them
*good*. If you want the axes that predict **victory**, you need a method that's actually been told
who won.

**4. 📄 When most of your data is empty.** If you're describing Pokémon by which of ten thousand moves
they know, nearly every entry is "no." PCA's centring step **fills all those blanks in with numbers**,
and your compact table becomes an enormous dense one. Use a variant that skips the centring.
