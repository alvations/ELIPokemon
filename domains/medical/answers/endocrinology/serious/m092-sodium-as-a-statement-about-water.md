# The number has a denominator, and the denominator is the thing that moves

The serum sodium is reported as a concentration. A concentration is a quotient: an amount of
solute over a volume of solvent. The quantity in the numerator is the body's total exchangeable
cation — sodium with potassium, because the two are osmotically interchangeable across the cell
membrane — and the quantity in the denominator is total body water *(mechanism)*.

That single observation reorganises the whole topic. The body regulates the numerator and the
denominator by **two separate control systems that answer to two different questions**, and only
one of them has any authority over the quotient. Sodium content is regulated by the
renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and the natriuretic peptides, and what it determines is
**extracellular volume**. Water is regulated by vasopressin and by thirst, and what it determines
is **concentration** *(mechanism)*.

So a low serum sodium is, with very few exceptions, a statement that there is too much water for
the solute present — not a statement that salt has been lost, and emphatically not a statement
about how much salt someone has been eating. Someone can be hyponatraemic while carrying several
litres of excess salt and water, and that combination is common rather than exotic *(consensus)*.

**This answer defers to two others.** m057 is where cortisol deficiency's impairment of free-water
excretion, and the reasons it is the endocrine diagnosis most costly to miss, are worked through —
this answer treats it as one entry on a differential and does not re-argue it. m059 is where
reading two results *as a pair* is established as the diagnostic move; the same structure is used
here for the two urine measurements and is not re-derived.

Claims below are marked *(mechanism)* where they follow from physiology and are checkable by
reasoning, *(definitional)* where the statement is what a term means, *(consensus)* where they are
settled professional agreement, and *(country-dependent)* where the answer differs between
countries and documents.

```
     → oedema, or postural drop         │     → the number on the report
                                        │
   ── AND HERE IS THE CROSS-LINK THAT CAUSES MOST OF THE CONFUSION ────────────

   Both systems can be driven by the SAME sensed variable: effective arterial
   volume. When that falls, aldosterone rises (hold sodium) AND vasopressin
   rises (hold water). The volume signal OVERRIDES the osmotic signal.

   So in a low-volume state the body will deliberately hold water it does not
   osmotically need, and the quotient falls. The hormone is behaving correctly.
   The number is still wrong.
```

Three statements follow and they are the examinable core.

**A change in the numerator alone moves volume, not concentration**, because water follows solute
and the quotient is preserved. Giving or losing salt and water in the same proportion changes how
much there is of both and changes the reading not at all *(mechanism)*.

**A change in the denominator alone moves concentration**, which is why water intake and water
excretion are the two levers that actually set the number *(mechanism)*.

**The volume signal beats the osmotic signal.** The osmoreceptor is the more sensitive of the two
in health, but when effective arterial volume falls, the volume pathway dominates and the body
defends volume at the expense of concentration *(consensus)*. Almost every clinically important
hyponatraemia is a version of that sentence.

## Before anything else: is the reported value a real concentration?

Two situations produce a low reported sodium without a low osmolality, and both are mechanical.

**Pseudohyponatraemia** is a measurement artefact. Methods that dilute the sample before measuring
report solute per litre of *plasma*, and plasma is water plus everything dissolved and suspended
in it. Severe hyperlipidaemia or a very high paraprotein reduces the fraction of each litre that
is water, so sodium per litre of plasma falls while sodium per litre of plasma *water* has not
moved *(mechanism)*. Methods that measure the undiluted sample are not affected, which is why the
answer depends on what the laboratory does and why the laboratory handbook is the authority
*(country-dependent)*.

**Translocational hyponatraemia** is real dilution by a real osmole. Another effective solute in
the extracellular space — glucose when insulin is lacking, or an infused agent such as mannitol —
holds water outside the cells, and that water dilutes the sodium *(mechanism)*. Osmolality is
normal or high. Correcting the sodium is not the problem; the other solute is.

This is why osmolality comes first. A low sodium with a normal or raised osmolality is a different
problem from a low sodium with a low osmolality, and the two have nothing in common except the
number.

## If the osmolality really is low, two readouts answer two questions

The question "why is there too much water" decomposes cleanly, and each half has its own urine
measurement.

**Question one: is vasopressin acting?** The readout is urine osmolality. If the urine is dilute,
the kidney is excreting water as it should, so the water load has overwhelmed a working system —
very large intake, or a diet so low in solute that there is nothing to carry the water out with
*(mechanism)*. If the urine is concentrated, water is being retained, and either vasopressin is
acting or the kidney is unable to dilute.

**Question two: if it is acting, is that appropriate?** The readout is urine sodium, read
alongside the clinical volume assessment. A low urine sodium says the kidney is holding sodium,
which it does when effective arterial volume is low. A urine sodium that is not low, in someone
who looks euvolaemic, says the kidney has no reason to hold sodium and the water retention is not
a response to volume at all *(mechanism)*.

Those two readouts are read **as a pair**, and the reason is structural rather than conventional:
neither answers the other's question, and each is uninterpretable alone. It is the same move the
calcium answer makes with calcium and parathyroid hormone.

Two cautions. Diuretic therapy wrecks the urine sodium as an index of volume, because the drug is
making the kidney excrete sodium whatever the volume state is *(mechanism)*. And thiazide-type
diuretics are a classical cause of hyponatraemia for a specific mechanical reason: they impair
dilution in the cortical segment while leaving the medullary concentrating mechanism intact, so
the kidney retains the ability to concentrate without retaining the ability to dilute
*(consensus)*. Loop agents, which blunt the medullary gradient itself, do this much less readily
*(mechanism)*.

## The hormone can be appropriate while the state is not

In heart failure and in cirrhosis the person is overloaded with total sodium and total water, and
is nevertheless retaining more of both. The explanation is that the *sensed* variable is not total
volume but **effective arterial volume**, and in both conditions that is low — in one because the
pump cannot generate it, in the other because the splanchnic circulation is dilated and the volume
is in the wrong compartment *(mechanism)*. Vasopressin is therefore being released correctly for
the signal it is receiving, and the result is a falling sodium in someone who is visibly fluid
overloaded.

That is the cleanest available illustration of the general point. Asking whether a hormone level
is appropriate is not the same as asking whether the state is appropriate, and a loop can be
working perfectly while producing a damaging result.

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis is the opposite case: water retention that is not a
response to any volume or osmotic signal, so the hormone is genuinely inappropriate
*(definitional)*. Its diagnosis is one of exclusion, and two of the exclusions are endocrine —
cortisol deficiency and hypothyroidism both impair free-water excretion, and the first of them is
dangerous to miss *(consensus)*.

## Hypernatraemia is the same arithmetic read the other way

A high sodium is a water deficit. Because thirst is a powerful and fast defence, sustaining one
requires both a water loss and something preventing its replacement — impaired access to water,
impaired thirst, or an inability to drink *(mechanism)*. That is why hypernatraemia carries the
information it does about circumstances rather than about kidneys.

The renal cause is a failure to concentrate: either insufficient vasopressin, or a kidney that
cannot respond to it. These two were long called central and nephrogenic diabetes insipidus; a
recent international consensus renamed them as vasopressin deficiency and vasopressin resistance,
on the grounds that the old name invited confusion with diabetes mellitus *(consensus)*. Adoption
of the new terms is uneven between countries and documents, and both sets of names remain in
current use *(country-dependent)*.

## Why the rate of change matters more than the value

Brain cells do not tolerate swelling, and they defend their volume against a fall in extracellular
osmolality by extruding potassium and then organic osmolytes, over hours to days *(mechanism)*. A
chronically low sodium is therefore being accompanied by a brain that has already adapted to it.
Restoring the extracellular osmolality quickly then pulls water *out* of cells that have given
away their own osmolytes, and the injury that follows is a demyelinating one *(consensus)*.

The mirror risk is that an acute, rapidly developed hyponatraemia has not been adapted to at all,
and the brain is swelling inside a fixed skull *(mechanism)*.

So the two things that decide what is done are **how fast it developed** and **whether there are
neurological symptoms** — not the value itself. This is the point at which this answer stops: the
limits on correction, the agents used, the monitoring interval and the arrangements for reversing
an over-rapid correction are protocol, they differ between countries and institutions, and none of
them appears here *(country-dependent)*.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Hyponatraemia is the commonest electrolyte abnormality in hospital practice, and the greater part
of the harm attributed to it comes from two directions that pull against each other: leaving a
genuinely symptomatic acute case uncorrected, and correcting a chronic case too quickly. The
second of those is an iatrogenic injury with a recognisable clinical picture, and it is produced
by treatment rather than by disease. That is worth naming as such rather than treating it as an
unavoidable complication.

There is also a diagnostic trap with human consequences. A low sodium is frequently recorded,
attributed to a medicine or to age, and not worked through — and two of the things it can mean are
cortisol deficiency and an occult malignancy. Neither is common; both are costly to miss.

A note about who is reading. Someone reading this may have been told their sodium is low, or may
be on a fluid restriction. Nothing here is a threshold, a target, a rate or a plan. Fluid
restriction, and anything else done about a low sodium, is set against the cause and the
chronicity in a particular person, and that assessment belongs to the team that ordered the test.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about mechanism, written for someone training in or qualified for the
field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and not about any individual's care.
**No correction limits, infusion rates, fluid regimens, thresholds or assay cut-offs appear here
on purpose** — they differ between countries and institutions, they are revised, and the rate at
which a sodium is corrected is one of the places where getting it from a revision page rather than
from a protocol would do real harm. Check your local protocol and the formulary. Nothing here has
had clinical review. Severe symptomatic hyponatraemia and severe hypernatraemia are emergencies:
if someone is unwell now, contact local emergency services.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The arithmetic of the quotient, the separation of volume control from concentration control, the
override of the osmotic signal by the volume signal, the dilution-versus-concentration asymmetry
behind thiazide-associated hyponatraemia and the cerebral osmolyte argument are mechanism and do
not date. What moves is the terminology — the vasopressin deficiency and resistance names are
recent and adoption is uneven — and everything procedural and numeric: the correction limits, the
monitoring intervals, the place of vasopressin-receptor antagonists, and which assay your
laboratory runs. Those are country-dependent and revised. Check current local guidance, your own
institution's protocol and your laboratory's handbook.
