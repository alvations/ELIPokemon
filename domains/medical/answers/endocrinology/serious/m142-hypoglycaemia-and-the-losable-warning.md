# Glucose has four defences arranged in order, and insulin treatment removes the first, loses the second and then degrades the third every time the third is used

"Too much insulin" describes the disturbance. It does not describe the event. A healthy person
given a glucose load and then a fall in glucose mounts a layered defence and almost never becomes
hypoglycaemic, because the system has several independent answers arranged in a sequence
(**mechanism**). What makes hypoglycaemia the limiting factor in insulin therapy is not the size
of the disturbance; it is that the layers are, one after another, unavailable (**consensus**).

There are four physiological layers and a fifth that is behavioural, and they are ordered by the
glucose level at which they engage (**mechanism**). The ordering is the whole structure, and it is
worth stating before anything else: in an intact system, **the layer that produces symptoms
engages at a higher glucose level than the one at which thinking is impaired.** That ordering is
what makes self-rescue possible. Everything that goes wrong in this topic is a disturbance of that
ordering.

This answer takes the control-theory framing from m022 as read: the open loop, the committed
actuator, the asymmetric cost function and the reason the whole system is deliberately run loose
are argued there and are not re-derived. What is left here is the defence stack itself and the
mechanism by which the warning is lost.

Claims below are marked (**mechanism**) where they follow from physiology and are checkable by
reasoning, (**definitional**) where the statement is what a term means, (**consensus**) where they
are settled professional agreement, and (**country-dependent**) where the answer differs between
countries and documents. **No glucose values, thresholds or targets appear anywhere in this
answer**, which is deliberate and is explained in the scope section.

## The defences, in the order they are supposed to engage

```
   GLUCOSE FALLING ──────────────────────────────────────────────►  lower

   ┌─ LAYER 1 ── insulin secretion falls ───────────────────────────────────┐
   │  Engages FIRST, while glucose is still within the ordinary range.      │
   │  The single most important defence, and the only one that works by     │
   │  REMOVING the cause rather than opposing it.                           │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        ↓
   ┌─ LAYER 2 ── glucagon rises ────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  Mobilises hepatic glucose. The first ACTIVE opposition.               │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        ↓
   ┌─ LAYER 3 ── adrenaline rises ──────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  Redundant back-up for layer 2, AND the source of the warning          │
   │  symptoms. One mechanism doing two jobs — remember this.               │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        ↓
   ┌─ LAYER 4 ── cortisol and growth hormone ───────────────────────────────┐
   │  Slower. Matters for a PROLONGED fall, not an acute one.               │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        ↓
   ┌─ LAYER 5 ── symptoms, and then eating ─────────────────────────────────┐
   │  BEHAVIOURAL. Requires being awake, being able to act, and noticing.   │
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        ↓
   ══ and only BELOW all of that ══  cognitive impairment

   THE ORDERING IS THE POINT. Warning above impairment is what makes
   self-rescue possible. Reverse those two and the defence is gone even
   though every component still exists.
```

Two features of that stack carry most of the clinical consequence. The layers are **redundant** —
losing one is survivable because the next engages — and layer 3 is doing **two jobs at once**,
back-up hormone and alarm (**mechanism**). A single mechanism serving both the remedy and the
warning is a structural fragility, because anything that attenuates it attenuates both.

## What insulin treatment removes, and why it is three losses rather than one

**Layer 1 is absent by construction.** Injected or infused insulin cannot be withdrawn. The most
important defence against falling glucose in a healthy person is that the insulin stops arriving,
and that defence is not available to a system where insulin arrives from outside on a timetable
set in advance (**mechanism**). This is not a degree of impairment; the layer is structurally
missing, and it is missing from the first day of treatment. m022's committed actuator is the same
fact stated as control theory.

**Layer 2 is lost early in type 1 diabetes, and specifically.** The glucagon response **to
hypoglycaemia** is lost, while glucagon secretion in response to other stimuli may be preserved
(**consensus**). That specificity matters because it tells you the defect is in the sensing or
signalling arm for this particular stimulus rather than in the alpha cell's capacity to secrete.

**Layer 3 is attenuated, and that attenuation is dynamic.** The adrenaline response persists but
is reduced, and — the key fact — **the glucose level at which it engages moves** (**consensus**).
That is the subject of the next section, and it is the mechanism by which the warning is lost.

So the stack is not weakened uniformly. It is decapitated at the top, missing in the middle, and
the remaining layer is both the last line of defence and the alarm. Layer 4 is too slow to rescue
an acute fall, and layer 5 depends entirely on layer 3 having worked.

## The warning is a threshold, and a threshold moves with recent history

The symptomatic warning is not a fixed alarm wired to a fixed glucose level. It is a threshold,
and the threshold tracks recent glucose exposure in **both** directions (**mechanism**,
**consensus**).

**Recent hypoglycaemia lowers it.** An episode of hypoglycaemia reduces the sympathoadrenal
response to a subsequent episode and shifts the glucose level at which symptoms appear downward.
Repeat that and the symptomatic threshold can fall to, or below, the level at which cognitive
function is already affected — at which point the warning arrives after the impairment it was
supposed to warn about, or does not arrive at all (**consensus**). The resulting state is
described as impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia, and the reduced counter-regulatory response that
accompanies it is described as hypoglycaemia-associated autonomic failure (**definitional**).
Terminology and the instruments used to score awareness differ between countries and documents
(**country-dependent**).

**Chronic hyperglycaemia raises it.** The same mechanism running the other way produces symptoms
at a *higher* glucose level in someone whose glucose has been running high — so a person can feel
unmistakably hypoglycaemic at a level that is not low (**consensus**). This matters for two
reasons. It demonstrates that the threshold is genuinely a variable rather than a damaged setting.
And it explains why improving glucose control can transiently produce more symptomatic episodes,
which is a mechanical consequence and not a sign that something has gone wrong.

**It is distinct from autonomic neuropathy**, and conflating the two is a common error. Structural
autonomic neuropathy can blunt symptoms and does not recover. The threshold shift described here
is **functional** and largely reversible, and that difference is the single most useful thing in
this answer (**consensus**).

## Why this is positive feedback laid on top of a broken negative loop

Put the two previous sections together and the structure is explicit.

Hypoglycaemia degrades the mechanism that defends against hypoglycaemia. Each episode makes the
next episode both more likely and quieter, which makes it more likely to go untreated, which makes
the one after that quieter again (**mechanism**). The direction of the feedback is positive, the
quantity being fed back is the defence itself, and the system it is layered on top of is a
negative loop that has already lost two of its layers.

That is a sufficient explanation for a clinical observation that otherwise looks like a statement
about a person: severe episodes cluster in the people who have had episodes. It is the mechanism,
not a disposition.

It also explains why the defence cannot be improved by effort. Layer 5 is the only layer anybody
can act on deliberately, and layer 5 is downstream of layer 3. If the alarm does not sound, there
is nothing for the behavioural layer to respond to.

## The settings where the remaining defence is unavailable

Five, and each is mechanical rather than a matter of care.

**Sleep.** The sympathoadrenal response to hypoglycaemia is reduced during sleep, and the
behavioural layer requires waking up (**consensus**). Nocturnal hypoglycaemia is therefore the
case where the two remaining layers are simultaneously weakest, and it was substantially invisible
before continuous monitoring made it measurable — which is m023's point about what a continuous
record changed.

**Exercise.** Glucose utilisation rises during activity and insulin sensitivity remains increased
for many hours afterwards, so the same dose of insulin does more later in the day than it did when
it was given (**consensus**). The disturbance is not the exercise; it is the long tail after it.

**Alcohol.** It impairs hepatic gluconeogenesis, which is the substrate layer 2 would have
mobilised, and it also impairs the recognition of symptoms (**consensus**). Two layers at once.

**Beta-adrenoceptor blockade.** It can attenuate the adrenergic symptoms without doing anything to
the glucose (**consensus**). The warning is blocked and the event is not — which is a different
thing from the threshold shift above, and arrives at the same place.

**Anything that removes the capacity to act.** The behavioural layer assumes an awake person who
can recognise a state, make a decision, and reach something. Each of those is a separate
assumption and each can fail independently.

## Why awareness is recoverable, and what that implies

This is the most clinically consequential claim in the answer. Because the threshold shift is
functional, **scrupulous avoidance of hypoglycaemia over a period can restore symptomatic
awareness and some of the counter-regulatory response** (**consensus**). The loop that degraded
the defence runs in reverse when the input stops.

Two implications follow and neither is a protocol.

The first is that impaired awareness is a **treatable state** rather than a permanent
characteristic, and the treatment is the removal of the thing that caused it. Which is to say the
treatment is not more vigilance; it is fewer lows, which is usually achieved by deliberately
accepting a less tight target for a while — a decision belonging entirely to a person and their
own team (**country-dependent**).

The second is about what the technology is for. Continuous monitoring with alarms, and automated
insulin delivery, matter here not primarily because they improve an average but because they
substitute an **external alarm** for an internal one that has stopped working, and because they
can act on a falling trend rather than on an arrived value (**consensus**). Structured education
programmes aimed specifically at restoring awareness exist, their content and availability differ
by country, and none of it appears here (**country-dependent**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

The mechanism above is clean. What it describes is not.

Hypoglycaemia is frightening while it is happening and often frightening afterwards. Mild episodes
are disruptive, embarrassing in public, and exhausting. Severe episodes can cause seizure, loss of
consciousness, and death. Someone who has had a severe episode, or who has watched somebody else
have one, is afterwards living with the knowledge that it can happen again without warning — and
the mechanism described in this answer is precisely a mechanism for losing the warning.

Fear of hypoglycaemia is therefore a rational response to an accurately perceived hazard. It is
routinely recorded as non-adherence, poor control, or anxiety. Running glucose deliberately high
to avoid an event that has previously happened without warning is not a failure of understanding;
it is a reasonable trade made by somebody who has more information about their own risk than any
number on a printout contains.

Two further things are worth saying because they are not mechanism.

Impaired awareness has consequences that are social and legal as well as clinical — driving above
all, and in many countries it is specifically regulated, with rules that differ and that change.
Those rules are a matter for the licensing authority where a person lives and for their own
clinical team, and nothing resembling them appears here.

And the burden falls on people around the person too. Someone who has needed help during an
episode has usually had that help from a partner, a parent, a colleague or a stranger, and the
people who provide it carry the memory of it. That is part of what this diagnosis costs and it
belongs in the account.

A note about who is reading. Anybody reading about hypoglycaemia is more likely to be living with
it than revising it. If that is you: there are deliberately no glucose numbers, thresholds or
targets on this page, and nothing here is about your regimen. If an episode is happening now,
treat it the way your own team has agreed with you. If somebody cannot be roused or is having a
seizure, call emergency services. If you are having episodes without warning, that is specifically
a reason to contact your own team — because it is the state that is treatable, not a thing to be
endured more carefully.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about mechanism, written for someone training in or qualified for the
field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, not a treatment protocol for
hypoglycaemia, and not about any individual's care. **No glucose values, thresholds, targets,
carbohydrate quantities, doses or agents appear here on purpose.** A number on this page would be
read as a threshold by somebody who needed their own, and the levels that matter are individual,
are set with a clinical team, and are not the same for two people. Check your own institution's
protocol and your national guidance. Nothing here has had clinical review. Hypoglycaemia that is
happening now is not a reading problem: treat it as your own team has agreed, and call emergency
services if somebody cannot be roused.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Why is the ordering of the defence layers more important than their individual strength?
* Why is the absence of layer 1 a structural consequence of treatment rather than a degree of
  impairment?
* What does the specificity of the lost glucagon response — lost to hypoglycaemia, preserved to
  other stimuli — tell you about where the defect sits?
* Why is it a structural fragility that adrenaline is both the back-up hormone and the alarm?
* Why does chronic hyperglycaemia moving the threshold the other way prove that the threshold is a
  variable rather than a damaged setting?
* Why is it important to distinguish a functional threshold shift from structural autonomic
  neuropathy?
* Why can the behavioural layer not compensate for a failure of layer 3?

## Where this stands, October 2026

The ordered defence stack, the structural absence of the first layer under exogenous insulin, the
specificity of the glucagon defect in type 1 diabetes, the bidirectional threshold shift, the
positive-feedback structure and the reversibility of the functional component are mechanism and
settled consensus, and they do not date. What moves is everything procedural and numeric: how
episodes are classified and graded, the instruments used to score awareness, every glucose value
and target, treatment protocols, the eligibility criteria for monitoring technology and automated
delivery, the content and availability of awareness-restoration education, and the statutory
driving rules — which differ by country and are revised independently of any clinical guideline.
Check current local guidance, your own institution's protocol, and the licensing authority where
you live.
