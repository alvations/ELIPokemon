# A lipid value is one factor in a product, so the same value in two people is two different quantities of harm and correctly produces two different decisions

A lipid result is not a measurement of a disease. It is a measurement of **one term in a
multiplicative estimate of future risk**, and almost everything that confuses people about this
area follows from treating it as the former (**mechanism**).

Three consequences fall out immediately, and the rest of the answer is those three plus the
exception that proves them.

**There is no natural threshold.** The relationship between the causal particle concentration and
risk is continuous and has no point at which something changes character, so any cut-off in use is
a decision about where to act and not a property of the molecule (**consensus**). That is m023's
argument about glycated haemoglobin arriving at a different analyte, and it is not re-derived
here.

**The same value means different things in different people.** Because risk is a product of
several terms, a given lipid value contributes a factor, and a factor applied to a large number
produces a large absolute quantity and applied to a small number produces a small one
(**mechanism**). Two people with identical panels receiving different recommendations is the model
working correctly rather than inconsistency.

**And the exposure that matters is an integral, not a level.** The mechanism is retention over
time, so the burden is concentration multiplied by duration, which is why a value at one moment is
a sample of a lifetime quantity (**mechanism**).

Claims are marked (**mechanism**) where they follow from physiology or arithmetic and are
checkable by reasoning, (**definitional**) where the statement is what a term means,
(**consensus**) where they are settled professional agreement, and (**country-dependent**) where
the answer differs between countries and documents. **No targets, thresholds, percentages,
risk-score outputs, numbers needed to treat or drug names appear anywhere in this answer.**

## The mechanism is retention, which is why it is a function of time

The causal particles are the apolipoprotein-B-containing lipoproteins — low-density lipoprotein
chiefly, together with remnant particles and lipoprotein(a) (**consensus**). Each such particle
carries one apolipoprotein B molecule, which is why a measurement of that protein is a count of
particles rather than a measure of the cholesterol they carry, and why the two can disagree in the
same person (**mechanism**).

The mechanism is not that cholesterol is deposited the way silt settles. It is that these
particles cross into the arterial intima, are **retained** there by binding to the extracellular
matrix, are modified once retained, and provoke an inflammatory response that builds and remodels
a plaque over years (**consensus**). Retention is a probabilistic event per particle per unit
time, which has one arithmetic consequence that governs the whole topic: **the accumulated burden
is a function of particle concentration and of how long that concentration has been present**
(**mechanism**).

The lifetime-integral argument is also why the strongest single piece of evidence in this area is
not about treatment at all. Conditions that raise the causal particle concentration from birth
produce clinical disease decades earlier than the same concentration arriving in midlife would,
and conditions that lower it from birth are protective — the duration term is doing visible work
(**consensus**).

## One term in a product, and why that settles the apparent paradoxes

Risk is estimated as a function of several terms, and in every commonly used model **age carries
more weight than the lipid value** (**consensus**). That single fact resolves most of what looks
paradoxical here.

**Why two identical panels get different advice.** The lipid term is one factor. The product
differs. The decision is made on the product (**mechanism**).

**Why the benefit of lowering is described in two different ways that are both true.** Lowering
the causal particle concentration reduces risk by approximately a *proportion*, and that
proportion is reasonably consistent across people. The *absolute* benefit is that proportion
multiplied by the person's untreated risk (**consensus**). So the same intervention, with the same
proportional effect, is worth a great deal to somebody whose other terms are large and very little
to somebody whose other terms are small. Both statements describe the same arithmetic.

**Why treatment decisions are made on estimated risk rather than on the lipid value.** Because the
product is the thing that predicts harm and the lipid value is one factor in it (**mechanism**).
The lipid value nonetheless gets treated as the target, and the reason is worth naming: of the
terms in that product, it is one of the few that can be moved substantially and measured easily.
**It is the lever, and a lever is easy to mistake for the destination.**

**And why a population-level intervention can be worth a great deal while offering almost no
individual a benefit they could notice.** That is the prevention paradox and m085 argues it in
full; it applies here with unusual force because the term is common, modifiable and modest in each
person.

One structural caution about the models themselves. A risk model is an equation fitted to a
particular population over a particular period. It calibrates poorly in populations unlike the one
it was derived in, several models exist with different inputs and different outputs, and which is
used is a national or institutional decision (**country-dependent**). A risk estimate is therefore
a statement about a reference population with your characteristics, and not a measurement of you —
and no score outputs from any model appear here.

## Association is not causation, and this field contains the cleanest example of it

The inverse association between high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and cardiovascular risk is
robust, long-observed and reproducible. It is also, as far as a target for intervention goes, **a
dead end** (**consensus**).

Two independent lines of evidence point the same way. Interventions that raise it
pharmacologically have not produced the risk reduction the association predicted. And genetic
variants that raise it are not associated with the lower risk that a causal relationship would
require.

The honest statement is therefore that it behaves as a **marker** of something — of metabolic
state, of particle handling, of other things correlated with both — rather than as a cause that
can be intervened on (**consensus**). It still carries information, which is why it appears in
risk models; the information is predictive rather than mechanistic, and acting on it directly has
not worked.

This is worth learning here specifically rather than in a statistics course, because the
association is so strong and so old that it feels causal, and because the same reasoning has to be
applied to every new marker that arrives. A term can earn its place in a prediction model without
being a target. Those are different jobs.

Two other components are worth a sentence each because they behave differently from the main one.
**Lipoprotein(a)** is largely genetically determined, is not meaningfully altered by lifestyle,
and contributes independently; whether, when and in whom it is measured differs substantially
between countries (**country-dependent**). **Triglycerides** are partly a marker of remnant
particles, which are themselves apolipoprotein-B-containing and causal, which is why they are not
simply noise — and **very** high triglycerides are a different problem altogether, carrying a risk
of pancreatitis, which is a disease-level issue and not a risk-factor one (**consensus**).

## And the case where it is a disease

Familial hypercholesterolaemia is the exception that establishes the rule, and getting the
distinction right is the most clinically consequential thing in this answer.

It is a monogenic disorder of the clearance pathway for the causal particles, producing a markedly
raised concentration **from birth** (**definitional**, **consensus**). Three things follow and
none of them is a matter of degree.

**The risk-factor framing is wrong for it.** The lifetime integral is enormous by early adulthood,
and a general-population risk model — in which age carries the most weight — will return a low
estimate for a young adult with the condition and be badly wrong (**mechanism**). Using such a
model here is a named error rather than an approximation.

**It is substantially underdiagnosed**, which follows from the fact that it is usually
asymptomatic until it is not, and from the lipid value being easy to read as "a bit high" rather
than as a diagnostic finding (**consensus**).

**And the single highest-yield act after a diagnosis is testing the relatives.** The condition is
inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, so each first-degree relative has a substantial prior
probability, and systematic cascade testing identifies affected people who have no reason to be
looked at otherwise (**mechanism**, **consensus**). How cascade testing is organised, whether
genetic testing is used and how it is funded differ between countries (**country-dependent**).
m045's distinction between what spreads within a generation and what is transmitted between them
is the relevant frame: this is the vertical one.

The diagnostic criteria in use combine the lipid value with family history, personal history and
physical signs, and several sets of criteria exist and differ — so no criteria and no values
appear here (**country-dependent**).

## What treating a number does and does not mean

Pulling it together, in the order a decision is actually made.

The quantity being targeted is the **lifetime exposure** to the causal particles, which cannot be
measured and can only be inferred from a current concentration and a duration. The quantity being
estimated is the **product** of several risk terms, of which the lipid value is one and usually
not the largest. The quantity being moved is the lipid value, because it is the term that moves.
And the benefit obtained is the proportional effect multiplied by the untreated risk.

Three practical consequences, each of which looks odd until the above is in place.

**A number that has not changed can warrant a different decision five years later**, because the
other terms, age above all, have changed.

**Lowering the value further in somebody at low risk buys very little**, not because the mechanism
is different but because the product is small.

**And a person with established disease is in a different category entirely**, because their
untreated risk is high by observation rather than by estimate, so the arithmetic that governs the
primary-prevention decision does not apply (**consensus**).

Everything about **who** is treated, **to what**, **with what** and **how it is monitored** is
national guidance, differs between countries, and has been revised repeatedly. None of it is here
(**country-dependent**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

The commonest harm in this area is not a side effect. It is a conversation that leaves somebody
believing they have a disease they do not have, or believing they do not have one they do.

Being told a cholesterol result is high, with no context about the other terms, hands a person a
number and no way to interpret it. Some will be frightened by a finding that in their
circumstances means very little. Others will be reassured by a result described as borderline when
the thing that actually matters about them is a family history of early cardiac death. Both are
failures of explanation and both are avoidable.

The reverse harm is specific and serious. A young adult with familial hypercholesterolaemia may be
told repeatedly that their risk score is low, which is true of the score and wrong about them.
People in this situation sometimes find out when a relative dies young. If there is one thing
worth carrying out of this answer it is that a markedly raised value in a young person with a
family history of early cardiac disease is not a slightly worse version of a common finding.

And a note about the social weight of this. Cholesterol has been an object of public moralising
for decades, with the result that a raised result often arrives attached to an implied verdict
about how somebody eats. Lipid handling is substantially genetic, and the main determinant of
somebody's risk in most models is their age, which nobody chose. A consultation that treats the
number as evidence about a person's discipline is both factually wrong and reliably
counterproductive.

A note about who is reading. Anybody reading about this is more likely to have had a cholesterol
result than to be revising for an examination. If that is you: there are deliberately no numbers,
targets or thresholds on this page to compare yours against, because a lipid value means something
only alongside your age, your blood pressure, your smoking history, your family history and the
rest — and that reading belongs to the clinician who ordered it. If there is early heart disease
in your family, that is specifically worth raising with them, because it changes what your own
result means. And nothing on this page is about any medicine you may have been offered or
prescribed.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about how a risk factor differs from a disease, written for someone
training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, not
advice about anybody's cholesterol, and not about any individual's care. **No lipid values,
targets, thresholds, risk-score outputs, percentage risk reductions, numbers needed to treat,
diagnostic criteria or drug names appear here on purpose.** A lipid value is interpretable only
alongside every other term in the estimate, and a number lifted from this page and compared
against somebody's own result would be exactly the misuse the answer argues against. Check your
own national guidance and your national formulary. Nothing here has had clinical review. A family
history of early cardiac disease materially changes what a lipid result means and is a matter for
a clinician.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The retention-and-modification mechanism, the causal role of apolipoprotein-B-containing
particles, the lifetime-integral nature of the exposure, the multiplicative structure of risk
estimation and what it implies about identical values in different people, the
proportional-against-absolute relationship, the non-causal status of high-density lipoprotein
cholesterol, and the categorical difference between a risk factor and a monogenic disorder are
settled and do not date. What moves is everything operational: which risk model is recommended and
in which populations it is trusted, every threshold and target, which agents are used and in what
order — this has changed substantially in recent years and continues to — fasting against
non-fasting sampling, whether apolipoprotein B or lipoprotein(a) is measured routinely, the
diagnostic criteria for familial hypercholesterolaemia, and how cascade testing is organised and
funded. All country-dependent and revised on a cycle. Check current local guidance and your
national formulary.
