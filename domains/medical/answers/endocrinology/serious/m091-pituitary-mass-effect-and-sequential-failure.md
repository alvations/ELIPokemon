# One lesion, two independent problems: what it secretes, and the room it is in

Nearly every other endocrine lesion causes trouble in one way — it makes too much of something, or
too little. A pituitary lesion causes trouble in two ways that have almost nothing to do with each
other. The first is hormonal: it secretes something it should not, or it destroys the cells that
should be secreting. The second is purely mechanical: the gland sits in a bony box with the optic
apparatus directly above it and the cranial nerves of eye movement in the venous sinuses on either
side, and anything that enlarges in that box presses on those structures *(mechanism)*.

These two are **orthogonal**, and holding that straight is most of the skill. A lesion too small
to see on a scan can be hormonally devastating. A lesion large enough to take someone's peripheral
vision can be hormonally silent — indeed the commonest large pituitary tumour is the one that
secretes nothing *(consensus)*. So "how big is it" and "what is it doing" are two separate
questions with two separate answers, and neither predicts the other.

**Three answers already hold ground this one needs, and it defers to all three rather than
restating them.** m056 is where the pituitary's output is established as an inverted, amplified,
slow reporter and where the pattern table for reading an axis is set out. m057 is where the

m058 is where suppression testing and the error of imaging before establishing the question are

## The anatomy, which is the whole of the mass-effect argument

```
                              OPTIC CHIASM
                   nasal fibres cross here; temporal fibres do not
                                    │
                                    │   compression from BELOW takes the
                                    │   crossing fibres first → the field
                                    │   loss is BITEMPORAL, and its shape
                                    ▼   is the chiasm's, not the lesion's
        ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │                    HYPOTHALAMUS                       │
        │                         │                             │
        │                     THE STALK                         │
        │          portal veins ──┼── axons                     │
        │          (anterior)     │    (posterior)              │
        └─────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┘
                                  │
   CAVERNOUS SINUS                ▼                CAVERNOUS SINUS
   III · IV · V1 · V2 · VI   ┌─────────┐      III · IV · V1 · V2 · VI
   lateral invasion →        │  SELLA  │      ← lateral invasion
   eye-movement palsy,       │ TURCICA │        eye-movement palsy,
   facial sensory change     └─────────┘        facial sensory change

   ── TWO SUPPLY ROUTES, AND A LESION OF THE STALK TAKES BOTH ──────────────

   ANTERIOR LOBE      reached by a PORTAL VENOUS system from the hypothalamus.
                      Its hormones need the channel. Cut the channel and the
                      anterior lobe loses its instructions even though the
                      cells are intact.

   POSTERIOR LOBE     reached by AXONS whose cell bodies are in the
                      hypothalamus. The hormone is made upstream and
                      delivered down the nerve. An adenoma inside the sella
                      usually leaves this alone; a stalk or infiltrative
                      lesion, or surgery, does not.
```

Three consequences follow from the picture and none of them is a guideline claim.

**The visual deficit has a shape that belongs to the chiasm.** Fibres from the nasal half of each
retina cross at the chiasm, and those fibres carry the temporal half of each visual field. A mass
growing up out of the sella meets the crossing fibres before anything else, so the classical
deficit is loss of both temporal fields *(mechanism)*. The pattern is diagnostic of *where* the
lesion is and says almost nothing about *what* it is.

**Lateral extension produces a neurological picture, not an endocrine one.** The nerves that move
the eye and the first two divisions of the trigeminal nerve run in the cavernous sinus on each
side, so a laterally invasive lesion presents with double vision or facial sensory change
*(mechanism)*.

**The gland's two lobes have two different supply routes**, which is why anterior and posterior
failure travel separately. The anterior lobe is instructed through a portal venous system; the
posterior lobe's hormone is made in the hypothalamus and delivered along axons. A lesion of the
stalk interrupts both. A lesion confined to the sella usually spares the posterior supply
*(mechanism)*.

## The stalk, and the one hormone that goes the other way

Every anterior pituitary hormone except prolactin is predominantly under **stimulatory**
hypothalamic control. Prolactin is predominantly under **tonic inhibitory** control, by dopamine
delivered down the stalk *(mechanism)*. The consequence is the single most useful fact in the
topic: interrupting the stalk makes every other anterior hormone fall and makes **prolactin rise**
*(consensus)*.

So a raised prolactin alongside a pituitary mass does not establish that the mass is a
prolactin-secreting tumour. It is equally the signature of a non-functioning mass that is
compressing the stalk. The two are usually separated by **how far** the prolactin is raised —
larger elevations favouring a genuinely secreting tumour — but the figures are assay-specific and
belong in the local laboratory handbook rather than here *(country-dependent)*.

The same mechanism explains why the prolactin-secreting tumour is the one pituitary tumour usually
treated with a medicine rather than with an operation: the lactotroph keeps its dopamine receptor,
so the physiological brake can be reapplied pharmacologically, and the tumour commonly shrinks
*(consensus)*. That is unusual. Restoring a hormone's own inhibition and watching the lesion get
smaller is not something the other pituitary tumours offer.

## Sequential failure, and what the order is actually about

Progressive compression of the anterior lobe tends not to take the axes at random. The order that
is taught, and that is broadly borne out, is **growth hormone and the gonadotrophins first, then
thyrotropin, then corticotropin**, with prolactin behaving separately for the reason above
*(consensus)*. It is a tendency and not a law; individual cases depart from it, and an answer that
states it as a rule has overstated it.

What is worth understanding is that the order is not a statement about how hard the lesion is
pressing. It is a statement about **reserve**: how much of each cell population there is, how much
of its output is needed before the deficit is noticeable, and how much spare capacity the axis has
*(mechanism)*. The axis whose loss shows up first is the one with the least margin, not the one
nearest the lesion. The mechanistic account of *why* those particular axes have the least margin
is not fully settled, and saying so is more honest than inventing a reason.

Two practical readings fall out of the ordering. A single deficit discovered in isolation should
prompt a look at the others, because the gland fails as a gland. And the one that is last to go is
the one that it is most dangerous to have gone, which inverts the usual relationship between how
late a finding is and how urgent it is.

## Reading the tests: the pair, and the reporter that fails to shout

The diagnostic move is the same one the thyroid and adrenal answers use: **measure the pituitary
hormone and the target hormone as a pair, and ask whether the pituitary hormone is behaving
appropriately for the target hormone's value** *(mechanism)*. A low target hormone with a raised
pituitary hormone places the lesion in the target gland. A low target hormone with a pituitary
hormone that is low, or merely unremarkable, places the lesion in the pituitary — and the trap is
that "unremarkable" reads as reassurance when it is in fact the finding. An unsuppressed reporter
sitting at an ordinary value, next to a target hormone that is clearly low, is **inappropriately
normal**, which is a positive result and not a negative one.

For the axes whose output is pulsatile or diurnal, a single basal pair does not settle it, and the
test becomes a dynamic one — push the loop in the direction it is supposed to resist. Which
stimulation test is used, how it is conducted and what thresholds are applied differ between
countries and laboratories, and none of that appears here *(country-dependent)*.

## Why the order of replacement is mechanical

Replacement of several axes at once raises a sequencing question that has a mechanistic answer
rather than a procedural one. Thyroid hormone raises metabolic rate and increases the clearance of
cortisol, so correcting an unrecognised thyroid deficit in someone who also has an unrecognised
cortisol deficit can turn a compensated state into a decompensated one *(consensus)*. That is why
the cortisol axis is assessed, and if necessary covered, before the thyroid axis is corrected. The
preparations, the amounts and the arrangements are protocol and formulary, they differ between
countries, and they are not described here.

## The acute version, which is a mechanism and not a protocol

Haemorrhage or infarction into a pituitary lesion produces a sudden syndrome that is the two
problems above arriving together: abrupt severe headache, visual loss, palsies of eye movement
from pressure in the cavernous sinuses, and acute loss of corticotropin *(consensus)*. It is the
one pituitary presentation that is an emergency, and the reason is the acute cortisol deficit
rather than the headache. Infarction of the gland can also follow severe peripartum haemorrhage
*(consensus)*. What is done about either, in what order and by whom, is local protocol and is
deliberately absent from this page.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Pituitary disease is slow, and the things it takes first are things people are routinely told to
expect from ordinary life — tiredness, loss of libido, mood change, reduced exercise tolerance.
Several years between the first symptom and the diagnosis is common and documented, and it is
usually nobody's single mistake. Saying that is more useful than presenting the diagnosis as
straightforward.

Visual field loss from chiasmal compression is often not noticed by the person who has it, because
the fields fill in perceptually and both eyes compensate for each other. That is a fact about
perception rather than about attentiveness, and it is the reason formal field testing exists
instead of asking.

A note about who is reading. Someone reading this may have a pituitary adenoma, or be waiting for
a scan or a surgical opinion about one. Nothing here is a threshold, a dose, a plan or a
prognosis. Replacement regimens, the arrangements for illness and surgery, and what any particular
result means in sequence all belong to the team that holds the case, and the plan a person already
has is the one that applies.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about mechanism, written for someone training in or qualified for the
field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and not about any individual's care. No
doses, thresholds, assay cut-offs, test protocols or replacement regimens appear here on purpose —
they differ between countries, institutions and laboratories, and a revision page is the wrong
place to get them from. Check the formulary and your local protocol. Nothing here has had clinical
review. Pituitary apoplexy and acute corticotropin deficiency are emergencies: if someone is
unwell now, contact local emergency services.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The anatomy, the two supply routes, the dopaminergic inhibition of prolactin, the reserve argument
behind the order of axis loss and the clearance interaction between thyroid hormone and cortisol
are mechanism and do not date. What moves is everything procedural and numeric: which dynamic
tests are used and how, the assays and the values they are reported against, the size conventions,
the imaging protocols, surveillance intervals, surgical indications and the drug classes named in
guidance for secreting tumours. All of those are country-dependent and revised. Check current
local guidance and your own institution's protocol.
