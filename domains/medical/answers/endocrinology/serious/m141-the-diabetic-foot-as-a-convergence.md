# Three independent mechanisms meet at one piece of anatomy, and the thing that looks like the problem is the last one to arrive

A foot ulcer is routinely filed as a complication of diabetes, which is true and almost useless.
It is the meeting point of three mechanisms that arise separately, progress on different clocks,
and are each managed by a different service (**mechanism**, **consensus**). The ulcer is where
they intersect. Treating it as one disease produces the characteristic failure of this area: a
wound dressed carefully for months, by people doing their jobs properly, while the term that is
actually driving it was never addressed.

The three are **neuropathy**, which removes the protective signal and redistributes the load;
**arterial disease**, which lowers the budget available for repair; and **infection**, which is
what gets in once the barrier is broken and which travels further than it looks (**mechanism**). A
fourth process, Charcot neuroarthropathy, is not one of the three and is dealt with separately
below, because mistaking it for infection is a named error rather than a subtle one
(**consensus**).

This answer owes two debts to others in the specialty and does not re-derive either. m024 is where
the microvascular and macrovascular mechanisms of diabetes are argued in full, with their
different cell biology and their different time courses; the arterial term below is that answer's
macrovascular argument arriving at one limb. m056 is where the problem of a *suppressed reporter*
is worked through for an endocrine axis, and the last section of this answer is the same
structural problem at a different level.

Claims below are marked (**mechanism**) where they follow from anatomy or physiology and are
checkable by reasoning, (**definitional**) where the statement is what a term means,
(**consensus**) where they are settled professional agreement, and (**country-dependent**) where

The arrows go one way. No term in that picture is downstream of another; they are three
independent inputs, and the per-day cost is their sum rather than any one of them (**mechanism**).
That has a direct consequence for management, which is the last section.

## Mechanism one: the warning is gone, and so is the architecture

Distal symmetrical polyneuropathy is the commonest neuropathy of diabetes, and its distribution —
longest fibres first, so distal before proximal and symmetrical rather than patchy — is what makes
the foot the site (**consensus**). Three fibre populations are involved and they do three
different things, which is why "neuropathy" as a single word hides most of the mechanism.

**Sensory loss removes the protective signal.** The normal defence against repetitive mechanical
load is not strength; it is that load becomes uncomfortable, so posture and gait change constantly
without anyone deciding to change them (**mechanism**). Remove that and the load continues
unaltered at the same point for as long as the activity does. Nothing has become weaker. The
feedback has gone.

**Motor involvement changes where the load falls.** Wasting of the intrinsic foot muscles
unbalances the long flexors and extensors, the toes claw, the metatarsal heads become prominent,
and the plantar fat pad that cushioned them migrates forward (**consensus**). The result is a
smaller number of higher-pressure points on a foot that cannot report pressure. The two together
are the whole of the first term: higher load, concentrated, unmonitored.

**Autonomic involvement is the one most often left out.** Loss of sudomotor function dries the
skin and it fissures, which is a breach in the barrier arriving from a direction nobody is
watching. Loss of vasomotor control opens arteriovenous shunts, so a neuropathic foot can be
**warm, pink and bounding** while being at high risk (**mechanism**). That appearance is often
taken as reassurance about perfusion, and it is not evidence about the tissue bed.

## Mechanism two: the repair budget, and why the usual measurement can mislead

Healing a wound is metabolically expensive: it needs oxygen, substrate, and the delivery of
inflammatory and immune cells, and any systemically administered antimicrobial has to arrive
through the same route (**mechanism**). Arterial disease is therefore not a separate problem that
happens to coexist. It sets the ceiling on everything the other two terms demand.

Two features of the arterial disease in diabetes matter more than its presence. It is
characteristically **distal and diffuse**, affecting the tibial and peroneal vessels rather than
presenting as a single proximal stenosis, which changes what revascularisation can offer and how
it is planned (**consensus**). And medial arterial calcification makes vessels less compressible,
so pressure-based non-invasive assessment can read **falsely high** in exactly the population
where it is most needed (**consensus**). An incompressible artery is not a patent one. This is the
general trap of a surrogate measurement whose assumptions have failed, and it is the reason
assessment of this limb does not rest on one index.

Which non-invasive tests a service uses, in what order, and what triggers referral for imaging or
revascularisation is a matter for local pathways and differs substantially between countries and
institutions (**country-dependent**).

## Mechanism three: what gets in, and why it goes further than it looks

Once the barrier is breached, the third term starts on a clock measured in days rather than years
(**mechanism**). Two anatomical facts do most of the work.

**The compartments are continuous.** The plantar compartments and the flexor tendon sheaths run
proximally, so infection introduced at a toe or a metatarsal head has a low-resistance path
towards the midfoot and the leg. Spread along a plane explains why the surface appearance
repeatedly understates the extent, and why assessment includes probing the depth rather than
describing the opening (**consensus**).

**Bone is close.** At the sites that ulcerate — the plantar metatarsal heads, the apices of clawed
toes, the heel — there is very little soft tissue between skin and bone, so osteomyelitis in this
setting is typically **contiguous spread from an adjacent ulcer** rather than haematogenous
(**mechanism**). That is why bone involvement is considered from the start and not only when a
wound fails, and why a probe reaching bone is a finding rather than a procedural detail
(**consensus**).

Which organisms are expected, whether a wound is sampled and how, and what is used are all local
questions governed by local microbiology and stewardship policy, and none of it appears here
(**country-dependent**). The stewardship argument itself is m045's.

## Charcot neuroarthropathy, which is a different thing wearing the same signs

A warm, swollen, sometimes red neuropathic foot, with or without a wound, may be an acute Charcot
process: inflammation and increased bone turnover in a joint that continues to be loaded because
the loading is not uncomfortable, producing progressive destruction and then deformity
(**consensus**). The mechanism is neuropathy plus inflammation plus continued load — and continued
load is the part that is modifiable.

It is named here for one reason. Its presentation overlaps with infection almost completely, the
two are distinguished with difficulty and often with imaging, and the error runs in both
directions (**consensus**). The deformity it leaves then becomes a new high-pressure point, so an
untreated Charcot foot manufactures the first mechanism's worst case.

## Why the severity readout is suppressed by the same things that cause the problem

This is the structural point and it generalises beyond the foot.

Severity is normally graded by the response to the problem rather than by the problem: how much it
hurts, how inflamed it looks, what the systemic markers do. In this limb, **every one of those
reporters is attenuated by terms one and two** (**mechanism**). Pain is absent because the sensory
fibres are gone. The local inflammatory signs are muted because the blood supply that delivers the
inflammatory response is the thing that is limited. Systemic markers and temperature may be
unremarkable in the presence of deep infection, particularly in an ischaemic foot (**consensus**).

So the reporter reads low because of the disease, not because of its absence, and a low reading
cannot be distinguished from a genuinely quiet foot by looking at the reading. The practical
consequence is that assessment of this limb is built on things that do not depend on the host
response: the **extent** of tissue loss, the **depth**, whether bone is involved, and whether
perfusion is adequate (**consensus**). Classification systems used to record exactly those
dimensions exist, several are in use, and which one a service uses differs
(**country-dependent**).

## Why one intervention addresses one term, and what follows

The three terms are independent, so the interventions are too, and each addresses one.

**Offloading addresses the load.** For a neuropathic plantar ulcer, removing mechanical load from
the ulcer site is the intervention rather than an adjunct to dressing it, because the load is what
is preventing closure (**mechanism**, **consensus**). A wound that is dressed and still walked on
is being treated in one term and not in the term that is driving it. Which offloading method is
used, and whether it is removable, is a matter of local practice and of what the person can live
with (**country-dependent**).

**Revascularisation assessment addresses the budget.** Not as a late option when healing fails,
but as a question asked early, because the answer determines whether anything else can work
(**consensus**).

**Infection is treated on its own clock**, which is faster than either of the other two, and that
difference in clocks is why urgency and chronicity sit side by side in the same foot.

And the reason this is organised as a multidisciplinary service rather than as a referral chain is
arithmetic rather than ideology: three independent terms addressed sequentially are addressed
slowly, and the fastest term does not wait (**consensus**). The structure of such a service, who
is in it and what it can access differs by country and by institution (**country-dependent**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

This is the part of diabetes care with the largest gap between how mechanical it looks written
down and what it costs the person living it.

An ulcer is usually painless, which is the single most consequential human fact here and the one
least intuitive to someone whose feet work. A painless wound does not feel like an emergency, and
the system around it often does not treat it like one either. Delay is therefore built into the
presentation rather than being a failure of the person's attention — and people are nonetheless
regularly described as having presented late, in notes they can read.

Offloading, which is the main treatment, is also the main cost. It constrains walking, work,
driving, caring for other people and leaving the house, for weeks to months, in order to heal
something that does not hurt. Adherence framed as a character trait here is a category error: it
is a question about whether someone can afford to stop moving.

Amputation is the outcome everybody in this area is thinking about and the one this page will not
discuss in mechanism terms, because the mechanism is not what matters about it. It is a loss, it
changes a life, and it is also not inevitable — the existence of specialist foot services is an
argument that trajectories change. Anybody for whom this is not abstract should be talking to
their own team, and nobody should be reading a page like this one to work out what their own foot
means.

A note about who is reading. Somebody reading about this is more likely to have diabetes than to
be revising for an examination. If that is you: nothing here is a threshold, a dressing, an
antibiotic or a reassurance, and there are deliberately no numbers to compare yourself with. A new
wound, a new warm or swollen foot, or a change in a foot that already had a wound is something to
get looked at by the service that knows you, promptly, rather than something to read about.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about mechanism, written for someone training in or qualified for the
field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, not a wound-care protocol, and not
about any individual's care. **No doses, antimicrobial choices, thresholds, classification
cut-offs or dressing selections appear here on purpose** — all of them are local, all of them
change, and a revision page is the wrong place to get any of them from. Check your own service's
pathway and your local microbiology policy. Nothing here has had clinical review. A foot that is
new to a service is an urgent clinical assessment and not a reading exercise.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The three-mechanism structure, the fibre-population basis of the neuropathy, the distal and
diffuse pattern of the arterial disease, the compartment anatomy that lets infection travel, the
contiguity of bone involvement, and the argument that the severity reporter is suppressed by the
disease are mechanism and do not date. What moves is everything procedural: risk-stratification
categories and screening arrangements, the classification systems used to record extent and depth,
non-invasive vascular thresholds, offloading methods and the evidence between them, antimicrobial
choice and duration, criteria for revascularisation, and how a foot service is staffed and
accessed. All of those are country-dependent and revised on a cycle. Check current local guidance,
your own institution's pathway and your local microbiology policy.
