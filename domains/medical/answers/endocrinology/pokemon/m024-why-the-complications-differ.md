# One is Badly Poisoned, counting turns. The other is Stealth Rock, waiting at the door.

**Badly Poisoned** takes a sixteenth of the victim's maximum HP multiplied by a counter that
climbs by one every single turn, to a ceiling of fifteen (**mechanism**). What hurts is not the
per-turn figure, it is the count — and the count is nothing but elapsed time. **Stealth Rock** is
the other shape entirely: set once, then it collects a fraction from **every switch-in**, sized by
that Pokémon's type matchup against Rock and not by how long the rock has been sitting there
(**mechanism**). Cumulative exposure against an entry toll. That is the difference between the
small vessels and the large ones, and it is why they answer to different moves, arrive on
different clocks, and pick different Pokémon.

| In the battle | What it stands for |
| --- | --- |
| **Badly Poisoned**: (max HP ÷ 16) × a climbing counter | Microvascular damage: dose × duration |
| The counter, which only ever counts turns | Cumulative glycaemic exposure |
| **Ingrain**: rooted, cannot switch out | Tissues that cannot refuse a glucose load |
| **Stealth Rock**: a fraction on entry, by matchup | Atherosclerosis: multiplicative, and abrupt |
| **Spikes** layering 1/8 → 1/6 → 1/4 | A risk-factor bundle stacking |
| Curing the poison, which returns no HP | Improving control: stops accrual, undoes nothing |
| **Rapid Spin** and **Defog**, which do not cure poison | Different levers for a different hazard |

Claims are marked (**mechanism**), (**consensus**) or *(guideline-dependent)* where it matters.

## Why these particular Pokémon

Almost anything on the field can leave. Switching is how you refuse a condition — you take the
Pokémon out and the field stops being its problem. The ones that get destroyed are the ones that
cannot. **Ingrain** roots its user in place: the HP comes back at a sixteenth a turn and the
Pokémon **cannot switch out at all** (**mechanism**). **Tangela** learns it at level one,
**Lileep** at twenty-two, **Cradily** from the moment it evolves, and **Cacturne**, **Roselia**
and **Sunflora** all pick it up too. Those are the tissues in question. They are not unlucky. They
have no exit.

Each also carries its own structural problem on top of being rooted. One has nothing behind it in
the party, so when its circulation fails there is no second line — that is the retina, a terminal
supply with no collateral (**mechanism**). One is under pressure *because pressure is its job*,
the way a Pokémon kept on the field precisely to absorb hits is being used correctly right up to
the point it breaks — that is the glomerulus, a capillary bed held at high filtration pressure by
design, so any rise is transmitted straight into the filter (**mechanism**). And one reaches
furthest from the centre, so the chip lands at its far end first — that is peripheral nerve, with
axons up to a metre long, which is why injury is length-dependent and shows up in the feet
(**mechanism**).

Two features of that clock matter. The first is that exposure accumulates, so **how long** counts
for as much as **how high**, and a long stretch of a modest counter is not harmless
(**consensus**). The second is what happens when you fix it. Cure the poison — a berry, a **Full
Heal** — and the counter is wiped back to zero, so nothing more accrues (**mechanism**). **Not one
point of the HP already lost comes back.** That is the shape of the real finding: improving
control slows further damage and does not undo what is done, and a stretch of good control early
pays off long afterwards, which is the effect described as metabolic memory in the long-term
follow-up of the major intervention trials in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes *(consensus; the
mechanism behind it is proposed rather than settled)*.

Here the analogy stops, because the outcomes are not battle outcomes. These are the processes that
cause irreversible sight loss, that bring people to kidney replacement therapy, and that —
together with peripheral arterial disease and the loss of protective sensation — lead to foot
ulceration and to amputation. Autonomic involvement adds gastroparesis, postural hypotension,
silent myocardial ischaemia, and the loss of the warning symptoms that makes hypoglycaemia more
dangerous (**consensus**). None of that is material for a metaphor and none of it is softened
above.

## The entry toll, and why it behaves differently

**Stealth Rock** is not diabetes-specific in the way the poison is; it is a hazard that is on the
field in plenty of battles that have nothing else wrong with them, and what this particular battle
contributes is a pile of things that make it bite harder. **Spikes** stack to a quarter of maximum
HP at three layers. **Toxic Spikes** poison at one layer and badly poison at two. The rock itself
scales by matchup: an eighth as standard, a quarter at one weakness step, **half** at two, and
only a sixteenth or a thirty-second against something that resists it (**mechanism**). Same
hazard, five different answers, depending entirely on what walks in.

Three consequences follow, and they are the ones people get wrong:

1. **The rock was down before you led.** Hazards go up early, often before the Pokémon that will
   suffer from them has even been seen, so the toll is already priced in by the time you are
   looking at the battle (**consensus**). The poison counter, by contrast, starts when the poison
   does. In the clinic: in type 2 diabetes the insulin-resistant state, the lipid pattern and the
   raised blood pressure usually predate the glycaemic diagnosis by years, so excess arterial risk
   is measurable before diabetes is diagnosed (**consensus**).
2. **Curing the poison does nothing about the rock.** This is the sharpest point on the page and
   it is a literal game fact: a **Full Heal** clears the status and leaves every hazard on the
   field exactly where it was. The rock needs **Rapid Spin** or **Defog** — different moves,
   different Pokémon, a different plan — and **Heavy-Duty Boots** sidestep it without removing it
   at all (**mechanism**). Lowering glucose has a large, reproducible effect on microvascular
   outcomes and a smaller, slower one on arterial events; the strongest levers there are lipid
   lowering, blood-pressure control and stopping smoking, alongside the specific glucose-lowering
   drug classes shown to reduce cardiovascular and kidney outcomes in their own right
   (**consensus**). Which agents, for whom, in what order, is explicitly a guideline question
   *(guideline-dependent)*.
3. **The rock does not chip. It lands.** The poison takes its slice every turn and you can watch
   it coming. The rock takes its whole fraction in a single instant on entry, at a moment the
   previous turn's reading gave no warning of (**mechanism**). And the fraction **multiplies**
   with the matchup rather than adding to it, which is why the bundle of hazards is so much worse
   than any one of them — exactly as the arterial risk factors multiply rather than add
   (**consensus**).

None of this makes the counter unimportant to the rock. It makes the counter one hazard among
several, and the sum of them worse than the parts.

## Where the metaphor stops

It stops here, and the rest of this section has no Pokémon in it.

A climbing counter and an entry toll are a fair picture of two hazards with different shapes. Chip
damage is not a fair picture of the endpoints, and HP is not a fair picture of an organ.

Retinopathy ends, when it is not caught, in irreversible sight loss. Nephropathy ends in kidney
failure, dialysis or transplantation. Peripheral neuropathy, together with peripheral arterial
disease and the loss of protective sensation, ends in foot ulceration and amputation. Autonomic
neuropathy produces gastroparesis, postural hypotension and silent myocardial ischaemia, and it
removes the warning symptoms of hypoglycaemia, making a separate hazard worse. The large-vessel
endpoints are myocardial infarction, stroke and critical limb ischaemia. The screening programmes
exist because detection before symptoms changes what happens.

The point about damage already done needs saying carefully, because it reads as blame. It is true
that exposure accumulates, and true that curing the status returns none of the HP. It does not
follow that anything is now pointless, and that is the inference people draw unaided: the finding
is that improvement from today still reduces future risk. Past control is also not purely a matter
of choices — insulin supply, cost, housing, work, food access, other illnesses and the quality of
care available all enter it.

A note about who is reading. Someone reading about diabetic complications may well have one, or be
afraid of having one, which is a different situation from revising. If that is you: nothing above
describes your eyes, kidneys, nerves or arteries, and this page cannot tell you your risk. Your
own clinical team and your national screening programme can. A new or changing foot wound,
numbness, or any sudden change in vision needs assessment now rather than reading.

## Scope and safety

The Pokémon is doing one job: separating a hazard that accumulates with time from one that
collects a toll on entry, so that the different time courses and the different levers stop being a
list to memorise. It is not a surveillance schedule, not a risk calculator, and not about any
individual's care. **No screening intervals, risk thresholds or treatment targets appear here** —
those are set by national programmes and guidelines that differ by country and are revised, and
local guidance is the authority. Every number on this page is a fraction of HP in a video game and
converts to nothing. Nothing here has had clinical review. Sight loss, kidney failure and
amputation are named plainly above because they are the outcomes, and the metaphor is dropped
where they are named; anyone concerned about their own eyes, kidneys or feet should be assessed by
their own clinical team rather than reasoning from this page. If someone is unwell now, contact
local emergency services.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The two shapes — a counter that climbs with time, and a toll collected on entry — are mechanism
and do not date. The cast does: **Stealth Rock**, **Toxic Spikes**, **Defog** as a hazard remover
and **Heavy-Duty Boots** all arrived in later generations than the poison counter did, and the
exact fractions have been adjusted, so check the current generation's data. On the clinical side
the therapeutic picture moves fastest — which drug classes have demonstrated cardiovascular and
kidney benefit, and where each sits in a sequence — along with every screening interval and risk
threshold. Read the current guideline, not a remembered one.
