# Resistance moves sideways between species, so no prescription is only one patient's business.

Stewardship is usually taught as a list: take cultures before the first dose, prescribe
empirically to a **local** guideline, document indication and review date, review at the point the
results arrive, de-escalate to the narrowest effective agent, switch from intravenous to oral when
appropriate, and stop at the shortest effective course. The list is impossible to remember and
trivial to remember, depending on whether you know what it is optimising.

It is optimising **two** things that are usually taught as one. The effect of this prescription on
this person — and the effect of this prescription on the susceptibility of the organisms that will
be in this building, and in this person's own flora, next month. The second is a real cost, it is
paid by people who are not in the room, and essentially none of it shows up in the consultation.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with its basis: (**mechanism**), (**definitional**),
(**consensus**), or (**country-dependent**) — the last meaning it differs by country,
institution or laboratory.

## The mechanism that makes it a commons problem

Resistance determinants are frequently carried on **mobile genetic elements** — plasmids,
transposons, integrons — and move between bacteria by **horizontal gene transfer**: conjugation,
transformation and transduction (**mechanism**). Three consequences follow, and they are the whole
argument:

* **It crosses species.** A determinant selected in one organism can end up in a different genus
  entirely, including in a pathogen that was never exposed to the drug.
* **It is faster than descent.** Vertical inheritance moves a trait down one lineage at the rate
  of replication. Horizontal transfer moves it sideways across a population in a single
  generation, and the two together move it faster than either alone.
* **It crosses people.** A determinant selected in one person's commensal flora can be transmitted
  — in a ward, a household, a food chain, a water course — and arrive in another person's
  infection.

```
   VERTICAL                                HORIZONTAL
   ───────────────────────────────────     ──────────────────────────────────────────
   parent → offspring                      organism → unrelated organism
   one lineage                             across lineages, and across species
   rate = rate of replication              one event; an element, not a generation
   ───────────────────────────────────     ──────────────────────────────────────────
   "it stays in the strain I treated"      THE STRAIN YOU TREATED IS NOT THE BOUNDARY

   ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   │  Everything structural about stewardship follows from the right-hand column.    │
   │  If resistance moved only vertically, "the consequences land on my patient"     │
   │  would be nearly true and individual judgement would nearly suffice. It does    │
   │  not, so it is not, and it does not.                                            │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Selection, stated correctly

Antibiotics do not create resistance. They **remove the susceptible competition**, so a resistant
minority that was already present — by pre-existing mutation, or by an element acquired earlier —
becomes the population (**mechanism**). That correction matters because it fixes what the pressure
is proportional to: **total exposure of organisms to the drug**, which includes exposure that did
no good at all.

So the list of things that select is longer and less intuitive than "using too much":

* a course given for a condition that was not bacterial;
* a course continued after the organism was known to be susceptible to something narrower;
* a course continued past the point of benefit;
* an agent broader than the organism required;
* and, separately, **sub-therapeutic exposure** — too low a dose, or poor penetration to the site
  — which selects without treating (**mechanism**).

Both directions of error select. That is why "give less" is not the principle; "give the right
amount of the right thing for the right length of time" is, and it is a harder thing to audit.

## The payoff structure, which is why the rules are structural

```
   ONE PRESCRIBING DECISION, as the decider experiences it

                        BROAD, EARLY, LONG            NARROW, REVIEWED, SHORT
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   this patient         covered for more              covered for the likely
                        possibilities                 organism
   my uncertainty       resolved, today               carried, until results
   my regret risk       low and immediate             higher and immediate
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   cost, and WHO PAYS IT
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   selection pressure   paid by the NEXT patients,    lower
                        over months
   collateral damage    paid by this patient's own    lower
   to commensal flora   flora, over weeks
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

   Benefit: concentrated, immediate, visible, and lands on the decider's own
            conscience.
   Cost:    diffuse, delayed, invisible, and lands on people who will never know
            whose decision it was.

   That is a textbook commons structure, and the response to a commons problem is
   never to ask individuals to internalise an externality by force of character.
   It is to change the structure.
```

Hence the machinery, all of which is (**country-dependent**) in its details and (**consensus**) in
its existence: restricted formularies, authorisation requirements for named agents, automatic stop
dates, prescription-level indication recording, audit with feedback, ward pharmacist review, and a
stewardship team with authority. Those are not an insult to clinical judgement. They are what
makes restraint survivable for the individual who exercises it, because an individual who narrows
a prescription alone carries all the regret risk and captures almost none of the benefit.

The World Health Organization's three-way **access / watch / reserve** categorisation of
antibiotics is the same idea applied to the drugs rather than the prescribers: it makes "this
one's usefulness is a shared, depletable resource" an explicit, published property of an agent
rather than a matter of local taste (**consensus**). Which agents sit where, and what the local
restriction list looks like, differs by country (**country-dependent**).

## What each rule is actually for

* **Cultures before the first dose.** The first dose reduces the yield, and without an isolate and
  a susceptibility result you can never narrow — so skipping the culture commits you to broad
  therapy for the whole course (**mechanism**).
* **Empirical choice from a *local* guideline.** The prior probability of the organism, and of its
  susceptibility, is a property of your population and your institution. A national guideline is a
  starting point; the local antibiogram is the data (**country-dependent**).
* **An explicit review point.** The review exists because the information arrives on a timetable
  and because an unreviewed prescription continues by default. The window is commonly set at 48 to
  72 hours, which is when cultures typically report, and the exact figure is institutional policy
  (**consensus**, **country-dependent**).
* **De-escalation.** A narrower agent exerts less pressure on the commensal flora. The collateral
  is not abstract: disruption of gut flora is itself a mechanism of harm, and *Clostridioides
  difficile* infection is the consequence always named (**consensus**).
* **Shortest effective course.** Pressure is proportional to exposure, and for a growing list of
  indications a shorter course has been shown non-inferior to a longer one. **That list is the
  part of this topic that dates fastest**, and it has moved consistently in one direction.
* **Intravenous to oral switch.** Reduces line-associated infection and length of stay. It is a
  patient-safety and resource intervention and only incidentally a resistance one — treating it as
  a resistance measure is a common exam error.

## What stewardship is not

It is **not** withholding antibiotics from someone who needs them, and the misreading does harm.
In sepsis, delay in effective therapy is itself a major cause of death, and stewardship frameworks
explicitly prioritise prompt, broad, effective empirical therapy in that situation
(**consensus**). The stewardship intervention in a septic patient is not hesitation at the front
door — it is the **review, the narrowing and the stopping** that follow once the organism is
known.

An answer that presents stewardship as rationing has got it backwards. The scarce resource being
protected is the *effectiveness* of the drugs, and the way you protect it is by using them
precisely, which usually means starting them faster and stopping them sooner.

## The human stakes, said plainly

A resistant infection is not an abstraction about the future. It means a person in front of you
whose infection does not respond to the drug that should have worked, treated instead with
something more toxic, less effective, or only available intravenously, for longer, with a worse
outcome. People die of this now.

And the honest part, which is uncomfortable: a prescription that contributed to that was almost
never reckless. It was written under uncertainty, out of hours, with incomplete information, by
someone trying to protect the person in front of them, and the system gave them no way to see or
feel the cost they were passing on. That is precisely why the response has to be structural.
Blaming individual prescribers for a commons failure is both unjust and ineffective, and it is
also the reason audit and feedback work better than exhortation.

Equally: this topic is not a reason for anyone to hesitate when they are unwell. Untreated
bacterial infection kills people far more reliably than resistance does, and the message
"antibiotics are overused" has been heard by some as a reason to delay seeking help. It is not
one. Nothing in this answer is advice about anyone's own treatment, and nothing in it suggests
stopping or declining an antibiotic — the person to ask about that is the prescriber or pharmacist
who can see the records and the results.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Why does horizontal transfer, rather than resistance itself, make this a collective-action
  problem?
* Antibiotics do not create resistance. What do they do, and why does the correction matter?
* Give two ways of selecting for resistance that involve giving *less* drug rather than more.
* Why is a restricted formulary a help to the individual prescriber rather than a constraint on
  them?
* Why is the intravenous-to-oral switch a weak answer to a question about resistance?
* In sepsis, what is the stewardship intervention? (Not the first dose.)

## Scope and safety

This is revision material for a pharmacy undergraduate, a prescriber revising, or anyone already
training in the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and has had no clinical
or pharmacist review. **No antibiotic, dose, course length or empirical regimen is named in this
answer**, deliberately: empirical choice is a function of local susceptibility data and local
policy, and a regimen remembered from revision material is exactly the wrong thing to carry to a
bedside. The one interval that appears — the common 48-to-72-hour review point — is quoted as a
widely used convention and is institutional policy, not a rule. Nothing here should be used to
make a decision about anyone's treatment, including your own, and nothing in it is a reason for
anyone to delay seeking help for an infection or to stop or decline a prescribed antibiotic. The
institution's guideline, the microbiology service and the local antibiogram are the authority.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanism — mobile elements, horizontal transfer, selection by removal of competitors — is
established biology and does not date. The commons analysis is structural and does not either.
What dates, and fast, is everything operational: recommended **course lengths**, which have
shortened repeatedly across many indications and continue to; the restriction and reserve lists,
which are revised locally and nationally; local susceptibility patterns, which are the input to
every empirical choice and change continuously; and the categorisation of individual agents. Rapid
diagnostics and procalcitonin- or biomarker-guided stopping have also been moving the review step,
at different rates in different health systems. Check your institution's current guideline and
your current local antibiogram rather than anything remembered.
