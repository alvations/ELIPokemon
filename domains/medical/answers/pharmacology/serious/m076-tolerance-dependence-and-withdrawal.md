# Tolerance moves a curve. Dependence is invisible until the drug stops. Addiction is a diagnosis.

Three words, three different kinds of claim. **Tolerance** is a statement about a dose–response
relationship: the same exposure now produces less effect. **Physical dependence** is a statement
about a homeostatic adaptation: the body has adjusted to the drug's continued presence, and the
adjustment becomes visible when the drug is taken away. **Addiction** — in current diagnostic
language, a substance use disorder — is a statement about behaviour, made against criteria, and it
is not a pharmacological measurement at all.

They dissociate in every direction. A person can be profoundly tolerant and dependent with no
disorder of behaviour whatsoever, which is the ordinary situation for someone on a long-term
opioid or a long-term antiepileptic. A person can have a substance use disorder with little
physical dependence, which is true of several stimulants. Conflating them is not a vocabulary
problem; it changes what is done, and in both directions it does harm.

Claims below are marked inline with what they rest on: (**mechanism**), (**definitional**),
(**consensus**), or (**country-dependent**).

## The three, side by side

| | Tolerance | Physical dependence | Substance use disorder |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What kind of claim | pharmacodynamic or pharmacokinetic | adaptive, homeostatic | behavioural, diagnostic |
| How it is detected | the dose–response curve has moved | a syndrome appears on withdrawal or on dose reduction | against published diagnostic criteria |
| Reversible? | usually, over a drug-free interval | usually, as the adaptation unwinds | not a question of this shape |
| What it predicts | loss of effect; pressure to escalate | a withdrawal syndrome on abrupt cessation | continued use despite harm, and relapse risk |
| What it does *not* predict | anything about behaviour | anything about behaviour | anything about receptor state |
| Expected on long-term therapy? | for some effects of some drugs | for several whole drug classes | no |

The last row is the one that matters most and is most often got wrong. Dependence on a drug class
that reliably produces it is an expected pharmacological consequence of taking the drug, is
(**definitional**) rather than diagnostic, and is not evidence of anything about the person.

## Tolerance, and the two routes to it

```
   PHARMACOKINETIC (dispositional) tolerance
     the drug induces its own clearance  ──►  less drug for the same dose
     mechanism: enzyme induction, which m009 works through in full
     signature: the CONCENTRATION falls. Measure it and you can see it.

   PHARMACODYNAMIC (functional) tolerance
     the same concentration now does less  ──►  the target has adapted
     mechanisms: receptor down-regulation, desensitisation and internalisation,
                 uncoupling from the signalling cascade, depletion of a releasable
                 mediator, and counter-regulation downstream of the receptor
     signature: the concentration is UNCHANGED and the effect has gone.
                A concentration cannot detect this, which is one of the five
                reasons m041 gives for not monitoring blindly.

   Two more terms that are not the same thing:
     TACHYPHYLAXIS   loss of effect over minutes to hours, often from depletion of
                     a releasable store or rapid receptor internalisation rather
                     than from any change in receptor number
     CROSS-TOLERANCE tolerance to a drug never given, because it acts through the
                     machinery the given drug has already adapted. It follows the
                     MECHANISM, not the chemistry: two structurally unrelated drugs
                     at one receptor cross-tolerate; two close analogues at
                     different receptors do not.
```

All of that is (**mechanism**). Two consequences are worth drawing out.

**Tolerance is selective, and the selectivity is the danger.** The several effects of one drug
tolerate at different rates and to different extents, because they are mediated in different
tissues by different adaptations. The teaching case is opioid analgesia: tolerance to sedation and
to nausea develops relatively readily, tolerance to constipation develops little or not at all,
and tolerance to respiratory depression is incomplete — which is why a dose arrived at by
escalating against pain is not a dose the respiratory centre has become equally indifferent to
(**consensus**). The same asymmetry appears with nitrates, where continuous exposure produces
marked tolerance to the vascular effect and an exposure-free interval is built into how the drugs
are used (**consensus**).

**Escalation narrows the margin.** If the effect you want tolerates and the toxicity you fear
tolerates less, each upward step moves the two curves by different amounts, and the therapeutic
index — `m008`'s subject — gets worse as the dose rises:

```
   effect, % of attainable maximum

   100 ┤        wanted effect, naive        ┌─────────
       │                                    │
       │        wanted effect, tolerant            ┌───────  shifted RIGHT
       │                                           │
    50 ┤  feared effect, naive         ┌─────────────────
       │                               │
       │  feared effect, tolerant       ┌───────────────   shifted right by LESS
       │                                │
     0 ┼──────────────────────────────────────────────────▶  log dose
                                     ▲        ▲
                            naive gap │        │ tolerant gap: NARROWER
```

The picture is schematic and the sizes of the two shifts are drug- and effect-specific; what is
(**mechanism**) is only that two curves adapting at different rates change the distance between
them.

## Dependence, and why a withdrawal syndrome has the shape it has

The useful model is an opponent process. A sustained drug effect is met by a homeostatic
adaptation in the opposite direction; the observable state is the sum. While the drug is present
the sum looks roughly normal, which is exactly why the adaptation is invisible. Remove the drug
and the adaptation is briefly unopposed, so **the withdrawal syndrome's features tend to be
opposite in sign to the drug's acute effects** (**mechanism**):

| Sustained effect | Adaptation | What appears on abrupt removal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| central nervous system depression | increased excitability | agitation, tremor, insomnia, and for some classes seizures |
| opioid receptor agonism | upregulated counter-regulatory tone | autonomic arousal, gastrointestinal hurry, restlessness |
| beta-adrenoceptor blockade | receptor upregulation | tachycardia and ischaemic risk on abrupt cessation |
| alpha-2 agonism, centrally acting | sympathetic counter-regulation | rebound hypertension |

Three structural points follow, and they are what make dependence a *design* problem rather than a
character problem.

* **The time course of withdrawal is set by the drug's kinetics, not by its potency.** A short
  half-life produces an early, sharp syndrome; a long one produces a later, flatter syndrome that
  can be missed or attributed elsewhere (**mechanism**). `m006` is the half-life arithmetic.
* **Tapering works because it lets the adaptation unwind while still partly opposed.** The rate
  that is tolerable is an individual matter and differs between drug classes and between national
  guidance documents (**country-dependent**); that tapering is the structural answer rather than
  willpower is (**consensus**).
* **Switching within a class is not a way round it.** Cross-dependence follows the same shared
  machinery as cross-tolerance, so an agent acting at the same target will suppress the syndrome
  and will not resolve the adaptation (**mechanism**).

A separate and genuinely different case is **suppression of an endogenous axis** by an exogenous
supply — most familiarly with systemic corticosteroids. That is not receptor adaptation to a drug;
it is a control loop switched off by the drug's own signal, and it needs the endocrine reasoning
in `m057`–`m059` rather than the vocabulary on this page.

## Addiction, which is a different kind of claim altogether

A substance use disorder is diagnosed against criteria describing a pattern of behaviour —
impaired control over use, use becoming salient at the cost of other activities, continuation
despite demonstrable harm — and it is assessed clinically, not inferred from a receptor or from a
dose. The criteria themselves, and the names given to the diagnosis, differ between the two major
international classification systems and have been revised repeatedly; which set applies is
(**country-dependent**) and the current wording of either is not something this answer should
state from memory.

Two negative statements are the load-bearing ones, and both are (**consensus**):

* **Tolerance and dependence are not diagnostic criteria for a disorder in a person taking a
  prescribed drug as prescribed.** Both of the major systems address this explicitly, because the
  earlier failure to do so produced exactly the misclassification described below.
* **Needing a drug is not the same as a disorder of wanting it.** The distinction is between a
  physiological adaptation and a pattern of behaviour, and nothing about the first establishes the
  second.

## The human stakes, said plainly

This is the part of the topic where the vocabulary has caused measurable harm, and it has done so
in both directions.

In one direction, people with pain have had analgesia withheld or reduced because tolerance or a
withdrawal syndrome was read as evidence of addiction. That reading is a category error, it has
been documented across many health systems, and the term coined for the behaviour it produces —
pseudo-addiction, meaning drug-seeking that resolves when the pain is adequately treated — exists
because the misclassification was common enough to need a name. The cost falls hardest on people
who are already least likely to be believed.

In the other direction, the reassurance that physical dependence is "not addiction" has been used
to minimise real risk, and that minimisation contributed to a prescribing pattern whose
consequences have been severe in several countries. Both errors come from the same place: treating
three distinct things as one, then arguing about which single thing it is.

The practical consequence is that abrupt cessation of a drug the body has adapted to is itself a
clinical event, with its own risks, and for some classes those risks are serious. The management
of that is a matter for the person's own prescriber and their national guidance, and it is not
something to be worked out from a revision answer. Nothing here describes how any particular drug
should be reduced or stopped, for anyone, including the reader. Anyone currently taking a medicine
they are worried about should raise it with their prescriber or pharmacist before changing
anything, because the change is the risky part and it is manageable when it is planned.

Finally, a note about language, because it is not decoration. "Addict", "abuse" and "clean" are
being replaced in professional writing by terms describing the condition rather than the person,
and the reason given is empirical rather than aesthetic: stigmatising terminology measurably
changes how clinicians assess the same described case.

  because they are drug-specific, effect-specific and contested.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material for a pharmacy undergraduate, a prescriber revising, or anyone already
training in the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and has had no clinical
or pharmacist review. It contains no doses, no tapering rates, no withdrawal timelines and no
incidence figures, and that is deliberate: those are drug-specific, they differ between countries,
and the formulary and your national withdrawal guidance are the authorities. Nothing here
indicates how any medicine should be reduced or stopped. Abrupt cessation of a drug the body has
adapted to is itself a clinical event and for some classes it is dangerous; anyone taking a
medicine they are concerned about should discuss it with their own prescriber or pharmacist rather
than act on anything read here. The diagnostic material is described, not reproduced — the
classification systems are the only authority for their own criteria.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanistic content — the two routes to tolerance, cross-tolerance following the receptor,
opponent-process adaptation and the mirror-image shape of a withdrawal syndrome — is mechanism and
does not date. Three things do. The **diagnostic criteria and the names of the diagnoses** have
been revised more than once in both major classification systems, and the current wording is
theirs. The **national guidance on reducing dependence-forming medicines** is comparatively recent
in several countries, is not uniform between them, and is actively being revised. And the
**terminology** is moving: the replacement of person-first language for older stigmatising terms
is ongoing rather than settled, and the professional bodies publishing on it differ in detail.
Check the classification system you work under and your own national guidance.
