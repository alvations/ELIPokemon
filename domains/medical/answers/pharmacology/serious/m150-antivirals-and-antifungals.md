# Selective toxicity is a property of the difference between the pathogen and you, and it shrinks.

An antimicrobial has to do something to the pathogen that it does not do to the host. That is only
possible where there is something to aim at — a structure or a process the pathogen has and the
host does not, or has in a usefully different form. The whole of this topic is the consequence of
how much such material each class of pathogen offers (**mechanism**).

Bacteria are prokaryotes and offer a great deal. Fungi are eukaryotes and offer very little.
Viruses have almost no machinery of their own at all, because they use the host's. The therapeutic
differences — number of available agents, width of spectrum, margin of safety, speed of
resistance, dependence on diagnosis, whether cure is even the goal — are all downstream of that
single fact.

Claims below are marked inline with what they rest on: (**mechanism**), (**definitional**),
(**consensus**), or (**country-dependent**).

## The ladder of available difference

```
   WHAT IS THERE TO AIM AT

   BACTERIA -- prokaryote: a different kind of cell
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     cell wall peptidoglycan        no human counterpart at all
     the bacterial ribosome         structurally distinct from the human one
     bacterial folate synthesis     humans take folate in; bacteria make it
     bacterial DNA gyrase and
       topoisomerase                distinct enzymes doing a shared job
     ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     consequence: several independent target families, each supporting a whole
     drug class, and several of them with NO host equivalent. A wide margin is
     available as a matter of structure, not of cleverness.

   FUNGI -- eukaryote: the same kind of cell
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     ergosterol in the membrane     the human membrane uses cholesterol:
                                    a DIFFERENT sterol, not a different idea
     the enzyme that makes it       a cytochrome P450 -- and so are many of
                                    the host's own enzymes
     beta-glucan in the cell wall   no human counterpart (the best target here)
     fungal nucleic acid handling   close enough to the host's to be dangerous
     ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     consequence: FEW target families, two of the main ones being a sterol and
     the enzyme that makes it, both of which have host analogues. Narrower
     margins, and inhibition of host enzymes as a built-in interaction problem.

   VIRUSES -- not a cell: the host's machinery is the machinery
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     the virus-coded polymerase     a genuine target, and the main one
     the virus-coded protease       a genuine target
     entry, fusion and release
       proteins                     genuine targets, often virus-specific
     everything else                is YOURS. Ribosomes, membranes, energy
                                    metabolism, nucleotide pools -- all host
     ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     consequence: the target list is the short list of proteins the virus codes
     for itself, which differs between virus families. So the agent is narrow
     by construction, and nucleoside analogues that the host's own polymerases
     can also use carry toxicity for exactly that reason.
```

The three blocks explain the headline numbers without needing any: there are many antibacterial
classes, few antifungal classes, and antiviral agents that work against one virus family and
nothing else (**consensus**).

## What follows for antifungals

Five consequences, all of them mechanistic (**mechanism**) with the practice around them
(**consensus**) and the specifics (**country-dependent**).

* **Narrower margins.** A drug aimed at a sterol or at a cytochrome P450 is aimed at something the
  host also has, in a different form. Host toxicity is therefore an expected feature rather than
  an accident, and some agents in this group are among the few antimicrobials for which plasma
  concentration monitoring is used — `m041`'s territory, and it is unusual for an antimicrobial to
  be there at all.
* **Interactions as a class effect.** An antifungal that inhibits a fungal cytochrome enzyme tends
  also to inhibit human ones, which makes interaction a predictable property of the class rather
  than a quirk of one member. `m009` is the mechanism and the time course; the point here is that
  you should expect it before you look it up, not be surprised by it.
* **Diagnosis is slow and often indirect.** Fungi grow slowly in culture, the specimen is often
  hard to obtain from the site that matters, and much diagnosis rests on antigen or molecular
  markers and on imaging rather than on isolating the organism. So empirical treatment is more
  often unavoidable, and susceptibility testing — routine for bacteria — is less available and
  less standardised.
* **Resistance is a real and growing problem**, including resistance that is intrinsic to a
  species rather than acquired, which makes species identification part of the treatment decision
  rather than an academic nicety.
* **Fungal infections concentrate in people with impaired immunity**, which means the population
  needing these drugs is also the population least able to tolerate them and most likely to be on
  several other things.

## What follows for antivirals

Six consequences, and the last two are the ones that change how treatment is organised.

* **Narrow spectrum by construction.** If the target is a protein the virus codes for itself, the
  agent works against the viruses that code for that protein. There is no useful analogue of a
  broad-spectrum antibacterial, which means **the agent presupposes the diagnosis**
  (**mechanism**).
* **The window is early.** Viral replication in an acute infection is typically at its highest
  early, often before or around the onset of symptoms, and much of the illness that follows is the
  host response rather than ongoing replication. A drug that blocks replication therefore has its
  effect concentrated in a narrow early window, which is why the practical question for acute
  antivirals is almost always one of timing rather than of choice (**consensus**).
* **Suppression is often the realistic aim, not cure.** Where a virus establishes a latent or
  integrated reservoir that no current agent reaches, stopping treatment allows rebound. The
  therapeutic goal becomes sustained suppression, which turns an infection into a long-term
  treatment problem and makes adherence a pharmacological variable rather than a courtesy —
  `m043`'s subject, with higher stakes.
* **Resistance emerges fast, and the reason is arithmetic.** A large replicating population and an
  error-prone replication step together make the appearance of a variant resistant to any single
  agent a near-certainty rather than a risk (**mechanism**). Hence the structural answer:
  **combination therapy**, chosen so that a single mutation cannot defeat the whole regimen. That
  is not caution; it is the only configuration that works, and it is why monotherapy for some
  viral infections is a recognised error.
* **Resistance testing exists and is used**, and for some infections treatment choice is guided by
  it. This is genuinely unlike the antibacterial situation, where an empirical choice followed by
  narrowing is the normal pattern — `m045`'s stewardship argument.
* **Host-directed agents are the alternative, and they trade one problem for another.** Targeting
  a host factor the virus needs sidesteps viral mutation but abandons selective toxicity by
  definition, because the target is yours.

## Why diagnosis is the bottleneck in both

Empirical antibacterial therapy is defensible because the agents are broad, the tests are
relatively fast and the likely pathogens are few. None of those three hold here (**mechanism**).

```
   WHY "TREAT NOW, TEST LATER" DOES NOT TRANSFER

                            antibacterial      antifungal         antiviral
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   agent covers many
     plausible pathogens         yes            partly              no
   test turnaround               hours-days     often days+         hours-days
   susceptibility testing
     routine and standardised    yes            less so             for some only
   illness often self-limiting   sometimes      rarely              OFTEN
   benefit depends on
     starting early              sometimes      yes                 critically
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

   The two bottom rows pull in OPPOSITE directions, and that tension is the
   whole clinical difficulty: the benefit is largest early, and early is
   exactly when you know least.
```

The self-limiting row is doing more work than it looks. Most acute viral illness resolves without
treatment, which means a trial of an antiviral has to detect a benefit on top of a high rate of
spontaneous recovery, which requires either a large trial or a population at high risk of not
recovering. That is why the evidence for these agents is often strongest in exactly the groups who
are hardest to recruit, and why a result in one population transfers badly to another — `m146`'s
generality problem with a specific cause.

## What is the same, and worth saying so

Nothing above suspends the ordinary rules. The pharmacokinetic reasoning in `m006` applies
unchanged. Dose adjustment in organ impairment, `m042`'s subject, applies and matters more because
the margins are narrower. The stewardship argument in `m045` applies in full: resistance is a
shared resource problem whether the organism is a bacterium, a fungus or a virus, and an
unnecessary course of an antifungal has the same structure of consequence as an unnecessary course
of an antibiotic. And the therapeutic-index reasoning in `m008` is why the antifungals are the
antimicrobials most likely to need monitoring.

The one derived rule worth memorising: **when the margin is narrow and the spectrum is narrow,
identifying the organism stops being good practice and becomes part of the treatment.**

## The human stakes, said plainly

Three things here are about people rather than about mechanism.

**These infections concentrate in people who are already seriously unwell**, through treatment
that suppresses immunity, through advanced organ disease, through prematurity, through untreated
or late-diagnosed infection that itself impairs immunity. The pharmacology above is therefore
practised in people with the least physiological reserve and the longest medication lists, which
is the setting in which every interaction and every dose adjustment above matters most.

**Delay is the dominant harm, and it is usually a systems delay rather than a knowledge gap.** The
specimen that was not taken, the test that was sent on a Friday, the imaging that was not
requested, the result that arrived in a system nobody was watching. `m147`'s argument applies
exactly: these are properties of the pathway, which is where the fix is.

**Access is the largest determinant of outcome worldwide, and it is not distributed by need.**
Antifungal and antiviral agents are expensive, some require infusion and monitoring, and
diagnostic capacity — not the drug — is the binding constraint in much of the world. The
documented consequence is that treatable infections go untreated for want of a test. This is also
where the resistance problem and the access problem meet: agricultural use of antifungal compounds
related to medical ones is an established driver of environmental resistance, and the countries
carrying the burden of the resulting infections are generally not the ones making that decision.
Naming that is a description of the structure, not a criticism of anyone's clinical practice.

Nothing in this answer indicates what anyone should take, start or stop. It names no agent, no
dose, no duration and no regimen, deliberately, and a question about a specific infection belongs
with the treating team, the local antimicrobial guideline and the infection specialist.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Why does a prokaryote offer more drug targets than a eukaryote? Give two targets with no human
  counterpart.
* Why is interaction a *class* property of one group of antifungals rather than an incidental
  feature?
* Why is there no useful equivalent of a broad-spectrum antibacterial among the antivirals?
* Explain, as arithmetic, why monotherapy fails for a rapidly replicating virus.
* What is the difference between suppression and cure here, and what makes the difference?
* Which two rows of the diagnosis table pull in opposite directions, and what clinical dilemma
  does that produce?
* Why is the evidence for acute antivirals hardest to generate in exactly the people who need them
  least — and what follows for the people who need them most?

This is revision material for a pharmacy undergraduate, a prescriber revising, or anyone already
training in the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and has had no clinical
or pharmacist review. **It names no antiviral or antifungal agent, no dose, no duration, no

guideline, the formulary and the infection specialist, all three of which are revised and all

an urgent clinical matter and not a revision question.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument — available difference determines available margin, and margin determines
everything else — is mechanism and does not date. The rest is one of the fastest-moving areas in
this specialty. **New antifungal classes** with genuinely new targets have been in late-stage
development, which would change the "few target families" claim if they reach practice.
**Antifungal resistance**, including the emergence of intrinsically resistant species in
healthcare settings, is actively changing which empirical choices are reasonable, and the
agricultural driver is an active policy question. **Antiviral development** moved faster in the
last decade than in the three before it, broad-acting agents against whole virus families are an
active research goal, and long-acting injectable formulations are changing the adherence picture
described above. **Resistance testing and molecular diagnostics** are becoming faster and more
available, which shifts the diagnosis bottleneck. Everything specific here should be checked
against your local guideline and your national formulary rather than trusted from this answer.
