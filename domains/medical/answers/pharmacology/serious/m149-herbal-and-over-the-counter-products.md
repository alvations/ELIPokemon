# A receptor cannot read a provenance. "Natural" describes where a molecule came from, and nothing a molecule does.

The word does real work in a shop and none at all in pharmacology. A molecule's behaviour is set
by its structure, the exposure achieved and the target it meets; whether it was extracted from a
plant or made in a reactor changes none of those (**mechanism**). The demonstrations are routine:
several staples of conventional therapeutics are plant-derived or were, and the most potent
poisons known are biological in origin. There is no pharmacological property that tracks origin.

What *is* different about herbal and over-the-counter products is real, important and almost
entirely **regulatory and behavioural** rather than pharmacological. Getting that distinction
right is the whole topic, because it tells you which problems to expect and which ones are
imaginary.

Claims below are marked inline with what they rest on: (**mechanism**), (**definitional**),
(**consensus**), or (**country-dependent**).

## What is actually different, and which column it is in

| | Prescribed medicine | Over-the-counter medicine | Herbal or traditional product |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Active entity | one, defined | one or a few, defined | often many, variably defined |
| Content per unit | specified and assayed | specified and assayed | varies by species, part, growing conditions, extraction and batch |
| Stated dose | yes | yes, for a licensed indication and duration | often absent or nominal |
| Evidential standard for marketing | full quality, safety and efficacy | usually full, with a track record of safe use | ranges from full licensing to a traditional-use registration to none at all, depending on country and product class |
| A prescriber in the loop | yes | sometimes a pharmacist; often nobody | usually nobody |
| Counted as a medicine by the user | yes | sometimes | frequently not |
| Pharmacology | the same physics as the other two columns | | |

The last row is the one that matters (**mechanism**). Everything above it differs. The behaviour
of the molecule does not.

## Why the interaction problem is harder

Five reasons, and only the first is about the person rather than the product.

**1. It is often not disclosed, because it was not asked about and does not feel like a
medicine.** This is the dominant mechanism and it is a history-taking problem, not a pharmacology
problem. A question about "medicines" reliably under-collects vitamins, supplements, herbal
products, topicals, inhalers borrowed from a relative, and anything bought in a supermarket. A
question that names the categories collects them (**consensus**).

**2. The interacting entity may be unknown.** A single-molecule drug interacts as that molecule. A
multi-constituent extract may contain several candidates and the responsible one may not be
established, which means the interaction can be real and reproducible while remaining
mechanistically unexplained (**mechanism**).

**3. The magnitude varies between products and between batches**, because the content does. An
interaction demonstrated with one preparation does not have a transferable size, and that is a
property of the manufacturing rather than of the pharmacology (**consensus**).

**4. The mechanisms are the ordinary ones.** There is nothing special here. Enzyme induction,
enzyme inhibition, transporter effects, additive or opposing pharmacodynamics and interference
with absorption — all of it is `m009`'s material, operating exactly as `m009` describes. The
classic and uncontroversial examples are worth knowing by name: an extract of St John's wort is a
potent inducer of drug-metabolising enzymes and transporters, with the slow onset and slow offset
that induction always has; grapefruit juice inhibits intestinal enzyme activity, with the fast
onset that inhibition always has (**consensus**). The direction, the time course and the
consequence all follow from `m009` and need no new theory.

**5. It matters most for the drugs where it matters most.** An interaction that shifts exposure by
some fraction is clinically unimportant for a drug with a wide margin and potentially serious for
one without. So the risk is concentrated in exactly the drugs `m008` is about — the narrow
therapeutic index, the ones that are monitored — and in the drugs whose failure is silent until
something happens.

## The over-the-counter half, which is a different problem

Herbal products fail mainly on content and disclosure. Over-the-counter medicines are usually well
characterised, and their characteristic failure is **duplication** (**mechanism**).

* **Combination products.** A single-pack remedy for a cold or for pain commonly contains an
  analgesic, sometimes with a second analgesic, a decongestant, an antihistamine or a cough
  suppressant. Someone taking a prescribed analgesic and a branded combination remedy may be
  taking the same molecule twice under two names. This is the single commonest route by which an
  over-the-counter purchase causes serious harm, and it is a naming problem rather than a
  pharmacological one (**consensus**).
* **The licensed over-the-counter indication is narrower than the drug.** The pack is licensed for
  a stated problem and usually a stated maximum duration, specifically so that continued use
  becomes a reason to seek advice rather than to buy another pack (**country-dependent**).
* **Self-medication can mask or delay.** Symptomatic relief that works postpones presentation, and
  for a minority of presentations that delay is the harm. This is `m012`'s safety-netting argument
  arriving in a pharmacy.
* **The same molecule is prescription-only somewhere else.** What is bought from a shelf in one
  country requires a prescription in another, with no pharmacological difference whatever
  (**country-dependent**).
* **The pharmacist is the control point**, and it is the one part of this structure that is
  designed. A supervised sale with a short set of questions is what converts an unsupervised
  purchase into something closer to a prescribing decision, and it only works if the person is
  asked and answers.

## What this changes in practice

The practical consequences are few and they are mostly about asking (**consensus**).

Ask by category rather than by the word "medicines": prescribed, bought, borrowed, herbal,
vitamins, supplements, topicals, teas and traditional preparations. Record what is actually being
taken rather than what was prescribed, because `m043` is right that those are different lists.
When a prescribed drug's effect changes without explanation, something added or stopped at the
other end is a leading candidate — and induction's slow onset and offset mean the temporal
relationship can be weeks wide, which is the thing that makes it hard to see. For the drugs where
exposure matters, the national formulary's interaction section is the place to check, and it is
the authority rather than any general principle stated here.

And the register matters: the argument for asking is not that these products are dangerous. It is
that they are **pharmacologically active**, which is, after all, why people take them.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Four things here are about people.

**People take these products for reasons that make sense.** Relief that conventional care did not
provide, a preference for something that feels less interventional, cost, access, cultural and
family practice, and the entirely reasonable inference that something sold freely in a shop must
be low-risk. None of that is ignorance, and a consultation whose purpose is to win that argument
will simply stop collecting the information it needed.

**The belief that "natural means safe" is a labelling artefact, not a failure of reasoning.** When
a product is sold with no stated dose, no stated active content and no warning, the packaging is
making an implicit claim. Correcting the belief is worth doing; blaming the person for holding it
is not.

**Non-disclosure is usually a question that was not asked.** Surveys consistently find that a
large share of people using these products have not told anyone in their clinical team, and the
commonest stated reason is that they were not asked and did not think it counted. That makes it a
fixable system problem, in the sense `m147` means, rather than a patient-behaviour problem.

**Regulation of these products is genuinely uneven, and that falls unevenly too.** Where
traditional and herbal products are a major part of how care is actually delivered, the quality
assurance available is often weaker, and adulteration and contamination of unregulated
preparations are documented problems — including undeclared pharmaceutical ingredients and heavy
metals. Saying so is a statement about supply chains and regulatory capacity, not about
traditional medicine, and the remedy is regulation and quality assurance rather than disapproval.

Nothing in this answer indicates what anyone should take, start or stop, including any herbal or
over-the-counter product. A question about a specific combination belongs with a pharmacist or
prescriber, who can look it up in the formulary.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Give the pharmacological definition of "natural". (There is not one — say why.)
* Which column of the three-way table does each real difference sit in: regulatory, behavioural or
  pharmacological?
* Why can an interaction with a multi-constituent extract be reproducible and mechanistically
  unexplained at the same time?
* An induction interaction and an inhibition interaction have different time courses. Which is
  which, and why does that make the induction one harder to attribute?
* The same shift in exposure is unimportant for one drug and serious for another. What property
  decides?
* By what route does an over-the-counter purchase most commonly cause serious harm?
* Why is a maximum duration printed on an over-the-counter pack, and what is it for?

## Scope and safety

This is revision material for a pharmacy undergraduate, a prescriber revising, or anyone already
training in the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and has had no clinical
or pharmacist review. It gives no doses, no interaction magnitudes, no maximum durations and no
list of products to avoid: the magnitude of a given interaction depends on the preparation, and
the authority is the formulary and the product information. The two named examples are textbook
illustrations of induction and inhibition, not a complete list and not a clinical instruction.
Nothing here indicates whether anyone should take, start or stop any medicine, supplement or
herbal product, including the reader's own. A question about a specific combination belongs with a
pharmacist or prescriber.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanism — provenance is not a pharmacological property, and the real differences are
regulatory and behavioural — does not date. The regulatory picture dates quickly and is the part
most worth re-checking. **How herbal and traditional products are regulated** differs by country
and is being revised in several, with registration schemes being introduced, tightened and in
places withdrawn. **Which medicines are available over the counter** changes in both directions:
products are reclassified from prescription-only to pharmacy supply and occasionally back.
**Supplement regulation** is the weakest-controlled part of the market in most jurisdictions and
is an active policy question. And the interaction evidence base for individual preparations is
still being built, so the absence of a documented interaction for a given product is not evidence
that there is none. Check your formulary and your national regulator rather than this answer.
