# Milcery evolves on how long you spun, and nobody writes that down

**Milcery** becomes **Alcremie** when it is holding a Sweet and the trainer spins. Three separate
inputs decide which Alcremie you end up with. The **Sweet** it was holding sets the decoration.
The direction and the length of the spin set the cream. And the time of day sets it further: a
short clockwise spin in daylight gives a Vanilla Cream, a long anticlockwise spin at night gives a
Mint Cream, and a spin in either direction in the evening gives a Rainbow Swirl. Seven Sweets
multiplied by nine creams is sixty-three different **Alcremie**, all from the same **Milcery**.

Two of those three inputs are things a trainer would naturally record — which Sweet, roughly what
time. **How long you spun is the one nobody writes down**, and it is doing as much work as the
other two put together. That asymmetry is the whole answer.

## Potency, and why the number is useless without its system

The healing ladder is explicit and numbered. In the **Kanto** games a **Potion** restores exactly
20 **HP**, a **Super Potion** 50, a **Hyper Potion** 200, and a **Max Potion** fills the bar; a
**Full Restore** fills it and clears status as well. Clean, ordered, usable.

Except the rungs were rebalanced in later generations. Quote the figure 50 for a **Super Potion**
without naming the generation it came from and you have handed somebody a number they cannot act
on. Topical corticosteroid potency is (**country-dependent**) in exactly that way. The United
Kingdom formulary uses four groups — mild, moderate, potent, very potent. The United States uses
seven numbered classes, I to VII, with class I the **strongest**, which is the opposite direction
from how most people assume a numbered scale runs. The same product can sit in differently named
groups in the two systems. **The formulary where you practise is the authority**, and that holds
for every figure in this answer.

## Vehicle, which changes both the pharmacology and whether it is used at all

The vehicle is not packaging; it is which **Ball** you threw. A **Net Ball** triples the catch
rate against a Water- or Bug-type and does nothing special against anything else. A **Dive Ball**
is worth three and a half times the base rate underwater and exactly nothing extra on land. A
**Nest Ball** is strong against a low-level target and tails off to nothing by level forty. Same
intention, same target, different carrier, and the right carrier is chosen for the circumstances.

For a topical the same molecule at the same strength is more potent in an ointment than in a
cream, because the ointment is occlusive, holds water in the stratum corneum and improves
penetration. Any occlusion does this — an ointment, a dressing, a nappy, a skin fold pressed
against itself — which is why the same preparation behaves differently in an axilla than on a
shin.

And the carrier has to be acceptable or it delivers nothing. A **Master Ball** that stays in the
bag catches less than a **Poké Ball** that gets thrown. Adherence is not a separate topic from
dose; it is part of the dose.

## Site, because the skin is not one organ

A **Dive Ball** underwater and the same **Dive Ball** on a **Route 1** verge are the same item
with entirely different results. The item did not change. The site did.

Percutaneous absorption works like that over a range that surprises people: thin skin — eyelids,
flexures, genital skin, the face — absorbs far more of the same preparation than palms and soles
do. Potency is matched to the **site**, not only to the severity, which is why one person on one
day may need different preparations on the face and on the shins.

## Quantity, and the unit that exists because of it

**Leftovers** restores a sixteenth of the holder's maximum **HP** at the end of every turn. That
is the arithmetic that matters here. Chip at something below that rate and the bar does not move;
you have not discovered that your move is useless, you have discovered that your damage per turn
is under the opponent's recovery per turn. The move works. The amount was wrong.

The **fingertip unit** is the ribbon of ointment or cream squeezed from a nozzle about 5 mm across
along the terminal phalanx of an adult index finger — about 0.5 g, covering roughly two adult
palms with the fingers included.

National formularies publish per-area quantity tables for precisely this reason, and the figures
run to around 100 g for a trunk or for both legs rather than the 30 g tube habit reaches for. Look
the table up rather than rebuilding it from the arithmetic, because the formulary figures are the
ones the pharmacy will use.

## Why the quantity is the variable that goes wrong

Several causes stack and none of them is carelessness. Topical corticosteroid phobia is
widespread, held by patients and prescribers alike, and it produces deliberate under-use on both
sides. The instruction to use the preparation sparingly, still in circulation, is an instruction
to under-treat. Tube sizes are dispensed by habit rather than by area. And most of all, almost
nobody is handed a unit. An instruction with no unit in it is the trainer who spun for an
unrecorded length of time and is surprised by the **Alcremie** they got.

## Why it presents as treatment failure

Deliver less per unit area than the inflammation needs and the course looks exactly like no
response. The usual conclusion is that the preparation does not work, and the usual next move is
to escalate potency or change molecule — the equivalent of reaching for a bigger attack when the
real problem was **Leftovers** out-ticking you. Both add risk and neither touches the variable
that was wrong. The cheaper step first is to establish how much was used, over what area, for how
long, and to weigh or ask after the tube. Fifteen grams out of a 30 g tube in a fortnight across a
widespread eruption is not a trial of that treatment.

## The other side, because this is not an argument for more

**Double-Edge** has 120 base power and takes a share of the damage straight back out of the user,
and **Explosion** ends the turn with the attacker gone. Stronger is not free.

Over-use of topical corticosteroids has real consequences: skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia,
easy bruising, perioral dermatitis and tachyphylaxis locally, and with very potent preparations
over large areas, under occlusion, or in infants, enough systemic absorption to suppress the
hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. The argument here is not that more is better. It is that the
quantity should be **specified** rather than guessed, in both directions, because a number nobody
states is a number nobody can get right.

## Where the metaphor stops

This section carries no Pokémon, because the mechanism is over and the consequence is not
something to be cute about.

Applying a topical preparation to most of the body twice a day is a substantial daily burden, and
it is routinely prescribed as though it were nothing. It takes real time. It ruins clothes and
bedding. Children resist it, and the adult doing it is often tired and alone. Many people cannot
reach their own back or their own feet.

Topical corticosteroid phobia is not irrationality to be corrected with a firmer tone. It comes
from real adverse effects, from contradictory advice given by different clinicians, and from years
of being told to use the preparation sparingly. Someone who has been frightened of a treatment is
not helped by being told the fear is unfounded; they are helped by being told what the amount is,
what it is for, and what would actually constitute too much.

The cost of mislabelling under-application as treatment failure is the part that lasts. A person
who is told that a treatment did not work for them walks away believing their skin cannot be
treated, and that belief outlives the consultation by years. Establishing how much was used costs
a minute and prevents it.

And without softening: no quantity, potency class or preparation named in this answer is for any
reader's own use. Anyone worried about a treatment they have been given needs to speak to the
prescriber or pharmacist who issued it.

## What a Gym Leader is listening for

Why an ointment is more potent than a cream carrying the same molecule at the same strength. What
you would check before escalating potency in someone whose eczema has not responded. Why United
States class I and United Kingdom group four are both the strongest, and what that does to your
reading of a paper from the other country. And how you would phrase a quantity so somebody can act
on it without a tape measure.

## Scope and safety

Revision material for someone already training in or qualified for the field. Not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and not reviewed by a clinician. It is not for use in making a
decision about anyone's care, including your own.

**No quantity, potency class or preparation named here is a prescribing instruction, and none of
it addresses any reader's own treatment.** The figures appear so the reasoning behind them is
visible — why a unit exists at all, why the amount is the variable that fails — and not so they
can be applied to a person. An answer here agreeing with your memory is not confirmation. Check
the formulary before anything reaches a patient, and note that the paediatric quantities, which
differ by age, have deliberately not been reproduced.

Anyone whose skin condition is not improving, or who is worried about a treatment they have been
given, needs to discuss it with the prescriber or pharmacist who issued it rather than with a
revision page.

Local guidance and the formulary where you practise are the authority on all of this, and they
differ by country and by institution.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The fingertip unit and the per-area counts have been stable for a long time and are reproduced
consistently across formularies, which is why they are usable — more consistently than **Hyper
Potion**, which has not been. The potency classifications remain genuinely divergent between
countries with no sign of convergence, so naming the system alongside the class is a permanent
discipline rather than a temporary one. What has moved recently is the attention given to topical
corticosteroid withdrawal and to steroid phobia as a named barrier to treatment, and that
discussion is live rather than settled as of October 2026.
