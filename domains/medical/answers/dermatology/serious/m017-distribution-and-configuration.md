# A papule tells you the compartment; the map tells you the route

Fifty papules are fifty papules. Fifty in a unilateral band that stops at the midline, fifty on
the extensor elbows and knees, fifty on the face and the V of the neck while the submental skin is
clean, and fifty confined to the axillae and groin are four different problems, and the morphology
is identical in all four. Distribution and configuration are not context for the primary
morphology. They are co-equal information, and the reason is *mechanism*: the primary morphology
says which layer of the skin the process is in, and the distribution says **how whatever is
causing it arrived there.**

The single most informative item in that table is the first one, and it is the one that is
reasoned from *mechanism* rather than memorised. **Symmetry implies a systemic or blood-borne
delivery**, because there is no plausible external exposure that lands identically on both
antecubital fossae. Asymmetry implies something arrived from outside. It is a heuristic with
exceptions rather than a rule, but it is the heuristic that does the most work, and it costs
nothing to apply.

The second most useful move is to look at what is **spared**. A photo-distributed eruption is
identified by the clean skin under the chin, behind the ears and in the upper eyelid creases, not
by the involved skin — which is why the shaded sites must be examined deliberately, and why a
close-up photograph of an affected cheek is nearly worthless for this.

Three systems of line exist and they are not interchangeable, which is worth separating because
they look alike on skin. A **dermatomal** band follows one sensory nerve root. **Blaschko's
lines** follow the embryonic migration of skin cells and are neither dermatomes nor vascular;
mosaic and genetic conditions follow them. And lesions appearing **in a line of trauma** — the
Koebner phenomenon — follow the injury, not any anatomy, which is why a straight scratch line of
lesions is a statement about the skin's tendency rather than about a nerve.

## What this means for how you actually look

It means the examination has an order, and the order is not negotiable: **from a distance first**.
Distribution and configuration are only visible at arm's length or further, across the whole
undressed skin surface. Only then close in for morphology, and only then palpate. Reversing the
order is how a plaque on a forearm gets described beautifully and diagnosed wrongly.

It also means the whole surface has to be seen. The sites skipped most often are the ones that
most often settle it by *consensus* among standard texts: scalp, behind the ears, the nails, the
mouth, the genitals, the natal cleft, between the toes, and the soles.

## Limits, stated honestly

Distribution is suggestive, not diagnostic. The patterns overlap, more than a table like the one
above admits. Age changes the distribution of the same condition. Scratching and treatment both
redistribute findings — a potent topical used on part of a rash will clear that part and make an
originally symmetrical eruption look asymmetrical. And a **single** lesion has no distribution at
all: a solitary changing lesion is reasoned about along a different route entirely, which is the
subject of the morphology and dermoscopy answers in this set, not this one.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Examining the whole skin surface means asking someone to undress completely, and then looking at
the scalp, behind the ears, the natal cleft, the genitals, between the toes and the soles of the
feet. That is intrusive. It is also exactly why those sites get skipped, and skipping them is how
diagnoses are missed — so the answer is not to skip them but to do it properly: explain why each
region matters, offer a chaperone, expose sequentially, and accept a refusal without making the
person justify it.

Widespread visible disease carries a social cost that is easy to under-weigh from behind a desk.
People cover up in hot weather, avoid swimming, avoid changing rooms, stop shaking hands. Asking
what the rash stops them doing is a better question than asking how itchy it is, and it changes
management more often.

And without softening: the reasoning in this answer is about groups of lesions and does not
transfer to one. Nobody can use it to settle a worry about a mole. A new, changing, bleeding or
non-healing lesion needs to be seen in person, and a rash with fever, or one that does not blanch,
needs to be seen urgently.

## What an examiner digs into next

Why the spared sites identify a photo-distributed eruption and the affected sites do not. What
symmetry rules out, and what the exceptions are. The difference between Blaschko's lines and a
dermatome, and why confusing them changes the differential. And what you would do differently if
the patient had already been treating half the rash for three weeks.

## Scope and safety

Revision material for someone already training in or qualified for the field. Not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and not reviewed by a clinician. It is not for use in making a
decision about anyone's care, including your own.

This answer explains how distribution and configuration narrow a differential. It deliberately
stops short of naming diagnoses for the patterns, and it cannot be used to identify a rash on a
real person. **Nothing here can be used to reassure anyone about a mole or any other single
lesion** — the reasoning in this answer is about groups of lesions and does not apply to one, and
suspected-skin-cancer pathways exist precisely because single lesions are not reliably
self-assessable. Anyone with a new, changing, bleeding or non-healing lesion, or a widespread or
rapidly spreading rash, needs to be seen in person, and a rash with fever or a rash that does not
blanch needs to be seen urgently.

Local guidance is the authority on referral and escalation, and it differs by country and by
institution.
