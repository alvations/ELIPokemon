# Three different failures produce one white patch, and only one is reversed by making more pigment

Three people have pale patches. The first has fine scaly macules across the upper back that came
on over a summer. The second has sharply demarcated chalk-white patches on the knuckles and around
the mouth, with a few white hairs inside them. The third has a pale patch that has been there
since infancy, the same size relative to the limb it sits on, with no scale and no change.

Grouping these by colour puts them together. Grouping them by what has failed pulls them apart,
and the three groups behave differently in every way that matters: the examination that separates
them, whether the colour can come back, where it would come back from, and whether the treatment
is aimed at the pigment or at the cell that makes it.

The pigmentary disorders are best taught as **three failures in one short pathway** — melanin is
not made, melanin is made but ends up in the wrong compartment, or the cell that makes it is gone.
This answer is about those mechanisms. It is **not** about how pigment change is described, or why
the standard descriptive vocabulary fails in darker skin; that argument is m018's and is not
re-derived here.

## Markers used in this answer

(**mechanism**) — follows from the biology and is checkable by reasoning. (**definitional**) — a
term's meaning. (**consensus**) — standard across current textbooks and national guidance.
(**country-dependent**) — differs between countries or institutions, and yours is the authority.

## The pathway, which is short enough to hold in mind

```
   THE EPIDERMAL MELANIN UNIT, AND THE THREE PLACES IT FAILS
   ==================================================================================

   STEP 1 -- SYNTHESIS, inside the melanocyte
   ------------------------------------------
     melanocyte sits in the BASAL layer, neural-crest derived
     tyrosine --[ TYROSINASE, a copper-dependent enzyme ]--> melanin
     packaged into MELANOSOMES; eumelanin and pheomelanin are
     different products of the same organelle
          |
          |   FAILS HERE -> the cell is present and the pigment is not.
          |   Hypopigmented, never chalk-white. Hair and eyes may be
          |   involved if the defect is constitutional.
          v
   STEP 2 -- TRANSFER, melanocyte to keratinocyte
   ----------------------------------------------
     dendrites deliver melanosomes to the surrounding basal
     keratinocytes, which stack them over the nucleus
     ONE melanocyte serves a fixed group of neighbours
          |
          |   FAILS HERE -> or runs too hard, or delivers to the wrong
          |   compartment altogether. This is where most of the common
          |   disorders live, and where the epidermal/dermal split decides
          |   the prognosis.
          v
   STEP 3 -- CARRIAGE AND DISPOSAL
   -------------------------------
     the keratinocyte carries its pigment upward and is SHED
     => epidermal melanin has a route out, on the timescale of
        epidermal turnover
     => melanin dropped into the DERMIS has no such route. Free, or
        inside dermal macrophages, it is cleared slowly or not at all
   ==================================================================
   AND SEPARATELY -- THE CELL ITSELF IS GONE
   -----------------------------------------
     no melanocyte, so no amount of upstream stimulus produces
     pigment. DEPIGMENTED, not hypopigmented. Recovery requires
     REPOPULATION, which is a different problem from synthesis.
   ==================================================================
   Melanocyte NUMBER per unit area is broadly similar across skin
   phototypes. What differs is melanosome size, degree of
   melanisation and dispersion within the keratinocyte.
   (**consensus**) That single fact explains why the same insult
   produces a more marked and more persistent pigment response in
   darker skin, and it is the mechanism behind m018's observation
   rather than a restatement of it.
   ==================================================================
```

## Failure one: the pigment is not made

The melanocyte is present, countable, and not doing its job. (**mechanism**) The appearance is
**hypopigmentation** — paler than the surrounding skin, with pigment still detectable — and never
the chalk-white of true depigmentation.

The constitutional version is the group of oculocutaneous albinisms, where a step in the synthetic
pathway is defective. (**consensus**) Because the same pathway operates in the hair bulb and in
the eye, hair and iris are involved too, and the ocular findings are part of the diagnosis rather
than an association. (**mechanism**) Tyrosinase being copper-dependent is the reason copper
deficiency produces hypopigmentation, and the reason some of the inherited forms are partial
rather than complete. (**mechanism**)

The common acquired version is **pityriasis versicolor**, where a commensal yeast overgrows in the
stratum corneum and the hypopigmentation is an interference with pigment production rather than
loss of cells. (**mechanism**, **consensus**) Two features follow directly and are worth having:
the scale is superficial and demonstrable, and the colour change **outlasts the organism**, so a
treated patient whose patches have not yet recolourised has not failed treatment. Explaining that
in advance prevents a second unnecessary course. The sampling argument for confirming the organism
is m086's and belongs there.

Other causes of reduced production without cell loss include the hypopigmentation that follows
some inflammatory dermatoses, where melanocyte function is suppressed rather than the cell
destroyed, and post-inflammatory hypopigmentation in general, which recovers as the inflammation
settles. (**consensus**)

## Failure two: the pigment is made, and the question is where it ends up

This is the largest group, and the single most useful distinction in it is **which compartment the
melanin is in**, because that determines whether it has a route out. (**mechanism**)

**Epidermal.** Increased production and transfer into basal keratinocytes, which will be shed.
Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation after acne, eczema or lichenoid inflammation is mostly this;
so is the epidermal component of melasma; so is the café-au-lait macule, where the melanocyte
count is normal and the melanin content of the basal keratinocytes is raised. (**consensus**)
Epidermal pigment fades, slowly, if the stimulus stops.

**Dermal.** When the basal layer is damaged, melanin is dropped into the dermis — **pigment
incontinence** — where it sits free or within macrophages. (**mechanism**, **definitional**) This
is the mechanism of the slate-grey or blue-grey discolouration of lichen planus pigmentosus, the
pigmented variants of fixed drug eruption, some drug-induced pigmentation, and the dermal
component of melasma. It has no clearance route through the epidermis, so it persists for months
to years and responds poorly or not at all to anything aimed at pigment synthesis. (**consensus**)

**Mixed**, which in practice is most melasma.

Because the compartment predicts the prognosis, there is a bedside test for it. **Wood's lamp**
examination with long-wave ultraviolet light accentuates epidermal pigment and does not accentuate
dermal pigment, and it accentuates true depigmentation sharply. (**consensus**) It is the cheapest
useful instrument in this territory and it answers the one question that changes what you say
about how long the colour will take to go.

Transfer can also simply be defective without any inflammation. The classic is **naevus
depigmentosus**, where melanocytes are present in normal number and melanosome transfer is
impaired: a stable, segmental, off-white patch present from early life, which is why the third
patient above has had the same lesion since infancy. (**consensus**)

And one lesion in this differential is not a pigment problem at all. **Naevus anaemicus** is a
localised vascular phenomenon — a persistent area of vasoconstriction — so it is pale without any
melanin abnormality. Two consequences follow mechanically: it does not accentuate under Wood's
lamp, and rubbing the surrounding skin makes the border vanish because the normal skin flushes and
the lesion cannot. (**mechanism**) It is the one entry in the white-patch differential where the
melanocyte is irrelevant.

## Failure three: the cell is gone, which is a zero and not a reduction

**Vitiligo** is loss of melanocytes from affected skin, immune-mediated, and the clinical
consequences all follow from the absence being absolute rather than partial. (**mechanism**,
**consensus**) The patches are **depigmented** — chalk- or milk-white rather than merely pale —
with sharply convex borders, they accentuate strongly under Wood's lamp, and white hairs within a
patch indicate that the follicular melanocytes have gone too. New lesions at sites of friction or
injury are the Koebner phenomenon. (**consensus**) It is associated with other autoimmune disease,
thyroid disease most consistently, which is why a review of systems is part of the assessment
rather than an optional extra. (**consensus**)

The acquired non-immune version is **chemical leukoderma**, where exposure to certain phenolic and
catecholic compounds destroys melanocytes directly. (**consensus**) It matters because it is
occupational and therefore preventable, and because it is the one depigmenting disorder where
taking an exposure history changes the outcome.

Melanocytes are also lost where the epidermis has been scarred: in discoid lupus, in burns and in
any scarring process, the depigmentation is permanent because the structure has gone with it.
(**mechanism**)

The clinical discriminator between failure three and failures one and two is therefore a single
question — **hypopigmented or depigmented?** — and it is answerable at the bedside. Treating a
depigmented patch with something that inhibits pigment production, or expecting it to fade back,
both fail for the same reason: there is no cell to act on. The reasoning is the one m007 sets out
for a drug with no mechanism against its target, and it is the same shape here.

## The reservoir, which predicts what will repigment and where from

The most useful mechanical fact about recovery is that the epidermis is not the only place
melanocytes live. A population persists in the **outer root sheath of the hair follicle**, and
repopulation of depigmented epidermis proceeds from there. (**mechanism**, **consensus**)

Three predictions follow and all three are observable:

* repigmentation in vitiligo characteristically appears as **perifollicular dots** inside a patch,
  spreading outward, rather than as uniform fading of the border;
* sites with few or no follicles — the fingertips, the palms and soles, the lips and the other
  mucosal margins — repigment poorly, which is why acral and perioral disease is the hard kind;
* a patch containing **white hairs** has lost the reservoir as well as the epidermal population,
  and predicts a worse response.

That is the same argument m087 makes about scarring and non-scarring hair loss: the recoverable
case is the one where the source structure survives, and the irreversible case is the one where it
does not. The two answers are describing one principle from two directions.

## What the mechanism changes about the plan

Three things, each at the level of principle. No agent, concentration, strength or regimen appears
in this answer.

**Treat the cause before the colour.** Post-inflammatory pigment change is driven by the
inflammation, so suppressing ongoing eczema, acne or lichenoid disease is the pigmentary
intervention, and anything aimed at the pigment while the inflammation continues is working
against a live source. (**mechanism**)

**Photoprotection is mechanistic, not cosmetic.** Ultraviolet exposure drives melanogenesis and
deepens existing hyperpigmentation differentially, which is why pigment-matching worsens over a
summer. The dose argument is m088's. (**mechanism**, **consensus**)

**Aim at the step that failed.** Reduced production can be addressed by reducing the stimulus or
by inhibiting synthesis; dermal pigment has no synthetic step left to inhibit; and depigmentation
needs repopulation — which is why the interventions for vitiligo are of a different kind from
those for melasma, and why depigmenting the remaining normal skin is a recognised option in
extensive stable disease rather than a contradiction. Which treatments are available, funded and
offered differs substantially between countries and services. (**country-dependent**)

## The human stakes, said plainly

No analogy in this section.

Pigmentary disorders are frequently described as cosmetic, and that description is wrong in a way
that affects care. Visible difference in pigmentation on the face and hands is strongly associated
with psychological distress, with avoidance of social and working situations, and in several
cultural contexts with stigma that extends to marriage and employment. The quality-of-life burden
of vitiligo in particular has been measured repeatedly and is substantial. Treating the condition
as trivial, or declining to discuss it because it is not dangerous, is a failure of the
consultation rather than a reasonable triage decision.

Two specific points are worth saying aloud rather than leaving implied. First, depigmented skin
has no melanin and therefore no photoprotection from melanin, so sunburn in those areas happens
faster and matters more; the practical advice around that is the only part of this territory that
is urgent. Second, skin-lightening products bought outside regulated supply are widely used for
hyperpigmentation and some have contained potent corticosteroids, mercury compounds or
hydroquinone at unregulated strength, with real harms including irreversible paradoxical
pigmentation. Asking what someone has already used, without disapproval, usually changes the
assessment.

Nothing in this answer is for any reader's own use, and no treatment named or implied here is a
recommendation. A new pale or dark patch, a pigmented lesion that is changing, a pale patch with
reduced sensation, or pigment change beginning after a new medicine, all need assessment in
person — the last two because leprosy and drug-induced pigmentation are both diagnoses that are
made by someone thinking of them.

## What an examiner digs into next

Why melanocyte number is similar across phototypes when melanin content is not, and what that
predicts. The difference between hypopigmented and depigmented, and how you decide at the bedside.
What Wood's lamp accentuates and what it does not, and why that is a prognostic test rather than a
diagnostic one. Why dermal melanin persists when epidermal melanin does not. The mechanism of
pigment incontinence and which eruptions produce it. Why pityriasis versicolor leaves pale skin
after the organism has gone. How naevus anaemicus is distinguished from naevus depigmentosus with
no instrument at all. Where melanocytes repopulate from, and the three predictions that follow.
And why calling any of this cosmetic changes the care.

## Scope and safety

Revision material for someone already training in or qualified for the field. Not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and not reviewed by a clinician. It is not for use in making a
decision about anyone's care, including your own.

**No agent, strength, concentration, phototherapy dose or treatment schedule appears in this
answer, and none of it addresses any reader's own treatment.** The omission is deliberate: topical
depigmenting and repigmenting agents differ in licensed strength and in availability between
countries, several are available outside regulated supply at strengths that cause harm, and a
figure quoted here without the formulary would be worse than no figure.

Anyone with a new or changing pigmented lesion, a pale patch with reduced sensation, pigment
change that began after starting a medicine, or widespread depigmentation, needs to be assessed in
person. A changing pigmented lesion is assessed urgently.

Local guidance and the policy where you practise are the authority on all of this, and they differ
by country and by institution.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The biology is settled and is the part worth memorising: three steps, three failure modes, and the
epidermal-versus-dermal compartment question that predicts whether colour can return. The
reservoir argument is settled too, and it is the one that makes the distribution of vitiligo's
treatment response intelligible rather than arbitrary.

What moves is treatment. Vitiligo has had the most movement of anything in this answer: targeted
immunomodulatory topical therapy has reached licensing in several jurisdictions within the last
few years, which changed the conversation from camouflage and phototherapy to active treatment,
and whether any of it is available or funded where you practise is firmly (**country-dependent**).
Phototherapy protocols and the combination of phototherapy with topical treatment continue to be
revised. Surgical melanocyte transfer remains specialist and its indications are narrow. In
melasma the move has been away from single-agent topical treatment toward photoprotection plus
combination regimens, with the visible-light component of ultraviolet-independent pigmentation
being taken more seriously than it was. Regulatory action on unlicensed skin-lightening products
is active in several countries and the specific compounds named in warnings change. Current as of
October 2026.
