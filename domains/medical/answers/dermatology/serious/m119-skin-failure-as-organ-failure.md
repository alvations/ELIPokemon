# The skin is an organ with six jobs, and losing most of it is organ failure with a dermatological cause

Someone is admitted red from head to foot, scaling, shivering under a blanket in a warm room, with
a pulse faster than their illness seems to warrant and ankles that have begun to swell. The
dermatological question is what caused it. The clinical question in the first hours is a different
one entirely, and it is the one this answer is about: what has the body lost, and what is going
wrong as a consequence.

The useful framing is **skin failure** — the proposition that extensive loss of skin function is
organ failure, and should be assessed and monitored like organ failure rather than like a rash
over a large area. (**definitional**) The term is not universally adopted and the concept is; the
reason to reach for it is that it generates the right observations, and a reader who has only the
word "erythroderma" tends to generate dermatological ones.

m089 sets out what happens when the barrier is lost over an area in a blistering disease, and m051
sets out the barrier argument for eczema. This answer is the organ-level version: all six
functions, not just the barrier, and the systemic consequences of losing each.

## Markers used in this answer

(**mechanism**) — follows from the physiology and is checkable by reasoning. (**definitional**) —
a term's meaning. (**consensus**) — standard across current textbooks and national guidance.
(**country-dependent**) — differs between countries or institutions, and yours is the authority.

## Six jobs, six failures

```
   WHAT THE SKIN DOES, AND WHAT GOES WRONG AT SCALE
   ==================================================================================

   1  PERMEABILITY BARRIER
      stratum corneum with its intercellular lipid lamellae
      FAILS -> transepidermal water loss rises; insensible fluid and
               electrolyte loss; dehydration and hypernatraemia.
               Requirement rises and the loss is invisible because it
               is not in a bag. (**mechanism**)

   2  THERMOREGULATION
      a controlled radiator: vasoconstrict to conserve, vasodilate
      and sweat to lose
      FAILS -> the COUNTERINTUITIVE one. Inflamed skin is widely
               vasodilated and CANNOT conserve heat, so the
               characteristic failure is HYPOTHERMIA, with shivering
               and a raised metabolic rate, in someone who looks hot.
               Where sweating is also impaired, the opposite failure
               -- inability to lose heat -- can occur instead.
               (**mechanism**, **consensus**)

   3  A HAEMODYNAMIC COMPARTMENT
      a large vascular bed under tone
      FAILS -> cutaneous vasodilation over a large area lowers
               peripheral resistance and raises cardiac output.
               High-output state, peripheral oedema, and
               decompensation of pre-existing cardiac disease.
               (**mechanism**, **consensus**)

   4  A STRUCTURAL PROTEIN STORE
      scale is protein; the epidermis is continuously rebuilt
      FAILS -> large-area shedding is a protein drain.
               Hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, negative nitrogen balance,
               and a raised nutritional requirement at the moment
               someone is least able to eat. (**mechanism**)

   5  A BARRIER AGAINST INFECTION
      physical exclusion, plus resident flora, plus local immunity
      FAILS -> colonisation, fissuring, cellulitis, and bacteraemia
               with the skin as the portal -- which is the mechanism
               behind the mortality in this group. (**consensus**)

   6  A PHARMACOKINETIC BARRIER
      the stratum corneum is the rate-limiting step for percutaneous
      absorption
      FAILS -> absorption of anything applied rises substantially
               over a large broken area, so a topical agent that is
               safe on a small area may not be. The quantity
               argument is m019's. (**mechanism**, **consensus**)
   ==================================================================
   AND, over the longer term, cutaneous vitamin D synthesis and the
   sensory and mechanical protective functions -- the latter being
   why shear injury and pressure damage are live risks in someone
   who is immobile with fragile skin.
   ==================================================================
```

One generalisation about those six is worth stating because reasoning the other way is a common
error. The six functions are **not spared or lost as a block**, and the set that fails is
determined by the *kind* of skin failure rather than by its extent. (**mechanism**) Extensive
exfoliation with an intact epidermis beneath loses protein and heat heavily while the permeability
barrier is comparatively preserved; widespread erosion loses fluid and offers a portal for
infection out of proportion to the protein drain; and a condition that also impairs sweating fails
thermoregulation in the opposite direction from the usual one. So an assessment that reads a
single severity figure off the body surface area and infers the whole physiological picture from
it has inferred too much. Each of the six is assessed on its own.

## Erythroderma, as the presentation this usually takes

**Erythroderma** is erythema with scaling affecting most of the skin surface. (**definitional**)
It is conventionally defined with a percentage of body surface area; that figure is in the
documents named below and is deliberately not reproduced here, for the same reason m089 omits the
detachment thresholds — a number quoted without its source is worse than the principle stated
cleanly.

It is a **syndrome with a differential, not a diagnosis**, and the distinction matters because the
supportive management is nearly identical across causes while the specific treatment is not.
(**consensus**)

| Cause group | What points to it |
| --- | --- |
| **Psoriasis** | Pre-existing history, nail changes, scalp involvement, typical plaques at the margins or in previously affected sites. Often precipitated by abrupt withdrawal of systemic corticosteroid or by another trigger — m052's prescribing trap |
| **Atopic or other eczema** | Pre-existing history, flexural emphasis earlier in the course, lichenification. m051's territory |
| **Drug reaction** | Timing relative to a new medicine, facial oedema, fever, eosinophilia, organ involvement. m054's territory and its thresholds for concern are deliberately low |
| **Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, including Sézary syndrome** | Lymphadenopathy, intense itch, long prodrome of "eczema that was never quite eczema", circulating atypical lymphocytes. The cause most often diagnosed late |
| **Pityriasis rubra pilaris** | **Islands of sparing** within the erythema, palmoplantar keratoderma, follicular prominence |
| **Crusted scabies** | Heavy crusting, immunosuppression or institutional setting, and extremely high mite burden — m086's territory, and the one where the treatment is wholly different |
| **Extensive immunobullous disease** | m089's territory |
| **In neonates and infants** | Inherited ichthyoses, immunodeficiency, and metabolic causes. This is a different differential and a paediatric emergency |
| **Idiopathic** | A substantial proportion remain unexplained after full assessment, and some declare a cause years later |

The diagnostic point worth having is that **the clue usually survives the generalisation.** Scalp,
nails, mucosae, the palms and soles, the margins, and any island of spared skin are where the
original disease is still legible after everything else has become uniformly red. (**mechanism**)
Those are the places to examine deliberately rather than the places most obviously affected.

And the biopsy caveat, which hands over to m120: histology in erythroderma is frequently
non-specific, the yield is better from multiple sites, and repeating it later in the course is a
recognised strategy rather than an admission of failure. (**consensus**)

## What is monitored, and why each one is on the list

Each of these follows from a numbered function above, which is the reason the framing earns its
keep. (**mechanism**)

* **Temperature**, because the failure direction is counterintuitive and someone who looks hot may
  be losing heat. A warm ambient environment is treatment, not comfort.
* **Fluid balance and electrolytes**, because the loss is insensible and therefore absent from the
  chart unless someone accounts for it. The limits of what a fluid balance chart measures are
  m005's argument and they apply with force here.
* **Albumin and nutritional assessment**, because the protein drain is continuous and the
  requirement is raised.
* **Circulation**, because the high-output state is a mechanism and not a coincidence, and because
  pre-existing cardiac disease is what makes it dangerous.
* **Infection surveillance**, with the discipline m055 sets out about not treating colonisation as
  infection, held against the fact that the portal here is enormous.
* **The medicines list**, in both directions: a culprit drug to stop, and the altered absorption
  of anything applied.

## What is done, at the level of principle

No agent, dose, strength, regimen, fluid volume or temperature target appears in this answer. The
principles are these, and they are deliberately the ones that do not depend on the cause.
(**consensus**)

**Supportive care is the treatment that keeps someone alive while the cause is identified**, and
it is not holding measures. Warmth, fluid and electrolyte replacement, nutritional support, bland
emollient care over the whole surface, meticulous handling to reduce shear, pressure-area care,
and infection surveillance. In extensive disease this is recognisably the same list as care after
a burn of similar extent, and in several countries extensive cases are managed in or with a burns
or specialist dermatology inpatient unit for that reason. (**country-dependent**)

**Stopping a culprit drug is the highest-yield specific action available**, because it is the one
cause that is reversed by subtraction and the one where delay is costly.

**Specific treatment of the cause follows the cause**, and in the acute phase it frequently cannot
start until the diagnosis is clearer — which is the reason supportive care is not a waiting room.
Two cautions are standard: systemic corticosteroid can precipitate severe deterioration in
erythrodermic psoriasis, and immunosuppression given before an infective or infestive cause is
excluded can be harmful, crusted scabies being the specific case where the mistake is well
described. (**consensus**)

## The human stakes, said plainly

No analogy in this section.

Erythroderma from any cause carries real mortality, and the deaths are predominantly from the
mechanisms set out above — infection with the skin as the portal, cardiovascular decompensation,
metabolic and fluid derangement — rather than from the dermatosis itself. Nothing in this answer
can tell anyone what will happen to any particular person; that belongs with the team who have the
findings. But the framing exists precisely because treating this as a severe rash rather than as
organ failure has a known and avoidable cost, and that is worth saying without hedging.

The experience is harder than the physiology suggests. Itch in erythroderma is frequently severe
and unrelenting, and in the lymphoma-associated forms it is often the dominant symptom and the
hardest to relieve. Shivering while feeling hot is distressing and is routinely misread by
everyone present, including the person. Being cold, exposed for repeated examinations, handled
frequently, unable to sleep and visibly changed from head to foot is a cumulative burden that the
observation chart does not record. The appearance is frightening to have and frightening for
family to see, and whole-body redness and shedding draws attention in a way that has its own cost.

Three things follow for anyone doing this work. Warmth and minimal handling are clinical
interventions and should be prescribed and recorded as such. Itch and discomfort need to be
addressed actively and by people with the relevant expertise, and treating them as secondary to
the physiological problem is a mistake rather than a prioritisation. And when a cause cannot be
found — which happens, and persists in a meaningful proportion — saying so plainly, with what the
plan is anyway, is better than an implied diagnosis that later changes.

Without softening: nothing here is for any reader's own use, and no treatment named or implied is
a recommendation. Widespread redness of the skin with shivering, fever, feeling unwell, or redness
beginning after a new medicine, is a medical emergency and needs assessment the same day — call
your local emergency number or use your local urgent care route. A red, scaling, unwell newborn or
infant is a paediatric emergency.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physiology is settled and is the part worth memorising: six functions, and the systemic
consequence of each failing. The hypothermia point is settled and is still routinely got wrong.
The differential for erythroderma is settled, as is the observation that a substantial proportion
remain idiopathic after full assessment.

What moves is the specific treatment of each cause, and in two places the organisation of care.
Biologic and small-molecule therapy has changed the management of erythrodermic psoriasis and of
severe eczema in several countries, which has shortened some of these admissions; availability and
funding are firmly (**country-dependent**). Treatment of Sézary syndrome and advanced cutaneous
lymphoma has moved with the arrival of targeted agents, and the pathways differ by country. The
framing of extensive skin loss as organ failure requiring critical-care-adjacent or burns-adjacent
management has gained ground rather than being settled, and where these patients are physically
managed differs markedly between health systems — which is the part of this answer most likely to
read differently depending on where you practise. The term "skin failure" itself is still
contested in the literature, though what it denotes is not. Current as of October 2026.
