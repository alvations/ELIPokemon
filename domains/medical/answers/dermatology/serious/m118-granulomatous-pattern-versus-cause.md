# A granuloma is the tissue's answer to a question the pathologist cannot see, and the report says so

A biopsy comes back as "granulomatous dermatitis". It is a true, careful and useful report, and it
narrows nothing by itself, because the differential it leaves open contains an infection that
needs treating with antimicrobials and an immune-mediated disease that needs treating with
immunosuppression — two plans pointing in opposite directions.

That is not a defect in histopathology. It is what a granuloma *is*. A granuloma is a **pattern of
response**, mounted when macrophages meet something they cannot clear or digest, and the pattern
is determined much more by the tissue's limited repertoire than by the identity of the stimulus.
(**mechanism**) So the report names the response. Finding the cause is a separate piece of work,
done with different instruments, and the commonest failure in this territory is treating the
pattern as if it were the answer.

## Markers used in this answer

(**mechanism**) — follows from the biology and is checkable by reasoning. (**definitional**) — a
term's meaning. (**consensus**) — standard across current textbooks and national guidance.
(**country-dependent**) — differs between countries or institutions, and yours is the authority.

## What a granuloma is, definitionally

An organised collection of macrophages, frequently with multinucleate giant cells, with or without
a lymphocyte cuff and with or without central necrosis. (**definitional**) The organisation is the
point: a diffuse infiltrate of macrophages is not a granuloma, and the structure forms because the
stimulus persists.

Three things follow mechanically and they are the whole argument. (**mechanism**)

* **It is a response to persistence**, so anything persistent can cause one — an organism that
  resists killing, a particle that cannot be digested, a self-antigen, a degenerate piece of the
  host's own matrix.
* **The repertoire is narrow.** Tissue has a small number of ways to wall something off, so very
  different stimuli converge on similar architecture.
* **Therefore the architecture is weakly informative about the stimulus.** Not uninformative — the
  patterns below do carry real probabilistic weight — but nothing like sufficient.

## The patterns a pathologist can name, and what each suggests

```
   GRANULOMA ARCHITECTURE, AND WHAT IT LEANS TOWARD
   ==================================================================================

   SARCOIDAL ("naked")
     tight, well-formed, scanty lymphocyte cuff, little necrosis
     -> sarcoidosis; also FOREIGN BODY reactions, including tattoo
        pigment, and some infections. (**consensus**)

   TUBERCULOID
     granulomas with CASEATING central necrosis, lymphocyte cuff
     -> mycobacterial infection high on the list; NOT a diagnosis
        of it, and the absence of necrosis does not exclude it

   PALISADING
     histiocytes arranged around a centre of ALTERED MATERIAL --
     degenerate collagen, mucin, or fibrin
     -> granuloma annulare, necrobiosis lipoidica, rheumatoid
        nodule. The centre's content is the discriminator, and it
        is a histological judgement

   SUPPURATIVE / mixed with neutrophils
     granulomas with neutrophil abscesses inside them
     -> think ORGANISM first: deep fungal, atypical mycobacterial,
        some bacterial. This is the pattern where the stain and the
        culture matter most. (**consensus**)

   FOREIGN BODY
     giant cells engulfing identifiable material
     -> suture, silica, talc, keratin from a ruptured cyst or
        follicle, injected filler, tattoo pigment
   ==================================================================
   AND THE CAUSES, WHICH CUT ACROSS ALL FIVE ROWS
   ----------------------------------------------
     INFECTION      mycobacteria (tuberculous, leprosy, atypical),
                    deep and subcutaneous fungi, leishmania,
                    treponemes
     FOREIGN        exogenous material, or ENDOGENOUS material in
                    the wrong compartment -- which is why m117's
                    ruptured follicle produces a granulomatous
                    response to the host's own keratin
     IMMUNE         sarcoidosis, granulomatous rosacea, cutaneous
                    Crohn's disease, granulomatous cheilitis
     MATRIX         granuloma annulare, necrobiosis lipoidica,
                    rheumatoid nodule
     IMMUNE         granulomatous disease in common variable
     DEFICIENCY     immunodeficiency and other inborn errors
     DRUG           granulomatous drug reactions, including to some
                    immunomodulatory agents
   ==================================================================
   No row in the top block maps onto one row in the bottom block.
   That non-correspondence is the answer to the question.
   ==================================================================
```

## The negative special stain, which is the trap

This is the part most often got wrong, and it is worth stating as a general principle because it
recurs across medicine.

Histochemical stains for organisms — the acid-fast stains for mycobacteria, the silver and
periodic-acid stains for fungi — are **specific but insensitive** in tissue. (**consensus**) In
paucibacillary disease the organisms are few, they are unevenly distributed, and the section
examined is a thin slice of a small sample. A negative stain is therefore **weak evidence of
absence** and is routinely over-read as strong evidence.

This is m089's argument transplanted to a different instrument. m089's version was about sampling
site: a confident zero obtained from the wrong structure is worse than no result, because a
genuinely absent result and an unobtainable one are reported identically. Here the zero comes from
an insensitive assay rather than the wrong site, and the failure is the same shape. The report
says "no acid-fast bacilli seen", which is exactly what it means, and it gets read as "not
mycobacterial".

So what actually interrogates cause:

| Instrument | What it can establish | Why it is often not done |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tissue culture, including mycobacterial and fungal culture** | Identification, and susceptibility | It needs **fresh tissue**, not the formalin pot, so it must be decided on **before** the biopsy is taken. This is the single commonest reversible failure in this territory |
| **Molecular testing on tissue** | Identification where culture is slow or fails | Availability and which targets are offered are (**country-dependent**) |
| **Polarised light on the existing section** | Foreign material that is birefringent — silica, talc, suture, some fillers | It requires someone to ask for it. The information is in the block already |
| **Serology, interferon-gamma release or tuberculin testing** | Exposure, not tissue diagnosis | Interpretation differs by population and by immune status |
| **Clinical and systems review** | The cause, in a large proportion of cases | Because it is not a laboratory test, it is left out of the list of "investigations" |

The polarised-light row is worth a sentence. Cross-polarisation is the same physics m020 sets out
for dermoscopy: changing the illumination reveals structure that was present and unseen. On a
histological section it costs nothing and is not routine, so the material sits in the block,
unrecorded, until someone asks a question that calls for it.

## Why sarcoidosis is the hardest row

Cutaneous sarcoidosis is diagnosed by a conjunction, not by a finding. (**consensus**,
**definitional**) It needs a compatible clinical picture, non-caseating granulomas on histology,
and **exclusion of the other causes of the same pattern** — infection and foreign material in
particular. There is no positive test.

Two consequences follow, and both are practical.

First, a report of non-caseating sarcoidal granulomas is **not** a diagnosis of sarcoidosis,
however often it is read as one. Second, because the diagnosis is partly one of exclusion, it is
unstable in exactly the way exclusion diagnoses are: a granulomatous reaction that was called
sarcoidosis and does not behave as expected should prompt a return to the infective and
foreign-body possibilities rather than an escalation of immunosuppression. (**mechanism**)

Cutaneous sarcoidosis also carries the requirement to look elsewhere, because skin involvement may
be the presenting feature of systemic disease, and the sites that need specific assessment — the
eyes, the lungs, the heart and the calcium handling — are in the guidance named below rather than
left to the dermatology consultation alone. (**consensus**) Lupus pernio is the variant
conventionally flagged for its association with more extensive systemic disease. (**consensus**)

## Why the direction of treatment makes this consequential

If the cause were only of academic interest, a pattern report would be enough. It is not, because
the two main branches need interventions with **opposite sign**. (**mechanism**)

* An infective granuloma treated with immunosuppression gets worse, and in mycobacterial and deep
  fungal disease that worsening can be substantial. This is the reason screening for latent
  tuberculosis precedes immunosuppressive and biologic therapy as a matter of routine.
  (**consensus**)
* An immune-mediated granuloma treated with antimicrobials does not respond, and the non-response
  consumes months.

One corollary is worth stating because it is often done without being named: a trial of
immunosuppression in a granulomatous disease of unknown cause is an **experiment**, not a
treatment, and it is an experiment whose downside is borne entirely by the patient if the cause
turns out to be infective. (**mechanism**) That is the argument for completing the identification
step before the therapeutic one wherever the clinical situation permits, and for being explicit
when it does not.

m055 has the device for an intervention whose sign reverses on the target rather than merely
weakening — one act, two opposite effects, invisible from the outside. Granulomatous disease is
the clearest dermatological instance of it, and it is the whole reason the pattern report is a
question and not an answer.

Treatment at the level of principle only; no agent, dose, strength or regimen appears in this
answer. Granuloma annulare is frequently self-limiting and the balance between treating and
waiting is a real one. Necrobiosis lipoidica has an association with diabetes that makes
assessment of glucose handling part of the work-up, and the lesions are at risk of ulceration.
Cutaneous sarcoidosis is treated according to extent, site and systemic involvement, with the
disfiguring facial forms treated more aggressively than their physical extent alone would suggest.
Infective causes are treated according to organism and susceptibility, which is why the
identification is worth the extra pot.

## The human stakes, said plainly

No analogy in this section.

Granulomatous conditions are slow. The diagnostic sequence described above routinely takes months,
often with an interim label that later changes, and the experience of being investigated for a
long time without an answer has its own weight — particularly when the shortlist contains both an
infection and an immune disease, because the two possibilities carry different implications and
someone waiting is holding both.

Several of these conditions are visibly disfiguring on the face. Lupus pernio and the plaque forms
of cutaneous sarcoidosis can be prominent and persistent, necrobiosis lipoidica on the shins is
visible and liable to ulcerate, and facial granulomatous disease is not concealable. The extent of
the skin affected is a poor guide to what it costs someone, and a treatment decision that weighs
only physical extent will systematically under-treat the disfiguring forms.

Two things are worth saying out loud to someone in this position. That a pattern report is
genuinely informative even though it is not a diagnosis, and why — because "we do not know yet"
and "the test was useless" are different statements and get confused. And that a change of label
later is the process working rather than an earlier error, because an exclusion diagnosis is
provisional by construction.

Nothing here is for any reader's own use, and no treatment named or implied is a recommendation.
Granulomatous skin disease needs specialist assessment, and the specific situations that need it
urgently are: any possibility of mycobacterial or deep fungal infection before immunosuppression
is started; eye symptoms with suspected sarcoidosis; a non-healing or ulcerating lesion; and any
granulomatous diagnosis in someone whose immune system is suppressed, where the infective
possibilities widen considerably.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The central argument is settled and is the part worth memorising: a granuloma is a response
pattern, the repertoire is narrow, and the architecture under-determines the cause. The
insensitivity of tissue stains for organisms is settled. The requirement that sarcoidosis be a
conjunction including exclusion of infection is settled.

What moves is the identification step. Molecular detection of mycobacteria and fungi directly from
tissue, including from formalin-fixed material in some laboratories, has improved and has
partially relaxed the requirement that fresh tissue be sent — but only partially, and only where
that service exists, so it is firmly (**country-dependent**) and sending fresh tissue remains the
safe default. Metagenomic sequencing of tissue is moving from research into selected diagnostic
use and its place is not settled. On the treatment side, the recognition of granulomatous
reactions to newer immunomodulatory agents has grown, which adds a drug column to a differential
that used not to have one prominently. Sarcoidosis treatment pathways and the role of
steroid-sparing and biologic agents in cutaneous disease continue to be revised, and funding
differs by country. Current as of October 2026.
