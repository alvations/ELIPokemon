# Dose is irradiance multiplied by time, and the number on the bottle is a laboratory ratio

The experiment that settles the first half of this question is available on almost every patient.
Compare the skin of the forearm with the skin of the upper inner arm, or the face with the
buttock, in the same person of the same age. The differences — coarse deep wrinkling, yellowish
thickening, scattered brown macules, visible fine vessels, mottled pigmentation, rough scaly
patches — track **exposure**, not age. [mechanism] [consensus] Chronological ageing happens to all
the skin at once. Photoageing happens where the light landed, in proportion to how much landed
there.

The second half is a measurement problem. The number printed on a sunscreen is the result of a
standardised laboratory test, performed with the product applied at a density considerably heavier
than anyone uses, against an endpoint that is mostly sensitive to one part of the spectrum. It is
an honest number about a defined experiment. It is not a prediction of what a person gets at the
beach. [consensus]

## Markers used in this answer

*mechanism* — follows from physics and biology and is checkable by reasoning. *definitional* — a
term's meaning. *consensus* — standard across current textbooks and national guidance.
*country-dependent* — differs between countries or institutions, and yours is the authority.

## Why the word that matters is dose

Dose is irradiance multiplied by exposure time. [mechanism] Irradiance is set by the sun's angle —
so by latitude, season and time of day — and modified by altitude, by cloud, and by reflection off
snow, water, sand and concrete. Time is set by behaviour. Every intervention in photoprotection
acts on one of those two factors, which is why they are not interchangeable.

The dose-dependence is not a statistical association bolted on afterwards. The histology of
photoaged skin is specific: loss and disorganisation of dermal collagen with accumulation of
abnormal elastotic material, which is why the clinical signs are those of a dermis that has lost
its mechanical scaffolding rather than those of a thinned epidermis. [mechanism] [consensus]

Two patterns of exposure are worth distinguishing because they do not carry the same consequences.
**Chronic cumulative exposure** — the outdoor worker's lifetime — is most strongly associated with
keratinocyte cancers and with actinic keratoses as a field change. **Intermittent intense exposure
with sunburn**, particularly in childhood, features more prominently in the epidemiology of
melanoma. [consensus] Both are dose; they are different distributions of the same dose over time.
The recognition and referral reasoning for the cancers themselves sits in the oncology answers
m061 to m065 and in this specialty's dermoscopy answer m020, and none of it is re-derived here.

## What the sun protection factor is, exactly

It is a **ratio**: the ultraviolet dose needed to produce a defined erythemal response on skin
with the product applied, divided by the dose needed to produce the same response without it.
[definitional] Four consequences follow directly from that definition and each of them is
examinable.

**It is calibrated on erythema.** Erythema is predominantly a shorter-wavelength endpoint, so the
factor is substantially a statement about that waveband and under-reports longer-wavelength
protection. That is why a separate measure of longer-wavelength protection exists at all — and the
systems for grading it, and the labels used, differ by country, with different marks and different
test methods in different regulatory regions. [country-dependent] A product with a high factor and
no longer-wavelength claim is not a broad-spectrum product.

**It is measured at a standardised application density, heavier than real use.** [consensus] Under
the thinner, patchier layer people actually apply, the protection achieved is lower than the label
figure, and the deficit is not small. This is the single commonest reason a product appears to
have failed.

**The relationship between the factor and the fraction of ultraviolet transmitted is reciprocal,
so the curve flattens.** [mechanism] Moving from a low factor to a middle one removes a large
share of the transmitted dose; moving from a middle one to the highest available removes a much
smaller additional share. The high numbers are not a confidence trick, but the marginal gain is
small, and it is routinely swamped by application thickness, missed sites and failure to reapply.

**It says nothing about how long the product stays where it was put.** Water, sweat, towel-drying,
rubbing and simple time all remove it, which is why reapplication exists as a concept and why
water-resistance claims are tested separately. [consensus]

## The groups for whom the arithmetic changes

Several situations raise either the dose or the consequence of a given dose, and they are where
this reasoning stops being general advice. **Photosensitising medicines** — several antibiotic
classes, diuretics, retinoids, amiodarone, some antifungals and many others — lower the dose at
which a reaction occurs, and the eruption is a drug eruption, which is m054's territory.
[consensus] **The photodermatoses** are disorders where ultraviolet is the trigger rather than a
risk factor, and their management is specialist and often involves rigorous avoidance.
**Immunosuppression**, especially after solid organ transplantation, substantially changes the
risk of keratinocyte cancers for a given exposure history, and these patients are usually in a
structured surveillance pathway. [consensus] **A previous skin cancer** changes subsequent
management. **Children** matter disproportionately because the cumulative total is a lifetime
total and because intermittent intense exposure in childhood is prominent in the melanoma
epidemiology. [consensus] **Genetic photosensitivity disorders** such as the DNA-repair
deficiencies are rare and change everything about management.

And the trade-off that is genuinely unsettled: ultraviolet exposure contributes to cutaneous
vitamin D synthesis, so complete avoidance has a cost, and the balance struck between
photoprotection advice and vitamin D sufficiency — including whether supplementation is
recommended and to whom — is **country-dependent** and differs by latitude, by population and by
national policy. Picking one country's answer and asserting it would be wrong.

## What can be done afterwards, stated honestly

Photoageing is partially treatable and not reversible. Topical retinoids have the best-established
evidence for improving the clinical and histological features of photodamaged skin; field
treatments for actinic keratoses reduce the lesion burden on a damaged field rather than removing
the damage; and the device-based treatments improve specific features. [consensus] None of that
restores the dermis to its unexposed state, and the comparison that proves it is the same one this
answer opened with: the upper inner arm is still the control, and nothing applied to the forearm
makes the forearm into it.

## The human stakes, said plainly

The people with the highest lifetime ultraviolet doses are mostly people who were working.
Construction, agriculture, fishing, landscaping, roofing, delivery, outdoor sport and military
service all involve exposure that is not a lifestyle choice, and in many countries occupational
ultraviolet exposure is poorly regulated compared with other occupational carcinogens. Advice
built around not going out at midday is advice for people whose employer decides when they go out.
Where a national occupational health framework does address it, that framework is the authority
and it differs considerably between countries.

Photoprotection advice also lands unevenly for another reason. The visible signs of photodamage,
the available product formulations, the cosmetic acceptability of mineral filters on different
skin tones, and the assumption that darker skin needs no advice at all are all areas where the
standard teaching is calibrated on a narrow range of skin — m018 makes that argument in full.
Higher constitutive melanin reduces but does not abolish ultraviolet-related harm, and
photoprotection remains relevant in all skin tones, with the pigmentary consequences of
ultraviolet and visible light being a prominent concern in skin that tans readily. [consensus]

Two things that should be said without euphemism. A tan is a response to DNA damage, not a sign of
health, and there is no such thing as a safe tan from ultraviolet exposure. [consensus] And
sunbeds deliver ultraviolet deliberately; their use is restricted or banned for minors in many
countries and the restrictions are **country-dependent**.

Without softening: nothing here is for any reader's own use, no product or factor named here is a
recommendation, and this answer cannot be used to judge any particular person's risk. Anyone with
a new, changing, bleeding or non-healing lesion on sun-exposed skin needs to be assessed in person
rather than reassured by a general argument about dose, and anyone with a severe reaction to sun
exposure, or a reaction after starting a new medicine, needs medical assessment.

## Scope and safety

Revision material for someone already training in or qualified for the field. Not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and not reviewed by a clinician. It is not for use in making a
decision about anyone's care, including your own.

**No product, factor, application amount, exposure time or supplementation advice named here is an
instruction, and none of it addresses any reader's own treatment or behaviour.** No numerical
factor value appears in this answer on purpose: the labelling systems differ by region and the
authority is the regulator and the public health body where you practise.

Anyone with a new, changing, bleeding, crusting or non-healing lesion on sun-exposed skin needs to
be assessed in person. A severe reaction to sun exposure, or any reaction to sunlight that begins
after starting a new medicine, needs medical assessment.

Local guidance and the policy where you practise are the authority on all of this, and they differ
by country and by institution.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physics and the histology are settled and are the part worth memorising. What moves is
regulation and product science. Filter approvals differ by region and several filters licensed in
one jurisdiction are not available in another, which is a live and long-standing difference rather
than a transitional one. Labelling of longer-wavelength protection has been through more than one
revision and still differs by region. The environmental impact of specific filters on reef
ecosystems has driven local bans in some jurisdictions, and systemic absorption of organic filters
has been the subject of regulatory review in more than one region, and what those regulators
actually concluded is readable only in their own published statements rather than here. The
vitamin D balance remains genuinely contested at the level of national advice. Occupational
ultraviolet exposure has been gaining recognition as an occupational carcinogen in several
countries, and that is the area most likely to look different in five years. Current as of October
2026.
