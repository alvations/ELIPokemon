# The first word you choose closes most of the list

Dermatology is a descriptive discipline before it is a diagnostic one. The diagnosis is reached by
naming what is in front of you in a fixed vocabulary, and the first term in that vocabulary — the
**primary morphology** — is the one that partitions the differential. Every other feature,
distribution included, refines a partition you have already committed to. Choose *vesicle* when
the thing is a *pustule* and you have not slowed yourself down; you have moved to a different
list. This is why experienced dermatologists appear to diagnose quickly. They are not
pattern-matching photographs. They have made one hard decision early and are now working inside a
much smaller space.

## The vocabulary, which is definitional rather than contested

These terms are stable across standard texts, and the basis of each is **definitional** — they are
conventions of language, not findings from a study. Three axes do nearly all the work: whether the
surface contour is changed, whether what is raising it is solid or fluid, and whether the surface
has been lost.

```
   THE ONE DECISION THAT PARTITIONS EVERYTHING ELSE

   is the surface contour changed?
   |
   +-- NO, the skin is flat -----------+-- < 1 cm --------------------> MACULE
   |                                   +-- >= 1 cm -------------------> PATCH
   |
   +-- YES, and it is solid -----------+-- < 1 cm --------------------> PAPULE
   |                                   +-- >= 1 cm, flat-topped,
   |                                   |   broader than it is deep ---> PLAQUE
   |                                   +-- >= 1 cm, deeper than
   |                                       it is wide ---------------> NODULE
   |
   +-- YES, and it holds fluid --------+-- clear,     < 1 cm ---------> VESICLE
   |                                   +-- clear,    >= 1 cm ---------> BULLA
   |                                   +-- purulent ------------------> PUSTULE
   |
   +-- YES, and it is transient -----------------------------------> WHEAL
   |   (dermal oedema, gone in hours)
   |
   +-- the surface has been LOST ------+-- epidermis only ----------> EROSION
                                       |                              (heals without a scar)
                                       +-- into dermis or deeper ---> ULCER
                                                                      (heals with a scar)
```

The 1 cm cut-off is *text-dependent*. Many standard atlases use it to separate macule from patch
and papule from plaque; others draw the line at 5 mm. Nothing clinical turns on which, provided
the size is recorded in millimetres as well as named, which removes the ambiguity entirely.

## Why the morphology is really a statement about depth

The reason primary morphology outperforms every other single descriptor is (**mechanism**): the
word you choose is a claim about which layer of the skin the process is in, and therefore about
what kind of process it can be.

```
   WHAT THE WORD COMMITS YOU TO

   LAYER        MORPHOLOGY             WHAT MUST BE HAPPENING THERE
   ---------    -------------------    ---------------------------------------------
   epidermis    macule / patch         pigment or vessel change, contour intact
                vesicle / bulla        a split: within the epidermis, or at the
                                       junction with the dermis below it
                pustule                neutrophils collected in or under it --
                                       which is not the same as infection
   ---------    -------------------    ---------------------------------------------
   dermis       plaque                 epidermal thickening plus an upper dermal
                                       infiltrate, spreading sideways
                nodule                 a mass with most of its bulk below the
                                       surface, so it is felt more than seen
                wheal                  transient dermal oedema, no epidermal change
   ---------    -------------------    ---------------------------------------------
   loss         erosion                floor is still epidermis  -> no scar
                ulcer                  floor is dermis or deeper -> scar
```

A blistering process and a papulosquamous process are not neighbours on any list. They are
different anatomical compartments. That is what the vocabulary is encoding.

## Secondary morphology is a record of time, not of diagnosis

*Scale, crust, excoriation, lichenification, fissuring, atrophy, scarring* are (**definitional**)
terms too, but they describe what has happened to a lesion since it appeared — the disease plus
scratching plus treatment plus weeks. A plaque that has been rubbed for two months is lichenified,
and the lichenification tells you about the itch, not the cause. Leading with a secondary feature
is the commonest way a description goes wrong, because secondary change is convergent: several
unrelated processes produce the same crust.

## The description that is worth writing down

A useful record is ordered so that the morphology decision is forced first, and so that the next
reader can re-reason from it rather than inherit a conclusion. By (**consensus**) among standard
texts the components are: number; size in millimetres; the body sites involved; the configuration
the lesions make; the **primary morphology**; secondary change; colour; surface; border
definition; and what palpation adds. Two practical points follow.

**Palpation is part of the description, not an addition to it.** A patch and a plaque cannot be
separated by eye, and a nodule is defined by having bulk you can feel. Induration, warmth,
tenderness, mobility, depth and whether a red lesion blanches under pressure are all description.
A photograph cannot supply any of them, which is the structural reason remote assessment of a
lesion is harder than it looks.

**Do not smuggle the diagnosis into the description.** Writing *eczematous* or *psoriasiform*
records your conclusion and destroys the evidence, because nobody downstream can now disagree with
you. Write the plaque, the scale and the sites, and let the conclusion be separable from them.

## The feature that varies most is not the feature that identifies

Within one diagnosis, the features that vary most between individuals are usually the ones that
catch the eye first: the precise colour, the exact outline of any single lesion, how many there
are, how angry the surface looks today. The primary morphology varies least of all.

That is not a coincidence. The morphology is a statement about the anatomical compartment, and the
compartment is a property of the disease process. Colour, extent and surface change are properties
of *this* person, *this* skin tone, *this* week, and this much scratching. (**mechanism**) A
description that leads with the most variable feature has led with the least informative one — and
it has led with the feature most likely to differ between the photograph in the textbook and the
patient in front of you, which is the subject of the sibling answer in this set on assessing skin
of colour.

## The human stakes, said plainly

A description is written about a person who has usually been asked to undress in a cold room in
front of a stranger, and who may have been self-conscious about that skin for years. Thoroughness
and kindness are not competing here: say what you are looking at and why, expose one region at a
time, offer a chaperone, and accept a refusal. A rushed examination that skipped the scalp because
asking felt awkward is not a kinder examination. It is a worse one.

The record has a human consequence too. The next person to read it may be deciding whether this
patient is seen today or in six weeks. A description that wrote down a conclusion instead of the
findings has quietly taken that decision away from them.

And this needs saying without softening: nothing in this vocabulary allows anyone to settle a
worry about their own skin. Recognising your own lesion in a definition is not an assessment and
is not reassurance. A new, changing, bleeding or non-healing lesion needs to be looked at by a
person who is trained to look at it.

## What an examiner digs into next

Why a pustule is not evidence of infection. The difference between a plaque and a patch and how
you would establish it on a real arm. What the presence of a wheal rules out by itself, given that
it is gone within a day. And how you would describe a lesion in a record where the next reader may
be deciding whether the patient needs to be seen urgently.

## Scope and safety

Revision material for someone already training in or qualified for the field. Not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and not reviewed by a clinician. It is not for use in making a
decision about anyone's care, including your own.

This answer explains the vocabulary used to describe lesions. It deliberately does not pair any
morphology with a diagnosis, and it cannot be used to work out what a lesion on a real person is.
**Nothing here can be used to reassure anyone about their own mole, patch or lump.** The reason
urgent assessment pathways for suspected skin cancer exist is that the relevant features are not
reliably self-assessable, and a description read off a page is not an assessment. Anyone with a
new, changing, bleeding or non-healing skin lesion needs to be seen in person.

Local guidance is the authority on everything procedural here, and it differs by country and by
institution.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The primary-morphology vocabulary has been stable for decades and there is no live dispute about
it, which is why it is the safest ground in the specialty. The 1 cm versus 0.5 cm cut-off varies
between textbooks and will continue to. Recording the measurement in millimetres alongside the
term makes the variation irrelevant, and that is the practice to carry forward from October 2026.
