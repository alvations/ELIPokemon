# Two of the three variables are in the record, and the third is the one that fails

A topical treatment has three largely independent inputs: the potency of the molecule, the vehicle
carrying it, and the quantity that actually reaches the skin over the course. The first two are
written on the prescription and are therefore auditable. The third is not recorded anywhere, is
rarely specified, and is the one that is usually wrong. That asymmetry is the whole answer.

## Potency, and why the number is useless without its system

Topical corticosteroid potency is classified, and the classification is (**country-dependent**) —
a point worth leading with because it is the commonest source of confusion when reading material
from elsewhere. In the United Kingdom the formulary groups them into four: mild, moderate, potent
and very potent. In the United States a seven-class system is used, numbered I to VII, with class
I the strongest — the opposite direction of travel from how most people assume a numbered scale
runs. Other countries use further variants. The same product can sit in differently named groups
in two systems, so a potency claim quoted without the system it came from cannot be acted on.
**The formulary where you practise is the authority**, and that is true of every figure in this
answer.

## Vehicle, which changes both the pharmacology and the adherence

The vehicle is not packaging. For the same molecule at the same strength, an ointment is generally
more potent than a cream, because it is occlusive, holds water in the stratum corneum and improves
penetration. (**mechanism**) Occlusion of any kind — an ointment, a dressing, a nappy, a skin fold
pressed against itself — increases absorption, which is why the same preparation behaves
differently in an axilla than on a shin.

By (**consensus**), the working division is: ointments for dry, thickened or fissured skin and
poor on weeping or hair-bearing sites; creams better tolerated cosmetically and usable on moist
skin, at the cost of preservatives that can themselves sensitise; lotions, gels, foams and
solutions for hair-bearing areas and large surfaces, where anything greasy will simply not be
used.

That last clause is the point. A vehicle the patient will not tolerate delivers nothing, and
adherence is not a separate topic from dose — it is part of the dose. Choosing a less potent
molecule in a vehicle somebody will actually use can deliver more drug than a stronger one in a
vehicle they abandon.

## Site, because the skin is not one organ

Percutaneous absorption varies by site over a range that surprises people: thin skin — eyelids,
flexures, genital skin, the face — absorbs far more of the same preparation than palms and soles
do. (**mechanism**) This is why potency is matched to the **site** and not only to the severity,
and why the same eruption may warrant different preparations on the face and on the shins in one
person on one day.

## Quantity, and the unit that exists because of it

The **fingertip unit** is the ribbon of ointment or cream squeezed from a nozzle of about 5 mm
across, laid along the terminal phalanx of an adult index finger. One FTU is approximately 0.5 g,
and covers roughly the area of two adult palms with the fingers included.

National formularies publish quantity tables for exactly this reason — suitable amounts to
prescribe per body area for a week of twice-daily use — and the figures run to around 100 g for a
trunk or for both legs rather than the 30 g tube that habit reaches for. Look the table up rather
than reconstructing it from the FTU arithmetic, because the formulary figures are the ones your
pharmacy and your audit will use.

## Why the quantity is the variable that goes wrong

Several causes stack, and none of them is carelessness. Topical corticosteroid phobia is
widespread, held by patients and by prescribers, and it produces deliberate under-use on both
sides. The instruction to use the preparation sparingly, still in circulation, is an instruction
to under-treat. Dispensed tube sizes are chosen by habit rather than by area. And most
importantly, almost nobody is given a unit at all: a quantity instruction with no unit in it is
not an instruction.

## Why it presents as treatment failure

If the amount delivered per unit area is below the threshold needed to outpace the inflammatory
process, the clinical course looks exactly like no response. (**mechanism**) The usual conclusion
is that the preparation does not work, and the usual next step is to escalate potency or change
molecule — both of which add risk while leaving the variable that was actually wrong untouched.
The cheaper step first is to establish how much was used, over what area, for how long, and by
weighing or asking after the tube. A patient who has used 15 g of a 30 g tube in a fortnight over
a widespread eruption has not had a trial of that treatment.

## The other side, because this is not an argument for more

Over-use has real consequences and they are not theoretical: skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia,
easy bruising, perioral dermatitis and tachyphylaxis locally, and with very potent preparations
over large areas, under occlusion, or in infants, enough systemic absorption to suppress the
hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. (**consensus**) The argument of this answer is not that more
is better. It is that the quantity should be **specified** rather than guessed, in both
directions, because a number that nobody states is a number nobody can get right.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Applying a topical preparation to most of the body twice a day is a substantial daily burden, and
it is routinely prescribed as though it were nothing. It takes real time. It ruins clothes and
bedding. Children resist it, and the adult doing it is often tired and alone. Many people cannot
reach their own back or their own feet.

Topical corticosteroid phobia is not irrationality to be corrected with a firmer tone. It comes
from real adverse effects, from contradictory advice given by different clinicians, and from years
of being told to use the preparation sparingly. Someone who has been frightened of a treatment is
not helped by being told the fear is unfounded; they are helped by being told what the amount is,
what it is for, and what would actually constitute too much.

The cost of mislabelling under-application as treatment failure is the part that lasts. A person
who is told that a treatment did not work for them walks away believing their skin cannot be
treated, and that belief outlives the consultation by years. Establishing how much was used costs
a minute and prevents it.

And without softening: no quantity, potency class or preparation named in this answer is for any
reader's own use. Anyone worried about a treatment they have been given needs to speak to the
prescriber or pharmacist who issued it.

## Scope and safety

Revision material for someone already training in or qualified for the field. Not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and not reviewed by a clinician. It is not for use in making a
decision about anyone's care, including your own.

**No quantity, potency class or preparation named here is a prescribing instruction and none of it
addresses any reader's own treatment.** The figures appear so that the reasoning behind them is
visible — why a unit exists at all, why the amount is the variable that fails — and not so that
they can be applied to a person. An answer here agreeing with your memory is not confirmation.
Check the formulary before anything reaches a patient, and note that the paediatric quantities,
which differ by age, have deliberately not been reproduced.

Anyone whose skin condition is not improving, or who is worried about a treatment they have been
given, needs to discuss it with the prescriber or pharmacist who issued it rather than with a
revision page.

Local guidance and the formulary where you practise are the authority on all of this, and they
differ by country and by institution.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The fingertip unit and the per-area counts have been stable for a long time and are reproduced
consistently across formularies, which is why they are usable. The potency classifications remain
genuinely divergent between countries and there is no sign of convergence, so the discipline of
naming the system alongside the class is permanent rather than temporary. What has moved recently
is the attention given to topical corticosteroid withdrawal and to steroid phobia as a named
barrier to treatment, and that discussion is live rather than settled as of October 2026.
