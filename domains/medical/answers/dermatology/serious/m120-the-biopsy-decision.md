# A pathologist reports what is in the block, so the decisions before the cut set the ceiling

A biopsy feels like asking a question. It is better understood as **building the instrument that
will answer it**, out of a few irreversible choices — which lesion, which part of it, by what
technique, to what depth, in what medium, with what written alongside — and then handing the
instrument to someone who cannot see the patient.

Everything downstream is bounded by those choices. A pathologist examining a shallow punch from
the burnt-out centre of a plaque, in formalin, with "rash?" on the form, will produce a careful
and accurate report that cannot contain the diagnosis. Nothing later recovers it. The repeat costs
weeks.

m089 makes the specific version of this argument for the immunobullous diseases, where the
immunofluorescence sample has to come from perilesional skin and not from the blister. That rule
is settled, it is still routinely got wrong, and it is **not re-derived here**. This answer is the
general case: the five decisions, what each one determines, and the things a biopsy cannot settle
however well it is taken.

## Markers used in this answer

(**mechanism**) — follows from the method and is checkable by reasoning. (**definitional**) — a
term's meaning. (**consensus**) — standard across current textbooks and national guidance.
(**country-dependent**) — differs between countries or institutions, and yours is the authority.

Two applications are worth naming because they are where the choice is most consequential.

**Panniculitis and large-vessel vasculitis live in the fat.** A specimen that stops in the dermis
cannot classify a panniculitis, because the classification rests on whether the inflammation is
septal or lobular and on the state of the vessels in the septa. (**definitional**) This is the
single commonest depth failure, and it is entirely a decision made before the cut. An incisional
biopsy taken deliberately deep, or a generous punch pushed to fat, is what the question requires.
(**consensus**)

**Where melanoma is a possibility, excision of the whole lesion is the standard where
practicable**, because the staging of melanoma rests on depth of invasion measured through the
thickest part, and a partial sample risks under-staging by missing it. (**consensus**) No
measurement, threshold or stage boundary appears in this answer; those are in the guidance named
below. Who performs the procedure, with what margin, and in which setting, is firmly
(**country-dependent**).

## Decision two: site, which is usually about activity rather than severity

The instinct is to sample the most impressive part of a lesion. That is frequently wrong, and the
reason is mechanical. (**mechanism**)

* **For an inflammatory dermatosis, sample the active edge**, not the centre. The centre of an
  established plaque or an annular lesion is frequently the oldest part, where the diagnostic
  infiltrate has resolved and secondary change has replaced it. m016's point that secondary
  morphology is a record of time applies directly: a sample from the centre is a sample of the
  past.
* **Avoid an ulcerated or necrotic centre** for anything other than a question about the ulcer
  itself. Necrotic tissue reports as necrosis.
* **For vasculitis, sample a fresh lesion.** The findings evolve over days and the characteristic
  changes are present in a defined early window, which is in the guidance rather than reproducible
  here. (**consensus**)
* **For hair loss, sample the active margin** of the affected area, and send it in a way that
  permits the sectioning the question needs — see decision four.
* **For suspected infection, the site matters less than the medium**: fresh tissue to microbiology
  as well as a fixed sample for histology, which is m118's argument and the commonest reversible
  failure in that territory.
* **For suspected immunobullous disease**, perilesional normal-appearing skin for
  immunofluorescence, plus the edge of a fresh blister for histology. m089, and not repeated here.

The general form is: **sample where the process is happening, not where it has happened.**

## Decision three: depth, handling and medium, which cannot be changed later

Formalin fixes tissue and makes most things possible and a few things impossible. Direct
immunofluorescence needs fresh tissue or a specific transport medium. Culture needs fresh tissue.
Some molecular tests work on fixed material and some do not, and which is which is a property of
your laboratory rather than of the technique. (**country-dependent**)

The consequence is a timing rule that is easy to state and easy to forget: **the decision about
medium is made at the moment of the request, and the specimen is in the pot within seconds of the
cut.** There is no recovery from putting the only sample in formalin when culture was the
question. (**mechanism**)

There is also a **treatment-timing** rule with the same shape. Treatment that suppresses the
process changes the histology — a topical or systemic corticosteroid can render an inflammatory
infiltrate non-diagnostic, and treatment of a suspected infection can render it unculturable.
Where the histology is going to matter, the biopsy is taken before the treatment that will alter
it, and where it has not been, the report must be read knowing that. (**mechanism**,
**consensus**) This is m086's argument about suppressing the readout, applied to a specimen rather
than to a clinical sign.

## Decision four: orientation, which can turn one specimen into two different answers

The same cylinder of skin, cut in two different planes, answers two different questions, and the
plane is chosen in the laboratory on the basis of what the request asked for.

**Vertical (conventional) sections** show the full thickness at one place: the epidermis, the
dermo-epidermal junction, the depth of an infiltrate, the level of a split.

**Transverse (horizontal) sections** cut across the specimen and show **many follicles at one
level at once**, which is what permits follicle counting, the ratio of terminal to miniaturised
hairs, and the ratio of anagen to telogen. That is the sectioning plane that answers a hair-loss
question, and it is not the default. (**consensus**) Asking for it is the difference between a
report that says "non-scarring alopecia" and one that quantifies the pattern — and m087's argument
about what the hair cycle records depends on having the plane that can read it.

Equivalently, for a neoplasm, **orientation and margin marking** are what permit a statement about
which margin is involved, and that information exists only if someone created it at the time.
(**mechanism**)

## Decision five: the request form is part of the specimen

A pathologist is performing **clinicopathological correlation**, which is a method and not a
courtesy. (**definitional**) The histological findings in a great many dermatoses are compatible
with several diagnoses, and the clinical information is what collapses the list. (**consensus**)

What changes the report: age, site, duration, distribution, morphology in the clinical sense,
current and recent medicines, immune status, relevant travel and occupational exposure, what has
already been treated and with what, and — most usefully — **the differential being entertained and
what each answer would change.** A form that says "exclude lymphoma" and a form that says "rash?"
produce different work on the same block, because the first tells the pathologist which stains and
which level of scrutiny the question deserves.

## What a biopsy cannot settle

Four things, and the first is the one that causes most harm.

**A non-specific report is not an exclusion.** "Non-specific dermatitis" and "no diagnostic
features seen" are statements about this sample. They cannot distinguish *the features are not
present in this person* from *the features are not present in this block*, and those are
completely different clinical situations reported with the same words. This is the
pertinent-negative problem m073 sets out for the clinical record, arriving in the laboratory: a
result that reads the same for "absent" and for "not sampled" cannot carry a negative.
(**mechanism**)

**Sampling error in a heterogeneous lesion.** A single sample of a lesion that varies across its
extent is a sample of one part of it. In neoplasia that is the whole argument of m097; in
inflammatory disease it is why multiple sites are taken in erythroderma, where the yield is better
from several and repeating later in the course is a recognised strategy rather than a failure.
(**consensus**) m119 is that territory.

**It cannot make a diagnosis that is clinical by definition.** Hidradenitis suppurativa is
diagnosed on lesions, sites and chronicity, and a biopsy neither confirms nor excludes it — m117.
Sarcoidosis requires a conjunction including exclusion of other causes, so the histology is one of
three elements — m118.

**And it cannot answer a question nobody formulated.** A biopsy taken because the diagnosis is
uncertain, with no prior statement of what each possible answer would change, usually changes
nothing. m086 makes this point about the skin scraping and it generalises: the result is worth
having when it is tied in advance to a decision.

## The test has its own harms, and they are paid whether or not it works

A biopsy is a wound, deliberately made, and the cost is real. (**mechanism**)

Scar, which is permanent and which at some sites — the upper trunk, the shoulders, the earlobes,
the jawline — carries a recognised risk of keloid or hypertrophic scarring that is higher in some
skin types and should be discussed before rather than after. Bleeding, which needs the medicines
history. Infection. Pain, both at the time and afterwards. Delayed or poor healing, which is the
specific problem on the lower leg, in someone with arterial disease, in diabetes, and on
irradiated or previously operated skin — the vascular assessment reasoning is m055's and it
applies before cutting as well as before compressing.

Those risks also do not compose into a single "fit for a biopsy" judgement, and treating them as
one is a recognisable error. (**mechanism**) The keloid risk is a property of the **site and the
skin type**; the bleeding risk is a property of the **medicines and the clotting**; the healing
risk is a property of the **blood supply**. Someone can be at low risk on two of the three and
high on the first, and the mitigation for each is different — choosing another site, changing
nothing but the timing, or not cutting the lower leg at all. Each is assessed on its own.

The structural point is that **these costs are paid for a non-diagnostic biopsy exactly as they
are paid for a diagnostic one.** The harm attaches to the act, not to the result. That is the
argument m104 makes for imaging, and it is the reason the five decisions above are worth making
deliberately: getting them right is not refinement, it is the difference between paying the cost
for something and paying it for nothing.

## The human stakes, said plainly

No analogy in this section.

Being biopsied is a more significant event than the people arranging it tend to register. It
involves being cut, usually while awake, often in a part of the body someone would rather not
expose, by someone they have just met, for a reason they may understand only partly. The commonest
thing said afterwards is that nobody explained what was being looked for — and where the question
includes cancer, the gap between the procedure and the result is a period of real fear that is
substantially modifiable by what was said at the time and by whether someone gave a realistic
date.

Three things follow and all three are within anyone's gift. Saying what is being looked for, in
plain words, including the possibility that is worrying. Saying what the result will and will not
settle — particularly that a non-specific answer is possible and what the plan would then be,
because an unexpected "inconclusive" is experienced as a failure unless it was named as a
possibility in advance. And giving a real timeframe and a route by which the result will actually
reach them, since results going astray between services is a common and entirely avoidable harm.

The scar also deserves its own sentence, because it is the part most often skipped. It is
permanent, it is in a place that was chosen by a clinician, and for a facial or visible site the
cosmetic consequence may matter more to the person than it does to the person holding the blade.
Asking before choosing the site, where there is any choice, is a small act with a lasting
consequence.

Nothing here is for any reader's own use and no procedure described is an instruction. Anyone with
a new, changing, bleeding or non-healing skin lesion needs assessment in person, and a changing
pigmented lesion is assessed urgently.

## What an examiner digs into next

Which technique reaches which compartment, and the "cannot answer" column. Why panniculitis cannot
be classified from a shallow specimen, and what to do instead. Why a pigmented lesion is excised
whole where practicable. Why the active edge is sampled and not the centre. What has to be decided
before the specimen enters the pot. Why treatment before biopsy changes the report. Transverse
against vertical sectioning, and which question each answers. What belongs on the request form and
why it changes the work done. The difference between a non-specific report and an exclusion. Which
dermatological diagnoses are clinical by definition and therefore not biopsy questions. And the
harms of the procedure, which are paid either way.

## Scope and safety

Revision material for someone already training in or qualified for the field. Not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, not a procedural guide and not reviewed by a clinician. It is not
for use in making a decision about anyone's care, including your own, and nothing here is
instruction in how to perform a procedure.

**No measurement, margin, depth threshold, staging boundary, anaesthetic agent, dose or time
window appears in this answer, and none of it addresses any reader's own treatment.** The omission
of the melanoma staging measurements and of the vasculitis timing window is deliberate: both are
defined in the documents named above, both differ in how they are applied, and a figure quoted
here without the document would be worse than the principle stated without it.

Anyone with a new, changing, bleeding or non-healing skin lesion needs to be assessed in person. A
changing pigmented lesion is assessed urgently.

Local guidance and the policy where you practise are the authority on all of this, and they differ
by country and by institution.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The method is settled and is the part worth memorising: the specimen bounds the report, the
compartment has to be in the pot, the medium is irreversible, the sectioning plane is a choice,
and the request form is part of the instrument. The pertinent-negative problem is settled and
remains widely mishandled.

What moves is at the edges. Molecular and sequencing-based testing on tissue has expanded what a
fixed specimen can be asked, which has slightly relaxed the fresh-tissue requirement in some
laboratories and not in others, so it is firmly (**country-dependent**) and sending fresh tissue
when infection is in the differential remains the safe default. Digital pathology and whole-slide
imaging have changed how second opinions are obtained more than they have changed what a specimen
can say, and computational assessment of dermatopathology slides is an active area whose place in
routine reporting is not settled. In skin cancer, who takes the diagnostic specimen — dermatology,
surgery, or primary care under a local scheme — has shifted in several countries and is the part
most likely to differ from what you were taught. Non-invasive imaging, including reflectance
confocal microscopy and optical coherence tomography, is reducing the number of biopsies taken in
some specialist centres without replacing them, and availability is narrow. Current as of October
2026.
