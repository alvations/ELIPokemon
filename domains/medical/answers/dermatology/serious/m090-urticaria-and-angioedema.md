# A weal lasts hours while the disease lasts months, and a swelling that stays is another pathway

Two facts about urticaria do most of the diagnostic work and they appear to contradict each other.
**Each individual weal is short-lived** — it appears, migrates, and resolves within a day, leaving
skin that looks entirely normal. **The condition is not**: chronic urticaria runs for months or
years, producing a continuous supply of new weals in new places. (**definitional** **consensus**)

Hold both and two conclusions follow immediately. First, a lesion that is still there tomorrow, in
the same place, is probably not a weal, and that single observation redirects the whole
assessment. Second, hunting for what caused *today's* weal is usually the wrong question, because
the thing that needs explaining is the supply, not the individual lesion.

The deeper swelling is a separate problem that happens to look related. It sits lower in the skin,
lasts longer, is tense rather than itchy, and — critically — has two possible mechanisms, one of
which does not respond to the treatment that works for weals at all. (**consensus**)

(**mechanism**) — follows from physiology and is checkable by reasoning. (**definitional**) — a
term's meaning. (**consensus**) — standard across current textbooks and national guidance.
(**country-dependent**) — differs between countries or institutions, and yours is the authority.

## The weal: one final common path, several ways in

The weal is the visible result of mast cell activation in the dermis. Released histamine and other
mediators dilate vessels, increase permeability so that fluid enters the interstitium, and
stimulate sensory nerves — giving redness, swelling and itch respectively, which is why all three
appear together. (**mechanism**) The superficial location of the mast cells involved is what makes
it itchy and what makes it transient: a small volume of fluid in a thin compartment is reabsorbed
quickly. (**mechanism**)

Several routes reach that same activation.

**IgE-mediated.** A specific allergen cross-links IgE on the mast cell. This is the mechanism
people assume and it is largely confined to **acute** urticaria with a clear temporal relationship
to an exposure. (**consensus**)

**Direct, non-IgE activation.** Some drugs, including opioids and radiocontrast media, activate
mast cells directly. (**consensus**) Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs act by a different
route again, through altered arachidonic acid metabolism, which is why they aggravate chronic
urticaria in a substantial proportion of people who have it without being its cause.
(**consensus**)

**Autoimmune and autoallergic.** A large share of chronic spontaneous urticaria involves
autoantibodies directed at the IgE receptor or at IgE itself, which activate mast cells without
any external allergen. (**consensus**) This is the fact that reorganises the consultation.

```
   THE MISCONCEPTION THAT COSTS THE MOST TIME
   ==================================================================================
   "Chronic urticaria means we have to find the allergy."
   ==================================================================================
   In chronic spontaneous urticaria, an external allergen is usually NOT the cause,
   and extensive allergy testing in the absence of a history pointing to one has a
   poor yield and generates false leads that restrict diets for no benefit.
   (**consensus**) The recommended investigation of chronic spontaneous urticaria is
   minimal -- the history and examination do most of it, with a small number of
   basic tests, and more only where something in the history asks for it. What
   tests, and how many, is set by your national guidance. (**country-dependent**)

   AND THE DISTINCTION THAT REPLACES IT
   ==================================================================================
   AGGRAVATING FACTORS ARE NOT CAUSES. Heat, alcohol, non-steroidal
   anti-inflammatory drugs, tight clothing, stress, exercise and intercurrent
   infection all make existing urticaria worse. Removing them improves the disease
   and does not cure it, because none of them set it going. (**consensus**)
```

## Duration is the classification, and the inducible ones are provokable

Urticaria is divided by duration, conventionally at six weeks: acute below, chronic at or above.
(**definitional** **consensus**) Chronic urticaria then divides into **spontaneous** — weals arise
without an identifiable external trigger — and **inducible**, where a specific physical stimulus
reliably produces them.

The inducible group is diagnostically satisfying precisely because it is reproducible. Stroking
the skin produces linear weals in symptomatic dermographism; cold contact, sustained pressure with
a delay, sunlight, heat, vibration, water and a rise in core temperature each define their own
form. (**consensus**) The provocation test is the diagnosis, which makes these the one part of
urticaria where a bedside test settles the question.

## The swelling that does not move: two mechanisms, and only one responds to an antihistamine

This is the single most important discrimination in the answer.

**Mast-cell-mediated angioedema** occurs with urticaria, often with itch, over minutes to hours,
and behaves as a deeper version of the same process. It responds to the treatments that work on
histamine, and where it is part of anaphylaxis, adrenaline is the treatment of the anaphylaxis.
(**consensus**)

**Bradykinin-mediated angioedema** is a different pathway entirely. Excess bradykinin increases
vascular permeability, and the clinical picture reflects the different mechanism: **no weals, no
itch**, slower onset over hours, longer duration, frequent abdominal attacks from bowel wall
involvement, sometimes a preceding tingling or a non-itchy marbled rash, and often a family
history. (**mechanism** **consensus**) The causes are angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors —
which reduce bradykinin breakdown, and where the first attack may come months or years after
starting the drug, which is the commonest reason the link is missed — and hereditary or acquired
deficiency of C1 inhibitor. (**consensus**)

**Antihistamines, corticosteroids and adrenaline do not work on this pathway.** (**consensus**)
That is not a dose problem. There is no histamine step to block, so there is nothing for the drug
to act on, and giving more of it does not help. The specific treatments are different in kind — C1
inhibitor replacement, a bradykinin receptor antagonist, and a plasma kallikrein inhibitor are the
classes involved, and which agents are available, licensed and funded is firmly
(**country-dependent**). No agent or dose is named here. What matters at the level of this answer
is that the airway risk is real, the treatment is pathway-specific, and the people who have
hereditary disease are managed by a specialist service with an individual plan.

## Treatment, at the level of principle

The mainstay for urticaria is a non-sedating H1-antihistamine, with national guidance describing
up-titration beyond the standard licensed amount under specialist advice where the response is
inadequate — the specific multiples, the licensing position and who may do it are
(**country-dependent**) and are in your national guidance and formulary, not here. (**consensus**)
The older sedating antihistamines are generally avoided for maintenance because of their effects
on sleep architecture, cognition and driving. (**consensus**) Long-term systemic corticosteroids
are not a treatment for chronic urticaria; a short course has a place in a severe flare in some
guidance. (**consensus**) Beyond that there are specialist escalations, including an anti-IgE
monoclonal antibody and immunosuppressants, with availability and funding differing by country.
(**country-dependent**)

For bradykinin-mediated angioedema the principle is the opposite of escalation: identify the
pathway, stop the responsible drug where there is one, and use a pathway-specific treatment
through a service that holds a plan for that person.

## The human stakes, said plainly

No analogy in this section.

**Anaphylaxis is the emergency and it is defined by what is happening beyond the skin.** Urticaria
with any of airway compromise, difficulty breathing, wheeze, stridor, hoarseness, tongue or throat
swelling, a sense of the throat closing, collapse, or low blood pressure is anaphylaxis until
proven otherwise, and the response is immediate adrenaline given by someone trained to give it,
emergency services, and nothing that delays either. Intramuscular adrenaline is the first-line
treatment for anaphylaxis in every mainstream guideline and antihistamines are not a substitute
for it. (**consensus**) **The doses and the devices are in your resuscitation council's guidance
and your formulary, and they are not in this answer.** Skin signs can be absent in anaphylaxis,
which is why their absence does not exclude it.

**Laryngeal angioedema is an airway emergency whichever pathway caused it**, and in the
bradykinin-mediated forms the drugs that work for anaphylaxis will not reverse it, so the airway
has to be secured and a pathway-specific treatment given. People with hereditary C1 inhibitor
deficiency are usually given written plans and medication to carry, and that plan is the authority
in an attack.

**And the chronic disease is not trivial.** Chronic urticaria is itchy day and night, disrupts
sleep, is visible, and is frequently dismissed as a rash. People commonly arrive having eliminated
most of their diet on the basis of a test result that never meant what they were told it meant,
and part of the consultation is undoing that. Angioedema of the face does not look like a minor
problem to the person who has it or to anyone who sees them. None of that is written here to be
affecting; it is written because it changes what the consultation has to contain.

Without softening: nothing here is for any reader's own use and no treatment named here is a
recommendation. Anyone with swelling of the lips, tongue, mouth or throat, any difficulty
breathing or swallowing, a hoarse voice with swelling, or a feeling that the throat is closing,
needs emergency help immediately — call your local emergency number. Anyone with recurrent
swelling without weals, or with unexplained recurrent abdominal pain and swellings, needs
assessment for the bradykinin-mediated causes rather than more antihistamine.

Swelling of the lips, tongue, mouth or throat, difficulty breathing or swallowing, a hoarse voice

— call your local emergency number. Someone with a prescribed adrenaline auto-injector and an

anything general.

Local guidance and the policy where you practise are the authority on all of this, and they differ
by country and by institution.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanisms and the discrimination between the two angioedema pathways are settled, and they
are the part worth memorising. What moves is the treatment ladder. The internationally adopted
urticaria guideline has been revised several times and the position on up-titration, on the
sequencing of specialist agents and on the role of short corticosteroid courses has changed
between revisions, so the edition matters. Several newer agents acting on mast cell signalling
have been in late-stage development for chronic spontaneous urticaria and the licensed landscape
is likely to look different within a few years. In hereditary angioedema the treatment options
have expanded substantially over the last decade, including long-term prophylaxis given by
different routes, and access to them varies sharply by country. The understanding of chronic
spontaneous urticaria as substantially autoimmune has strengthened rather than reversed. Current
as of October 2026.
