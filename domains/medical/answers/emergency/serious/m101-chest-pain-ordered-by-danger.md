# The complaint is an output, and the order of the workup states the cost of being wrong

Chest pain is not a diagnosis, a system or a severity. It is a single reported output with a long
list of possible generators, several of which are unrelated to one another in anatomy, in
mechanism and in treatment. The workup is therefore not organised around the question *what is
most likely*, which a textbook can answer, but around *what would cost most to miss in the time I
have*, which only the ordering can answer. **(Mechanism** — this is a decision-theoretic
consequence of an asymmetric loss function, checkable by reasoning rather than by
citation.**)**

That single sentence explains almost every feature of the assessment that looks strange from
outside it: why a person with a story that sounds entirely muscular still gets looked at for
something else, why the first tests are not the most informative ones available, and why a
reassuring finding is recorded rather than acted on.

## One word, and the generators behind it

```
   the reported complaint                 the generators, by mechanism
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                                   ┌── coronary: supply and demand mismatch, or occlusion
                                   ├── aortic: a wall splitting along its length
                                   ├── pulmonary vascular: obstruction of the pulmonary circuit
   "it hurts in my chest"  ────────┼── pleural and pleural space: inflammation, air, fluid
                                   ├── pericardial: inflammation, or filling that is restricted
                                   ├── oesophageal: spasm, inflammation, or a full-thickness tear
                                   ├── chest wall: muscle, rib, joint, cartilage, nerve
                                   ├── referred from below the diaphragm: biliary, pancreatic,
                                   │   gastric
                                   └── and presentations where the mechanism is not structural
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     ONE output, reported in one phrase, usually in one sentence.
     The phrase does not carry which row produced it, and the rows differ in
     time-to-harm by orders of magnitude and in treatment completely.
```

The rows are not variants of one another. Two of them are treated in ways that are actively
harmful if the other is the true diagnosis, which is the strongest possible argument against
settling the question by pattern recognition alone. **(Consensus** that the major vascular
differentials require separating before treatment commits, and that treatment for one can worsen
the other; the specific agents, thresholds and pathways are **country-dependent** and are not
given here.**)**

## Why ordering by danger is not the same as ordering by probability

Two lists exist and they are almost disjoint. The probability-ordered list is dominated by causes
that are self-limiting. The harm-ordered list is dominated by causes that are uncommon in any
unselected group of people reporting chest pain.

The quantity that actually matters is a product of three terms: how likely this generator is here,
how much harm follows from missing it, and how much that harm is reduced by finding it *now*
rather than later. A generator with a small first term and a very large second and third term can
outrank a generator with a large first term and a second term close to zero. **(Mechanism.)** That
is the whole content of "rule out the dangerous things first", and it is worth saying as a product
rather than as a slogan, because the slogan gets misremembered as *assume the worst*, which is a
different and worse policy. Assuming the worst would mean treating the worst; the actual policy is
to **exclude** the worst while expecting the common.

The third term is the one most often dropped. A condition that is catastrophic but equally
catastrophic whether found now or in an hour does not earn an early place on the grounds of
severity alone. What earns an early place is severity *multiplied by* the difference early action
makes — which is why the ordering here and the ordering in question m031's primary survey are the
same kind of sort with the same kind of key.

## The first tests are chosen for what they change, not for what they show

A test belongs in the first pass if its result changes what happens in the next few minutes. By
that criterion the earliest investigations in this presentation are not the most sensitive or the
most specific available; they are the fast, repeatable, widely available ones whose result
redirects everything downstream. Slower tests with greater discriminating power come second, not
because they matter less but because their answer arrives after the window in which the first
decision had to be made. **(Consensus** on the principle; which tests, in what order, with what
turnaround, is institutional and **country-dependent**.**)**

Three consequences follow, and all three look illogical from outside.

* **Time-critical possibilities are pursued in parallel rather than in sequence.** Serial
  exclusion costs the sum of the waiting times, and the shortest clock in the set is the one that
  binds.
* **Some tests are repeated rather than interpreted once.** Where the quantity being measured
  changes over hours, a first measurement and a second measurement are different pieces of
  information, and the first on its own cannot do the work of both. A normal first result in a
  process that declares itself late is not an exclusion. **(Consensus.)**
* **A test is ordered to change management, and if no result would change management it is not
  part of the first pass.** This is the same principle question m104 develops for imaging, where
  the test carries a harm of its own.

## Two things can be true at the same time

The most consequential error in this presentation is not failing to think of the dangerous
generator. It is finding a plausible benign one and treating that as an exclusion. The generators
are not mutually exclusive: chest-wall tenderness that reproduces the pain can coexist with
coronary disease, a long history of reflux does not confer immunity from anything, and a person
can have two active processes at once. **(Consensus.)**

So the reasoning has to hold a disjunction open rather than collapse it. The failure mode has a
name — premature closure, the same shape question m048 describes in general practice — and it has
a recognisable signature in a record: the first plausible label appears early, and every
subsequent observation is interpreted as consistent with it. Question m068's anchoring argument is
the same mechanism with the prior supplied by the mechanism of injury instead of by the first
guess.

## What the character of the complaint buys, and what it does not

The history is for building a prior over the rows in the diagram, and it is good at that. It is
much weaker than it feels as a verdict on any one row. Individual descriptors — where it is, what
it feels like, what it moves with, what it radiates to — shift the probabilities, usually less
than the confidence they generate; and several national bodies have moved away from describing
presentations as *typical* or *atypical* on the grounds that the vocabulary encoded a false
confidence and systematically under-weighted presentations in groups the old descriptions were
not derived from. **(Consensus** that no single historical feature is sufficient on its own;
**country-dependent** for the terminology now preferred, which differs between national bodies and
is still moving.**)**

That is why the history is treated as a prior rather than a conclusion — the same use question
m011 makes of the encounter table, where the setting changes what is probable without ever
determining what is in front of you.

## Why the order is written down

The ordering is not reconstructed by each clinician in each case. It is written into pathways and
checklists for exactly the reason question m032 gives: the decision is made under time pressure,
rarely by any one person, and with a cost of variance that is paid by the people on the tail of
the distribution. A written order also makes the omissions auditable, which an order held in
someone's head does not.

## The human stakes, said plainly

A person reporting chest pain is frightened, and usually frightened of precisely the diagnosis the
assessment is trying to exclude. The work happens around them for a period in which nobody can
honestly say what it is yet, and that period is itself part of what they experience.

Three things follow that are not technicalities. The first is that the reasoning above is a
reasoning about a person and not about a presentation, and that the person can be told what is
being looked for and why without being told a conclusion that has not been reached. Uncertainty
stated plainly is tolerated far better than confidence that later has to be withdrawn. The second
is that a benign final answer is not a reason to treat the assessment as unnecessary; the
assessment is what made the answer benign, and a person told their pain was *nothing* has been
given a worse account of their own care than one told what was excluded and how. The third is
that the groups whose presentations were historically described as atypical — and whose outcomes
are, on audit, worse — are worse off because of how the reasoning was applied to them, not because
of how they presented. **(Consensus** that recognition is less reliable in some groups and that
this contributes to avoidable harm; the size of the effect is a matter of published audit and is
not quantified here.**)**

None of this is a protocol, and nothing above is a sequence of actions. No test, order, threshold,
interval, score, agent or dose appears, because the reader's own local pathway is the authority on
every one of them and this is an explanation of why that pathway has the shape it has.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Chest pain is one complaint. Name the mechanism families behind it, and say which pairs are
  treated in ways that would harm the other.
* Write the quantity that sets the order of the workup as a product of three terms. Which term is
  most often dropped?
* Why is "rule out the worst first" not the same instruction as "assume the worst"?
* Why are the first tests not the most discriminating ones available?
* A plausible benign explanation has been found. What has and has not been established?
* What is the history for, if it is not for the verdict?
* Why is the ordering written down rather than reconstructed each time?

## Scope and safety

**If someone is unwell right now, call your local emergency number.** This is not for use during
an emergency, and reading this instead of calling for help would be worse than doing nothing at
all. Chest pain in particular is a presentation where delay is the harm.

This is revision material about *why an assessment is ordered the way it is*, written for someone
already trained. It is deliberately not a protocol and not a decision aid: it names no test
sequence, no risk score and no score's criteria, no threshold, no interval, no agent and no dose,
and it is not something to consult while assessing anyone. Chest-pain pathways differ between
countries, between regions and between institutions, and they are revised. The reader's own
national guidance, society guidance and local pathway are the authority; this is not, and it has
had no clinical review. Nothing here describes any real person or any real case.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument is stable: that one complaint has many unrelated generators, that the
ordering of the workup is a harm-weighted rather than a probability-weighted sort, that the
generators are not mutually exclusive, and that the history functions as a prior are all
long-standing teaching and follow from the structure of the problem rather than from any single
document. What is not stable is everything specific. Which investigations sit in the first pass,
how serial measurement is used, which risk stratification tool is in force, and the vocabulary for
describing presentations all differ between national bodies and move with each revision cycle; the
retreat from *typical* and *atypical* language is recent, uneven between countries, and still in
progress. Treat this as an explanation of a principle dated October 2026, and read the current
pathway from the national body, society and institution that govern where the reader actually
works for anything beyond the principle.
