# These are parameters of excitable tissue, so they present as behaviour and the number confirms

A laboratory result is the usual way a clinician meets an electrolyte derangement, and that order
of encounter teaches the wrong model. The number arrives, it is outside a printed range, it is
treated as the problem, and the problem is treated by moving the number back. Almost every error
in this area is a consequence of that sequence.

The better model is the other way round. Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, glucose
and hydrogen ion concentration are not findings that happen to be measurable; they are
**parameters in the equations that govern excitable tissue and enzyme function**. They do not
cause one disease. They shift the operating point of every nerve, every muscle fibre including
cardiac muscle, every secretory cell and every enzyme at once. So the presentation is a *syndrome*
— confusion, weakness, seizure, arrhythmia, ileus, tetany, reduced consciousness — and the
laboratory value is the **confirmation** of a hypothesis the bedside generated, not the origin of
it. (**Mechanism.**)

Three things follow from taking that seriously, and they are the three sections below.

## One scalar, many readouts, and the readouts are not specific to it

```
   a single plasma concentration moves
            │
            ├──► resting membrane potential and the threshold for firing
            │         └──► weakness, paraesthesia, tetany, reduced reflexes, seizure
            │
            ├──► cardiac conduction and repolarisation
            │         └──► arrhythmia, which may be the FIRST manifestation
            │
            ├──► smooth muscle contractility
            │         └──► ileus, urinary retention, gastrointestinal symptoms
            │
            ├──► cortical function
            │         └──► confusion, drowsiness, reduced consciousness
            │
            └──► enzyme activity and oxygen delivery
                      └──► non-specific malaise, nausea, the complaint with no name

   and NONE of those outputs identifies which scalar moved.
   Every one of them is also produced by a dozen unrelated things.
```

The diagram is the argument for why these present as a syndrome and not as a disease. **The output
set is wide and the mapping from output to cause is not invertible.** (**Mechanism.**) A person
who is confused and weak has produced a reading consistent with a derangement of any of several
ions, with sepsis, with a drug effect, with a structural neurological event, and with a dozen
other things — which is why the derangement is something looked for in a long list of
presentations rather than something with a presentation of its own.

Two corollaries are worth stating because they are what make this an emergency topic rather than a
biochemistry one.

* **The first manifestation can be the most dangerous one.** For some derangements the cardiac
  consequence does not arrive after a sequence of milder warnings; it can be the opening event. So
  a derangement is not graded by how unwell the person currently looks. (**Consensus.**)
* **Several move together, and one can be the reason another will not correct.** Derangements of
  these ions share causes — gastrointestinal losses, renal handling, diuretics, refeeding,
  endocrine disease — so finding one raises the probability of the others, and in some
  well-recognised pairings correcting one is futile until the other has been addressed. The
  specific pairings and their management are formulary and guideline material and are not
  described here. (**Consensus.**)

## The rate of change does more work than the value, and the panel cannot show it

This is the single most useful idea in the topic and it is the one a printed reference range
actively obscures.

A plasma concentration is a **level**. The clinical consequence of that level depends heavily on
**how fast it got there**, because the tissues that matter adapt — cells move solute and water,
the brain in particular adjusts its own osmolality over hours to days — and an adapted tissue
tolerates a value that an unadapted tissue does not. (**Mechanism.**) So:

* **The same number can be an incidental finding in one person and an emergency in another**, and
  the difference is the slope rather than anything about either value.
* **A previous result is therefore part of the test.** A value with no prior is a level with no
  derivative, which is the same structural point as question m126's single abdominal examination:
  one observation cannot contain a trend.
* **And the correction has a rate of its own, which is an intervention with its own harms.** For
  several of these derangements, moving the number too quickly causes harm that is distinct from —
  and sometimes worse than — the harm of the derangement itself, because the adaptation that made
  the abnormal value tolerable now makes the normal one intolerable. This is why correction is
  prescribed as a rate with a ceiling and monitored against it, rather than as a target to be
  reached. The specific ceilings, intervals and fluids are guideline and formulary material, they
  differ between countries and institutions, and **none of them appears here**. (**Consensus**
  that the principle is rate-limited correction; (**country-dependent**) for every number attached
  to it.)

The asymmetry between high and low is worth naming as a separate fact, because the reference
range's two-sided presentation hides it. For several of these ions the high and low states are not
mirror images: they have different presentations, different speeds, different first manifestations
and entirely different treatments. A reader who has learned the range as *too much or too little
of the same thing* has learned a symmetry that is not there. (**Consensus.**)

## What is measured is not what is present

The last structural point, and the one that causes the most confusion at the bedside.

| What the number is | What it is not |
| --- | --- |
| a concentration in one compartment, usually plasma | the total amount in the body |
| the result of a distribution between compartments that can shift in minutes | a stable property of the person |
| a measurement made on a sample, with pre-analytic conditions that can change it | an observation of the patient |
| for some ions, a total that includes a bound fraction | the physiologically active fraction |

Each row generates a real error. **A normal concentration with a large total deficit** is routine,
because the compartments buffer; treating the concentration as the inventory under-treats. **A
concentration that moves without any change in total body content** is also routine, because a
shift between compartments does it — which is why some apparent corrections are redistribution
rather than repletion, and reverse. **A pre-analytic artefact** — sample handling, haemolysis, the
site and manner of collection, a drip arm — produces a number that is a fact about the specimen
and not about the person, and the first response to an implausible result is to ask whether the
specimen explains it. (**Consensus.**) And for ions carried partly bound, **the measured total and
the active fraction diverge** whenever the binding partner or the pH moves, which is exactly when
the patient is unwell.

So the bedside has a role the laboratory cannot take over: the number is interpretable only
against the clinical state, the trajectory, the drugs, the losses and the plausibility of the
specimen. (**Mechanism.**)

Nothing above names a reference range, a threshold, a correction rate, a fluid, an agent or a
dose, and no electrocardiographic criterion is listed. All of those are local, several are
actively revised, and the reader's own formulary and local guideline are the authority. The
relationship between a laboratory value and the decisions that follow it is exactly where
remembered numbers do harm.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Two things make this topic unusually easy to get wrong in a way that lands on a person rather than
on a chart.

The first is that the presentation is frequently behavioural, and behaviour is the presentation
most likely to be attributed to the person rather than to a mechanism. Confusion, agitation,
drowsiness, apparent uncooperativeness, slurred speech, unsteadiness, a change in personality: all
of these are produced by metabolic derangement and all of them are also what a frightened,
exhausted or unwell person looks like, and all of them are routinely attributed to intoxication,
to dementia, to a psychiatric cause or to difficulty. The derangement is then never looked for,
and the label does the suppressing — the same mechanism as question m105, where a word naming the
location of a cause is read as naming the absence of one. The person least likely to be believed
is the person whose presentation is most likely to be metabolic.

The second is that the information needed to interpret the number lives with the person and the
people around them. What they have been eating and drinking, what has been coming out and for how
long, what medicines they take and which were started recently, whether they have been vomiting,
whether the dose of something changed, whether there has been a period of eating almost nothing.
Someone who is confused cannot supply that, which means the account of whoever is with them is not
background: it is the history, and it is frequently the only route to the mechanism. A department
that collects it at the start has a much better chance than one that collects it after the result
arrives.

Two smaller points, said plainly. Bloods hurt, repeated bloods hurt more, and a plan that involves
repeated sampling should be explained rather than simply happening to someone; that explanation is
also what makes the fifth sample possible. And someone whose confusion has resolved after
correction often has no memory of a period in which they were frightened and behaved unlike
themselves, and may have been told about it afterwards. That is worth handling carefully. Nothing
about prognosis or outcome appears anywhere in this answer, which is deliberate.

And the honest closing point: the reason this presents as a syndrome rather than as a finding is
not that clinicians are careless about biochemistry. It is that the bedside signal genuinely is
non-specific, and the correct response to a genuinely non-specific signal is to test for several
things at once rather than to reason harder about which one it is.

## Scope and safety

**If someone is unwell right now, call your local emergency number.** This is not for use during
an emergency, and reading this instead of calling for help would be worse than doing nothing at
all.

This is revision material about *why a class of abnormalities presents as behaviour and is
confirmed by a number*, written for someone already trained. It is deliberately not a protocol and
not a decision aid: it names no reference range, no threshold, no correction rate, no monitoring
interval, no fluid, no replacement preparation, no agent, no dose and no electrocardiographic
criterion, and it is not something to consult while assessing or treating anyone. Replacement and
correction are areas in which an error causes direct harm, and the formulary and the local
protocol are the only acceptable sources. Reference ranges are assay-specific and
laboratory-specific; correction guidance differs between national bodies and between specialist
societies within one country. The reader's own formulary, laboratory and local policy are the
authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here describes any real person
or any real case.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physiology is stable and the numbers are not, and in this topic the gap between those two is
unusually wide. That these ions are parameters of excitable tissue and enzyme function rather than
findings, that the output set is wide and the mapping from output to cause is not invertible, that
the first manifestation can be a cardiac one, that derangements share causes and therefore
co-occur, that adaptation makes the rate of change more informative than the value, that
correction is therefore rate-limited because adaptation reverses the direction of the harm, that
high and low states of one ion are not mirror images, and that a concentration in one compartment
is not an inventory are all long-standing and follow from physiology rather than from any single
document. What moves: reference ranges are assay-specific and are revised by laboratories;
correction-rate recommendations differ between national bodies and between nephrology,
endocrinology and intensive-care sources and are periodically restudied, including the question of
how fast is too fast; the preparations available and their licensed routes differ by country and
change; the classification of some of these syndromes has been rewritten more than once; and
electrocardiographic interpretation in this area is taught differently in different places. Treat
this as an explanation of a principle dated October 2026, and read the current national guidance,
the current formulary and the reader's own laboratory's documentation for anything beyond the
principle.
