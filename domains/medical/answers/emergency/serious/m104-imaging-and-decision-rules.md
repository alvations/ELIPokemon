# A test is an intervention, and its cost is paid whether or not it answers anything

The default framing of a test is that it adds information at no cost, so that ordering one is
always at worst neutral. That framing is wrong in a specific and useful way: an imaging test is an
act performed on a person, it has effects of its own, and those effects are delivered whether the
result turns out to be helpful or not. The benefit is **conditional** on the result changing what
happens next. The cost is **unconditional**. Once that asymmetry is stated, most of the apparently
awkward behaviour around imaging — the reluctance, the rules, the asking of the question
differently — becomes straightforward. **(Mechanism.)**

## The four costs, only one of which is the famous one

```
   ordering an imaging test
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   1  ionising radiation, where the modality uses it: a stochastic, dose-related
      risk that accumulates over a lifetime and weighs more heavily the younger
      the person is                                           ── paid on every scan
   2  time: the interval to the result, during which the decision it was meant to
      inform has not been made, plus time spent away from where observation
      happens                                                 ── paid on every scan
   3  capacity: the scanner, the reporting, the transfer — and therefore the next
      person's access to the same resource                    ── paid on every scan
   4  information that answers a question nobody asked: findings unrelated to the
      presentation, each capable of generating its own investigations, its own
      procedures and its own label                            ── paid on some scans
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     Costs 1 to 3 do not depend on the result. Cost 4 is a function of
     looking, not of what was wrong. The benefit depends entirely on the result
     changing a decision.
```

Cost 1 is the one that gets taught and it is not always the dominant one. **(Consensus** that
ionising radiation carries a dose-related stochastic risk, that the relevance is greater in
younger people and with repeated exposure, and that justification and dose minimisation are
accordingly standard practice; every figure, dose-reference level and national framework is
(**country-dependent**) and none is given here.**)**

Cost 4 is the one that is systematically under-weighted, because it is invisible at the moment of
ordering and lands weeks later as somebody else's problem. **(Consensus** that incidental findings
are common on cross-sectional imaging and that the resulting cascade carries its own harms; the
rates are a matter of published series and are not quantified here.**)**

## The arithmetic that follows

Write the decision as a comparison and the structure is obvious. The expected benefit of a test is
the probability that its result changes management, multiplied by how much that change is worth.
The expected cost is the sum of the four above, mostly incurred with certainty. **(Mechanism.)**

Three consequences, and they are not intuitions:

* **A test with a low probability of changing management is net harm even when it feels
  harmless.** Not because it is dangerous, but because its benefit term is near zero while its
  cost term is not.
* **"To be safe" is not a reason unless it names what is being made safer.** Reassurance is a real
  benefit and it belongs in the first term — but only where the test can deliver it, which depends
  on what the test can exclude rather than on how worried anyone is.
* **The relevant comparison is the test against its alternative, not the test against nothing.**
  Where the alternative is a period of observation that itself carries risk, or a decision made
  without a fact that only imaging supplies, the cost of the scan can be a bargain. The argument
  here is about trades, not about abstinence.

## What a decision rule is actually for

The common misreading is that a decision rule exists to find disease. It does not. In this
presentation most rules exist to make **not** testing defensible, reproducible and auditable. That
is a different job from diagnosis and it has different design consequences.

* **It is tuned asymmetrically on purpose.** A rule whose job is exclusion is built to miss as
  close to nothing as its derivation allows, and it pays for that with a large number of people
  who satisfy it and have nothing. A reader who criticises such a rule for low specificity has
  mistaken the objective. **(Consensus.)**
* **It is a rule, not an estimate.** It takes defined inputs and returns a category. That is what
  makes it reproducible between clinicians and auditable afterwards — question m032's argument
  about written protocols, applied to a decision about a test.
* **It is only valid in the population it was validated in.** Derivation finds the candidate
  predictors; validation in a separate population is what makes the rule a rule; and use outside
  that population is the single commonest misuse. **(Consensus.)**
* **It shifts the default.** Before a rule, the defensible act under uncertainty is to test; after
  one, the defensible act is to apply the rule and record the result. The change in behaviour
  comes largely from the change in what has to be justified.

**No rule is named or reproduced in this answer, and that is deliberate.** A decision rule used
from memory is one of the recognisable ways they fail: criteria drift, a threshold is
misremembered, and the version in someone's head is not the version that was validated. The
reader's own institution specifies which rules are in force and holds their exact wording.

## Three shapes of restraint, which are not interchangeable

| | How it decides | Reproducible? | Auditable? | Where it is strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A decision rule | defined inputs, fixed output | yes | yes | making an omission defensible, and making variance small |
| Clinical judgement | a probability, uncalibrated and unwritten | no | barely | the case the rule was not derived for, and the outlier a rule would wave through |
| System-level change | availability, defaults, prompts, feedback | yes | yes | behaviour in aggregate, with nobody having to decide anything |

All three reduce the number of tests ordered. Only the first is explicit at the level of the
individual decision; only the third works without anyone's cooperation; and judgement is the one
that cannot be audited and also the one that cannot be dispensed with, because every rule has a
boundary and someone has to notice when a person is outside it. **(Consensus** that rules
supplement rather than replace judgement; the strength of the evidence for each varies by rule and
by setting.**)**

## How rules fail, each with a name

* **Out-of-population use.** The rule is applied to someone who would not have been enrolled in
  the study that validated it.
* **Inversion.** A rule derived to permit exclusion is read as an indication to test, so a tool
  designed to reduce imaging increases it.
* **Partial application.** Some inputs are applied and others are skipped, which produces a result
  that resembles the rule's output and is not it.
* **Costless compliance.** The rule is documented and the test is ordered anyway. The audit then
  shows adherence and the behaviour has not changed.
* **Missing inputs.** A rule cannot use a finding nobody obtained or recorded. An absent
  observation is not a negative one — the same error question m070 calls the missing pertinent
  negative.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Two people are affected by this reasoning and they want opposite things. One has come in wanting a
scan, and a decision not to image can be experienced as being dismissed. The other does not want
to be in the scanner at all, and a decision to image carries them into a process that is
frightening, sometimes uncomfortable, and often poorly explained.

Three things follow and none is a technicality. A decision not to image is a positive decision and
is best communicated as one: what was considered, what makes it unlikely, what would change the
answer, and what to do if things change. An unexplained *no* and a reasoned *no* contain the same
clinical content and are not the same event for the person receiving it. Reassurance is a real
benefit but it is not a reliable one — a normal scan does not always reassure, and for some people
it starts a search rather than ending one. And the harm in cost 4 above lands on a specific person
who was well when they walked in: a finding of no consequence, discovered while looking for
something else, can convert someone into a patient with a surveillance schedule and an anxiety,
and nobody involved in the original decision ever sees that consequence. That asymmetry of
feedback is why the restraint has to be built into the system rather than left to be rediscovered.
**(Consensus** that incidentally detected findings cause anxiety and downstream investigation and
that this is a recognised harm of imaging.**)**

Nothing above is a protocol. No rule, no criterion, no threshold, no dose, no modality
recommendation and no interval appears, because every one of those is local, is revised, and
belongs to the reader's own guidance.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Name the four costs of an imaging test and say which of them are independent of the result.
* Write the decision as a comparison of expected benefit against expected cost. Which term is
  conditional?
* Why is a test with a low probability of changing management net harm even if it is physically
  harmless?
* What is a decision rule for, if it is not for finding disease?
* Why is low specificity not a criticism of an exclusion rule?
* Give three ways decision rules fail in use, and say which of them an audit of adherence would
  miss.
* Why is "the test against nothing" the wrong comparison?

## Scope and safety

**If someone is unwell right now, call your local emergency number.** This is not for use during
an emergency, and reading this instead of calling for help would be worse than doing nothing at
all.

This is revision material about *the structure of a testing decision*, written for someone already
trained. It is deliberately not a protocol and not a decision aid: it names no decision rule,
reproduces no criteria, and states no threshold, dose, dose reference level, modality
recommendation or interval, and it is not something to consult while deciding anything about
anyone. Imaging referral guidance, radiation protection law and the set of decision rules in force
differ between countries, between regulators and between institutions, and they are revised. The
reader's own national body, regulator and local policy are the authority; this is not, and it has
had no clinical review. Nothing here describes any real person or any real case.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument is stable: that a test is an intervention whose cost is largely
unconditional while its benefit is conditional, that the four costs include one that lands on
somebody else later, that an exclusion rule is asymmetrically tuned by design, and that rules are
valid only in the populations that validated them are all long-standing and follow from decision
theory and from the design of the studies rather than from any single document. What is not stable
is everything specific. Which rules are in force, their exact criteria, the imaging referral
guidance for any given presentation, dose reference levels and the legal duties attached to
requesting an exposure all differ by country and by institution and move with each revision cycle;
the incidental-findings literature is active and the estimates in it move. Treat this as an
explanation of a principle dated October 2026, and read the current guidance from the national
body, regulator and institution that govern where the reader actually works for anything beyond
the principle.
