# Fever is a readout the host builds, so an impaired host returns a different one

The ordinary reasoning about fever is a chain with an unexamined link in it. A higher temperature
means a larger response, a larger response means a bigger insult, and a normal temperature means
the insult is small or absent. The unexamined link is that **the temperature is produced by the
host and not by the organism**, so every inference from its magnitude is an inference about the
host's capacity to mount it. Change that capacity and the whole chain has to be rebuilt.

Two things change at once in someone who is immunocompromised, and they pull in opposite
directions, which is why the topic is confusing before it is separated.

* The **signal gets weaker**: fever may be blunted or absent, and the localising signs that
  normally point at the site are frequently made of the very cells that are missing.
* The **consequence gets worse and faster**: for a given organism and a given portal, the time
  available before serious deterioration is shorter.

A threshold is a function of both of those, so it moves twice in the same direction: a weaker
signal is acted on, and it is acted on sooner.

## The sign and the harm are built by different machinery

```
   organism / portal of entry
        │
        ├───────────────────────────────► tissue invasion, bacteraemia, organ dysfunction
        │                                          ▲
        │                                          │   THE HARM
        │                                          │   built by the organism
        │
        └──► recognition by host cells ──► cytokine release ──► hypothalamic set point rises
                      │                           │
                      │                           └──► FEVER, rigors, the localising signs
                      │                                THE SIGNAL
                      │                                built by the HOST
                      ▼
             neutrophils, monocytes, complement, antibody, spleen, intact mucosa
             ── remove any of these and the lower branch is attenuated
                while the upper branch is unchanged or amplified
```

Read that way the result is not surprising at all. Pus is neutrophils; consolidation on a film is
substantially an inflammatory infiltrate; meningism is an inflammatory response in a space. A
person without neutrophils can have an established infection with no pus, a near-normal film, and
an unimpressive examination. (**Mechanism.**) The same logic covers the drugs: corticosteroids and
other immunosuppressants attenuate the febrile response itself, so the signal can be suppressed by
the treatment that created the vulnerability. (**Consensus.**)

And the attenuation is not uniform. Hypothermia rather than fever, isolated new hypotension,
unexplained tachycardia, new confusion, rigors without a recorded temperature rise, or simply a
carer's report that the person is not themselves all carry weight in this group that they would
not carry in an otherwise well adult. (**Consensus.**) The replacement for the missing threshold
is not a different number; it is a wider set of admissible signals.

## Which defect is present predicts which organisms matter

"Immunocompromised" is not one state, and treating it as one is the commonest error after
over-reading a normal temperature. The useful question is *which component is missing*, because
each component defends against a different class and its absence opens a different route.
(**Consensus**, as the broad pattern; the detail of local epidemiology and local resistance is
(**country-dependent**).)

| Component impaired | Typical cause | What becomes available |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neutrophils (number or function) | cytotoxic chemotherapy, marrow failure or infiltration, some drugs | bacteria from the gut and skin flora; invasive fungal disease as the duration lengthens |
| Cell-mediated immunity | corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, some biologics, advanced untreated HIV infection, transplantation | intracellular bacteria, mycobacteria, some fungi, reactivating herpesviruses and other latent organisms |
| Antibody and complement; absent or non-functioning spleen | splenectomy, sickle cell disease, some haematological conditions, some biologics | encapsulated bacteria, with a presentation that can escalate very fast |
| Barrier integrity | mucositis, indwelling vascular access, catheters, drains, surgical wounds | whatever is colonising that barrier, which is why the device is part of the history |
| Prior exposure | recent antimicrobials, recent hospitalisation, known colonisation | a shifted spectrum and shifted resistance, which changes what empirical cover has to mean here |

Two consequences of the table rather than of any row in it. The first is that **the history is the
diagnostic test** — which agent, how long ago, which cycle, which device, which previous organism
— and in this presentation the history frequently exists only in a record the assessing clinician
cannot see. The second is that **the defects combine**, often in the same person on the same day,
so the list is not a set of mutually exclusive boxes to choose between.

The time course of the neutrophil defect specifically is predictable rather than random, and
question m028 is where that argument lives; it is not re-derived here.

## What a threshold is made of, and why this one moves

A threshold is not a property of a measurement. It is the point at which the expected cost of
acting is less than the expected cost of waiting, and it therefore has two inputs: how much the
signal changes the probability, and what the two errors cost. (**Mechanism.**)

* **The probability term moves** because the prior is different. A given temperature in someone
  with no neutrophils is more likely to represent serious bacterial infection than the same
  temperature in an otherwise well adult, and a *normal* temperature excludes much less.
* **The cost term moves** because the untreated course is faster and the consequences of delay are
  more severe.

Both terms push the same way, which is why guidance in this area is written as *treat first,
confirm after* rather than as *investigate, then treat*. The specific temperature value, the
specific neutrophil count and the specific time target that define and operationalise this
syndrome differ between national guidelines and between institutions, they are revised, and **none
of them is stated in this answer**. (**Country-dependent.**) Neither is any risk-stratification
tool named, nor any of its criteria listed: a stratification instrument recalled from memory is
one of the failure modes this subject is about, and a reader who needs it needs the current
version in front of them rather than a remembered one.

## Empirical before specific, and why that is not sloppiness

The structure of the decision is worth making explicit, because it looks like a shortcut and is
not one.

* The organism is unknown at the moment the decision is made, and the test that would name it
  takes longer than the window in which treatment changes the outcome. So the choice is not
  *empirical or targeted*; it is *empirical now or targeted later*, and later is after the harm.
  (**Mechanism.**)
* **Cultures first, then treatment, without the first delaying the second.** The specimens are
  what make narrowing possible afterwards, and omitting them converts a reversible broad decision
  into a permanent one.
* **Broad cover is a statement about uncertainty, not about severity.** Its width is set by what
  the defect makes available and by what local resistance patterns make likely, which is exactly
  why it cannot be recalled from a textbook written for somewhere else. (**Country-dependent.**)
* **Narrowing is part of the plan and not an afterthought.** The collective-action problem that
  makes this hard — every individual decision to stay broad is defensible and the aggregate is not
  — is question m045's argument and it applies here in its sharpest form, because this is the
  population in which nobody wants to be the one who narrowed.

## Where the system rather than the clinician is the variable

The failure mode in practice is almost never that a clinician who knew the person was
immunocompromised reasoned badly about the temperature. It is that nobody knew.

Three structural answers exist, all (**country-dependent**) in their detail. Patient-held
information — an alert card, a treatment record, a written instruction about what to do and where
to go — which turns the person into the carrier of the fact. Direct-access arrangements that let
someone bypass the general queue, which exist precisely because the ordinary queue is calibrated
for a population whose signals mean something else. And a triage question asked of everybody
rather than of the people who look unwell, because the whole point is that this group does not
look unwell early. (**Consensus** that these three are the recognised structural responses; what
they are called, who provides them and whether they exist at all differs sharply between systems.)

Nothing above names a temperature, a cell count, a time target, an agent, a dose or a
stratification tool, because all of them are local, all are revised, and the reader's own guidance
is the authority.

## The human stakes, said plainly

The person who has to act on all of this first is usually not a clinician. It is someone at home,
often tired, often having been told at some earlier point that a temperature means coming in
immediately, and now having to decide at two in the morning whether this counts. They are
frequently frightened in a specific way: they have been told that this is the dangerous
complication, and they are being asked to judge it themselves with a thermometer.

Four things follow, and none of them is a mechanism.

The first is that the instruction has to be given in a form that survives being frightened at two
in the morning. A single sentence, written down, that says what to do and where to go, is worth
more than a careful explanation of neutrophil kinetics. People keep the card; they do not keep the
explanation.

The second is that this is the group for whom *waiting in a waiting room* is itself a harm — both
because the delay matters more and because the room is full of other people's infections. A
department that cannot act on that has a problem it should own as a problem, rather than attaching
it to the person who is sitting there.

The third is that being repeatedly told that the thing that nearly happened did not happen is
wearing. Someone who has come in five times and been sent home five times learns, correctly, that
most of these episodes resolve, and that learning is exactly what the advice is asking them to
override. Treating the sixth attendance as a nuisance is therefore a predictable error, and
question m130 is the argument about why repeat attendance is a signal rather than an annoyance.

The fourth is that the people around the person carry information that nothing else carries. A
partner or parent who says *she is not right* is making an observation against a baseline nobody
in the department has access to, and in a group whose signals are blunted that observation is
frequently the strongest one available.

And the honest closing point: the advice given to these patients is deliberately over-inclusive,
because the alternative is worse, and that means most people acting on it correctly will turn out
not to have needed to. Saying that out loud — *most of the time this will be nothing, and we still
want you to come* — is part of making the advice work, rather than an admission that it is
excessive.

## Scope and safety

**If someone is unwell right now, call your local emergency number.** This is not for use during
an emergency, and reading this instead of calling for help would be worse than doing nothing at
all.

This is revision material about *why an inference from a sign depends on who produces the sign*,
written for someone already trained. It is deliberately not a protocol and not a decision aid: it
names no temperature, no cell count, no time target, no antimicrobial agent, no dose and no
risk-stratification instrument, it lists no criterion of any such instrument, and it is not
something to consult while assessing anyone. Empirical antimicrobial choice is set locally from
local resistance data and nothing general can substitute for it. Definitions, thresholds, targets
and service arrangements differ sharply between countries and institutions. The reader's own
national guidance and local policy are the authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical
review. Nothing here describes any real person or any real case.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanism is stable and the numbers are not. That the febrile response is generated by the
host, that its attenuation and the attenuation of localising signs follow from the absence of the
cells that produce them, that immunosuppressant drugs suppress the signal as well as the defence,
that different immune defects open different organism classes, and that a threshold is a function
of both informativeness and the cost of a miss are all long-standing and follow from physiology
rather than from any single document. Everything operational moves. The temperature value and the
cell count that define the syndrome differ between national guidelines; the time-to-treatment
target differs and is periodically re-examined; risk-stratification instruments and the question
of who can be managed as an outpatient are actively revised; empirical regimens are rewritten as
local resistance changes, on a shorter cycle than guidelines are republished; and the availability
of direct-access routes and patient-held information is institutional. The classification of
immunosuppressive agents also keeps changing as new biologics enter use, so a mapping from drug to
defect that is accurate today may not be in five years. Treat this as an explanation of a
principle dated October 2026, and read the current national guideline and the current local
antimicrobial policy for anything beyond the principle.
