# Supportive care is agent-independent, which is why it is the treatment and not the fallback

Almost everything that a poison does to a person that could kill them, it does through a small
number of **final common pathways**: loss of the airway, failure of ventilation, failure of the
circulation, seizure, a disturbance of cardiac rhythm or conduction, a derangement of temperature,
and a derangement of the internal chemical environment. There are a very large number of toxic
agents and a very small number of ways they reach the exit. **(Mechanism** — this is a structural
observation about physiology and is checkable by reasoning rather than by citation.**)**

That structure does the whole work. Supporting those pathways is **agent-independent**: it does
not require knowing what was taken, which matters enormously, because in a large share of real
presentations the agent, the amount and the time are uncertain, misreported or unknown. And it
buys the one thing almost every poisoning needs, which is time — time in which the body's own
elimination reduces the exposure. Supportive care is therefore not what is done when nothing
better is available. It is the treatment, and it is the treatment in the overwhelming majority of
cases.

## One common pathway, and a short prologue in front of it

```
   the agent                 the final common pathways                the outcome
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                   ┌───►  airway lost                        ┐
     a very        ├───►  ventilation failing                │
     large         ├───►  circulation failing                ├──► supported, agent-unknown,
     number        ├───►  seizure                            │    for as long as it takes
     of agents     ├───►  rhythm or conduction disturbed     │
                   ├───►  temperature deranged               │
                   └───►  internal chemistry deranged        ┘
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                     ▲
                     │
     a specific antidote is a SHORT PROLOGUE in front of this, not a replacement for it
                     │
     and it exists only where all four of the conditions below hold at once
```

The diagram's last line is the real answer to the second half of the question. A specific antidote
is never an alternative to supporting the pathways; it is something done in addition, and the
supportive care continues around it.

## The four conditions, all of which must hold

An antidote has to clear every one of these to be worth having, and most agents fail at least one.
**(Mechanism** for why each condition is necessary; the list is a way of organising standard
teaching rather than a quotation from any document.**)**

**A defined target that can be acted on.** The agent must do its damage through a mechanism
specific enough to be interfered with — a receptor that can be occupied, an enzyme that can be
restored or bypassed, a metabolic step that can be out-competed, a molecule that can be bound or
sequestered. Agents whose harm comes from direct tissue destruction, or from a diffuse disturbance
with no single lever, offer nothing to aim at. The absence of a target is the commonest reason
there is no antidote, and it is not a gap in the pharmacopoeia — there is nothing there to make.

**A window that overlaps when people actually arrive.** An intervention whose benefit is confined
to a period that has usually passed before anyone presents is not useful however elegant its
mechanism. This condition fails quietly and often: the antidote exists, the mechanism is sound,
and by the time the diagnosis is made the lever no longer moves anything. It is also the condition
that makes a small number of antidotes genuinely time-critical, which is a different and much
stronger claim than being effective.

**Enough diagnostic confidence.** A specific agent is specific — it is aimed at one mechanism, and
aimed at the wrong one it does nothing useful while consuming attention, time and the belief that
something is being done. Several carry their own risks, and some produce effects that are harmful
if the suspected agent was not in fact involved. So the threshold of certainty required before
reaching for one is higher than for supportive care, which needs no diagnosis at all. This is the
condition that most often turns an available antidote into one that should not yet be used.

**Availability where and when it is needed.** It has to be manufactured, stocked, in date,
findable and known about. An antidote that exists in the literature and not in the building is not
an option in the first hour, and stock arrangements — regional stores, named holding sites, agreed
transfer routes — are part of the system rather than logistics attached to it.
**(Country-dependent** — which antidotes are stocked, where, and in what quantity differs by
country and by institution, and is exactly the sort of thing a reader must check locally.**)**

## Why decontamination occupies a narrower place than it used to

The same reasoning applies one step earlier. Preventing absorption is attractive in principle and
is strongly time-dependent in practice, and several once-routine measures were found to harm more
than they helped — in particular because the harms arrive in everybody who receives them while the
benefit arrives only in the subset who were going to absorb a dangerous amount in the window.
Where decontamination remains in use, it is in a narrower set of circumstances than it occupied
historically, and the circumstances differ between countries. **(Consensus** that the role has
narrowed and that several historical measures were withdrawn; (**country-dependent**) for what
each national body currently recommends, which is why nothing specific is stated here.**)** The
parallel with question m035 is exact: an intervention with a benign-sounding failure mode, given
to a population much wider than the one that benefits, is a net harm even when its mechanism is
real.

## The ordering of the options is not antidote-first

Everything above makes a point that is easy to lose: the interventions in this subject form a
rough order of robustness, and it runs opposite to their order of specificity.

* **Preventing the exposure, or removing the person from it**, is the most robust of all, needs no
  diagnosis, and is the whole content of occupational and environmental toxicology as well as of
  the safe storage of medicines. Where an exposure is continuing, interrupting it is the
  intervention, and it precedes every other consideration. **(Mechanism.)**
* **Supporting the final common pathways** comes next: agent-independent, correct under
  uncertainty, and the mainstay.
* **An intervention that covers a class** sits between the general and the specific — something
  that addresses a mechanism shared by a group of agents rather than one agent. It needs less
  diagnostic precision than a single-agent antidote and more than supportive care, and in practice
  this middle band is where a good deal of useful toxicology lives.
* **A single-agent antidote** is last on robustness and first on precision, and it demands that
  the diagnostic question has already been answered correctly.

Read upward and it is also a hierarchy of what survives being wrong. The top of the list is
unaffected by a mistaken hypothesis; the bottom of it is wasted by one.

## The structural answer, which is not a drug

Three features of how poisoning is actually managed follow from everything above, and all three
are about the system rather than about the agent.

* **A dedicated information service, not memory.** Most countries operate a poisons information
  service, usually with a telephone line for clinicians and a reference database behind it, and it
  is the authority on agent-specific management. The reason is precisely the structure of the
  problem: there are too many agents, the information changes, and the questions are rare for any
  individual clinician. Consulting it is the standard of care rather than an admission of
  ignorance. **(Consensus** that such services exist and are the reference;
  (**country-dependent**) in name, scope and how they are accessed.**)**
* **Risk assessment before intervention.** The useful question is not what was taken but what
  exposure this is likely to amount to in this person over the next several hours, which is a
  judgement about dose, timing, formulation, co-ingestion and the person's own physiology.
  Supportive care begins regardless; the antidote question is answered after that assessment, not
  before it.
* **Repeated reassessment, because exposure is a moving quantity.** Absorption continues, active
  metabolites appear, and a person who looked well on arrival may be at their worst some hours
  later. A single reassuring assessment carries much less than it would in a static problem. This
  is question m034's argument with a time axis bolted on.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Poisoning is not a category of chemistry. The people in it are frequently frightened, frequently
ashamed, often brought in by somebody else, and often in a situation that is not primarily medical
at all.

Two things follow, and neither is optional. A substantial share of presentations involve
self-harm, intentional overdose, or an exposure that happened in the context of a mental-health
crisis, and nothing in this answer addresses that: the assessment of risk to a person, the
assessment of capacity, safeguarding, and the involvement of mental-health services run alongside
the physiology and are at least as important as it. Those are specialist domains with their own
guidance, their own legal framework in every country, and their own services, and they are named
here rather than explained because an answer about final common pathways is the wrong document for
them. Anyone affected by thoughts of self-harm needs their local crisis service, their own
clinician, or the local emergency number — now, and not a revision page.

The second is about children and about older people, who arrive through a different door: an
exploratory exposure in a small child and an error with regular medicines in an older person are
both common, are both usually accidental, and are both poorly served by a framing built around
intentional overdose. And across all of these, the honest statement about supportive care is that
it is frequently the whole of the treatment, that this is not a lesser outcome, and that *waiting
while supporting* is an active, demanding, skilled thing to do rather than an absence of
intervention. **(Consensus.)**

This answer names no agent, no antidote, no dose, no threshold and no decontamination indication,
and that is deliberate rather than cautious. Agent-specific management differs by country, is
revised, and is the one part of this subject where a half-remembered specific is more dangerous
than no specific at all. It belongs to the poisons information service and the reader's own local
guidance.

## Scope and safety

**If someone is unwell right now, call your local emergency number.** This is not for use during
an emergency, and reading this instead of calling for help would be worse than doing nothing at
all. **If anyone may have been poisoned, the local emergency number and the local poisons
information service are the route, immediately.** Anyone having thoughts of harming themselves
needs their local crisis service, their own clinician or the local emergency number now, rather
than this page.

This is revision material about *why a field is organised the way it is*, written for someone
already trained. It is deliberately not a protocol and not a decision aid: it names no agent, no
antidote, no dose, no threshold, no decontamination indication and no sequence of actions, and it
is not something to consult while acting. Agent-specific management, antidote stocking and
decontamination guidance differ by country and by institution and are revised. The reader's
poisons information service, national guidance and local policy are the authority; this is not,
and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here describes any real person or any real case.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument is stable and uncontroversial: that poisoning kills through a small number
of final common pathways, that supporting those pathways is therefore agent-independent and is the
mainstay, and that specific antidotes are the exception rather than the rule are long-standing and
follow from physiology rather than from any single document. The four conditions are a way of
organising that teaching, not a quotation. What is not stable is everything specific: which
antidotes are considered worthwhile, which are stocked and where, what role decontamination
retains, and the risk-assessment thresholds for common agents all differ between countries and are
revised — and the position on several historical decontamination measures has changed more than
once within living memory. Treat this as an explanation of a principle dated October 2026, and use
the poisons information service and local policy for anything beyond the principle.
