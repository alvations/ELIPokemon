# Breathlessness is a mismatch, and the differential splits where the chain can break

Breathlessness is not a measurement. It is the sensation produced when the drive to breathe and
what breathing is achieving do not match, and that mismatch can be produced by a fault in any link
of a long chain. **(Mechanism.)** Severity tells you how fast to move. It does not tell you which
link is broken, and the treatment lives entirely in the link. That is the whole reason the
differential is drawn by system: a system is where an intervention can act, and severity is a
property of the chain as a whole.

This is the same shape as question m066's perfusion chain, one system along. There the single
observable was a pressure that could be normal while the product behind it was failing; here the
single observable is a sensation that is real, non-specific and produced by at least eight
unrelated mechanisms.

## The chain, and where each category attacks it

```
   drive ──► upper airway ──► lower airways ──► alveoli ──► carriage ──► pump ──► delivery
     │             │                │              │            │          │
     │             │                │              │            │          └─ cardiac: failure,
     │             │                │              │            │             rhythm, filling that
     │             │                │              │            │             is restricted
     │             │                │              │            └─ carriage: not enough carrier,
     │             │                │              │               or a carrier occupied by
     │             │                │              │               something else
     │             │                │              └─ parenchyma and pleural space: filled,
     │             │                │                 stiffened, collapsed, or compressed by air
     │             │                │                 or fluid outside the lung
     │             │                └─ lower airways: narrowed, or narrowed and inflamed
     │             └─ upper airway: obstructed, swollen or displaced — question m067's conjunction
     └─ drive and bellows: a metabolic demand the lungs are being asked to answer, a central drive
        that is reduced, or muscles and a chest wall that cannot do the work
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     ONE sensation. Eight loci. Treatment is specific to the locus and
     almost nothing in the list responds to the treatment for anything else in it.
```

Two of the loci are worth naming as traps because they are not lung at all. A problem of
**carriage** can produce breathlessness with entirely normal lungs, and a **metabolic** demand can
produce fast deep breathing in someone whose respiratory system is working perfectly and is simply
being asked to answer an acid load. **(Consensus.)**

## What the first observations buy: a partition, not an answer

The first few observations are cheap, and their value is that each one cuts the list rather than
naming a member of it. Three partitions whose boundaries are not parallel leave a small number of
cells, which is a great deal of progress for nothing spent.

* **The pattern of breathing, and whether a sentence can be completed.** This separates a problem
  of the *path* from a problem of the *supply*, and a drive that is working hard from a drive that
  is failing. A sentence that cannot be finished says the breath cannot be spared; question m067
  is about how much one sentence establishes.
* **The saturation, with its blind spot in view.** This separates faults in gas exchange from
  faults that are not about oxygen at all. The separation is only as good as the next section
  explains.
* **Whether the findings are symmetrical, and whether there is a noise and where in the cycle it
  falls.** Asymmetry points at a single-sided mechanical cause. A noise localises a narrowing, and
  the fact that a noise exists means air is still moving through the narrowing — so the
  disappearance of a noise is not improvement unless the work of breathing fell with it.
  **(Consensus.)**

What the three do **not** buy is a diagnosis. They buy a partition, and the partition is what
makes the next, more expensive, investigations worth their cost — the ordering argument of
question m101 applied to a different presentation.

## The readout that is a proportion and not a quantity

Pulse oximetry reports the proportion of the available carrier that is carrying oxygen. It does
not report how much carrier there is, how fast it is moving, or whether what it is carrying is the
right thing. **(Mechanism.)** The quantity that matters to tissue is a product, and saturation is
one term in it.

Three consequences, all of them routine traps:

* Someone with too little carrier can show an entirely normal proportion while the absolute amount
  delivered is low. **(Consensus.)**
* Conventional pulse oximetry cannot distinguish a carrier occupied by carbon monoxide from one
  carrying oxygen, so the reading can be normal or high in carbon monoxide poisoning. This is a
  property of the measurement, not a malfunction. **(Consensus** that standard pulse oximetry is
  unreliable in this setting; which device and which test is used instead is **country-dependent**
  and institutional.**)**
* A metabolic cause of breathlessness can give a wholly normal saturation, because nothing about
  it is a failure of oxygen uptake.

This is question m006's distinction between a proportion and an absolute, with a different
numerator; and it is the same lesson as m001's summary number, which is honest about what it
summarises in a way its readers often are not.

## Where the drive itself is the finding

The rate is the most-recorded and least-interpreted number in the presentation, and its meaning
reverses depending on which way it is moving.

A fast rate with little apparent distress and an unremarkable chest shifts weight towards a
metabolic drive rather than a mechanical fault. A slowing rate in someone who was breathing fast
is not reassurance: it may be the point at which the work can no longer be sustained, and the same
number means opposite things before and after that point. **(Consensus** that a falling
respiratory rate in a previously tachypnoeic person is an ominous sign rather than an improving
one.**)** This is exactly question m034's argument — a normal-looking number can be an output
being defended — and it is why a single value without its trend is close to uninterpretable.

## Two producers at once is the normal case, not the complication

People have more than one organ. The lung disease and the heart disease coexist, and so do the
anaemia and the airway disease, and the presentation is then a sum to which both contribute. Two
things follow.

The first is that identifying one producer does not exclude another, and a partial response to a
treatment aimed at one is weak evidence about mechanism — the same non-exclusivity question m101
describes, where finding a benign generator establishes that it is present and nothing else.
**(Consensus** that response to treatment is a poor diagnostic argument.**)**

The second is that severity and mechanism have to be recorded as two separate things, because they
govern different decisions: severity governs how quickly, mechanism governs what. A record that
conflates them produces either a fast intervention aimed at the wrong link or the right one
arrived at too slowly.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Breathlessness is frightening in a way that is specific to it. Fear increases the drive, and the
increased drive increases the work, so the experience and the physiology are not separable and the
person is not being unhelpful by being frightened.

Three things follow and none is a technicality. A person's own report that this is worse than
their usual is primary data from the only vantage point inside the system, and it is frequently
the earliest signal available and frequently discounted; discounting it is a described failure
rather than a hypothetical one. **(Consensus** that patient-reported deterioration is
under-weighted and that recognition failure is a major source of avoidable harm.**)** A person who
has lived with a chronic lung or heart condition usually knows the shape of their own
deterioration better than a stranger assessing them for the first time, and that knowledge is an
observation that can be asked for. And a position someone has adopted and will not give up is
information obtained without asking for anything, which is a reason to notice it rather than to
correct it.

Nothing above is a protocol. No oxygen target, no rate, no threshold, no device setting, no drug
and no dose appears, because every one of those is local, is revised, and belongs to the reader's
own guidance. This answer is about why the list is drawn by system and what the cheapest
observations are worth; it is not a sequence of actions and it is not to be consulted while
assessing anyone.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument is stable: that the sensation is a mismatch, that the differential is
drawn by locus because treatment is specific to locus, that the first observations partition
rather than diagnose, that saturation is a proportion and not a quantity, and that a falling rate
can be deterioration are all long-standing teaching and follow from physiology rather than from
any single document. What is not stable is anything more specific. Oxygen targets and the
conditions attached to them have changed more than once and still differ between national
societies; the devices and confirmatory tests used where oximetry is unreliable are institutional;
and the wording and trigger points of observation charts vary by country and by hospital. Treat
this as an explanation of a principle dated October 2026, and read the current guidance from the
society and institution that govern where the reader actually works for anything beyond the
principle.
