# Shock is a statement about which term in a product failed, and the pressure is the product

Shock is inadequate delivery of oxygen to tissue relative to what tissue needs. It is defined at
the level of the cell, not at the level of a cuff, and that single definitional choice is what
forces the classification to be mechanistic. **(Definitional** — this is what the word names;
every account of the subject agrees on it even where the categories are drawn differently.**)**

The reason a pressure cannot carry the classification is arithmetic. Arterial pressure is the
product of flow and resistance; flow is the product of rate and the volume ejected per beat; the
volume ejected per beat depends on what filled the ventricle, on how well it squeezed, and on what
it had to push against. Pressure therefore sits at the far end of a chain of multiplications, and
a product is exactly the kind of quantity that is preserved when one factor falls and another
rises to meet it. A normal pressure says the product is normal. It says nothing whatever about the
factors, and it is the factors that name the problem and imply what would help. **(Mechanism** —
this follows from the relationships themselves and needs no document.**)**

## The chain, and where each category attacks it

```
   oxygen delivered   =   flow            ×   oxygen carried per unit of blood
                              │                        │
                              │                        └── anaemia, poor saturation,
                              │                            carriage that cannot unload
                              │
             flow   =   rate   ×   volume ejected per beat
                                        │
                      ┌─────────────────┼─────────────────┐
                      │                 │                 │
                  what filled       how well it       what it pushed
                  the ventricle     squeezed          against
                      │                 │                 │
   ────────────────────┼─────────────────┼─────────────────┼────────────────────────────
   hypovolaemic     the filling      intact            intact
   cardiogenic      intact           the squeeze       intact
   obstructive      blocked          intact            blocked
                    mechanically                      mechanically
   distributive     intact           intact            resistance lost, and the
                                                       distribution of flow fails
   ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   pressure  =  rate × volume per beat × resistance   ← the only term routinely measured
```

Read down the right-hand column and the point of the classification becomes visible. Each category
names a different broken factor; the categories are not degrees of the same thing. That matters
because what helps in one is, in the general case, unhelpful or harmful in another — the direction
of useful action differs by category, and it differs *because* the broken factor differs. This
answer deliberately states no management sequence; the point here is only that the category is
what determines the direction, which is the whole reason the category is worth getting right.

Distributive shock is the one that defeats a pressure-based classification most completely, and it
is worth being explicit about why. There, flow can be normal or above normal while delivery to
tissue fails, because the problem is in the distribution of that flow and in the capacity of
tissue to extract from it. A classification built on pressure, or even on flow, has no vocabulary
for a patient whose numbers are adequate and whose cells are not. **(Mechanism** for the failure
of extraction and distribution; the terminology for the subcategories is **country-dependent** and
has been revised.**)**

## Why the pressure is the worst available single variable

* **It is defended, so it moves late.** Arterial pressure is a regulated output with reflexes
  dedicated to holding it. By the time it has fallen, the compensation that was holding it has
  already been spent. This is question m034's argument applied to the one number most often
  treated as the answer.
* **It does not distinguish.** A single low pressure is consistent with every category in the
  table, and a single normal pressure is consistent with every category too. A classifier that
  returns the same value for all classes carries no information about class.
* **It is not the quantity of interest.** The quantity of interest is delivery to tissue. Pressure
  is a determinant of perfusion in some beds and not in others, and the relationship between the
  two is not fixed within a patient, let alone between patients.
* **It is normal in people who are unwell and low in people who are well.** A habitually low
  pressure is unremarkable in some people; a pressure that is normal for the population may be
  profoundly low for someone whose usual pressure is much higher. The informative quantity is the
  displacement from that person's own baseline, which is the one thing a single reading cannot
  give. **(Consensus.)**

## Where the information actually is

Because the measured term is the product, the readable signals are the ones that reveal the
factors — and almost all of them are clinical rather than numerical.

* **The effectors, not the output.** The work being done to hold the product: the rate, the
  pattern of breathing, the state of the periphery. These move before the defended variable does.
* **Gradients within the patient.** A warm centre and a cold periphery, or the reverse, says
  something about where resistance is and where supply has been withdrawn from. The categories
  differ in this far more reliably than they differ in pressure.
* **Evidence of the consequence.** Markers of tissue that is not receiving enough — the state of
  consciousness, urine output over time, and the laboratory evidence of anaerobic metabolism —
  speak to delivery rather than to pressure. **(Consensus** that these are the quantities
  monitored; the thresholds attached to them are **country-dependent** and are revised, so none is
  stated here.**)**
* **The trajectory.** A factor that is failing produces a moving picture. Any of the above,
  repeated, carries more than all of them once.
* **The history and the context.** The prior probability over categories is set long before any
  measurement: what happened, over what period, to whom, with what already wrong. This is the same
  reasoning as question m068's.

## Why categories are a working hypothesis and not a label

Three qualifications, all of which follow from the mechanism rather than from caution.

**Shock is frequently mixed.** More than one factor can fail at once, and in a sick patient
usually does: a failing pump raises filling pressures and reduces renal perfusion, which changes
volume; prolonged poor perfusion impairs the squeeze. The categories are not mutually exclusive
bins. They are a set of hypotheses to hold simultaneously, weighted by context.

**The category can change under you.** A mechanism that was mechanical can become distributive as
tissue is injured, and a problem that began as volume can end as a pump problem. So the question
is asked repeatedly, and the answer is allowed to change without that being an error.

**A category is a commitment to a direction, which is why it is revisited.** If acting on a
category does not produce the response the category predicts, that failure is information about
the category, not only about severity. Building that reassessment in is the reason these are
taught as mechanisms rather than as labels: a label has nothing to be falsified, and a mechanism
does.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is about a quantity. What the quantity is for is a person whose tissue is not
getting what it needs, and who will frequently look, for a while, like someone who is coping.

The clinically important consequence is that reassurance from a normal pressure is the single most
expensive mistake available in this subject. The pressure is held by work the patient is doing,
and the people who hold it best — those with the most reserve — are the people whose collapse,
when the work can no longer be done, is the most abrupt. The reverse error is real too, and
quieter: a pressure that reads low in someone who is warm, alert and perfusing well may be that
person's ordinary pressure, and treating the number rather than the person carries its own harm.
**(Mechanism** for both; **consensus** that unrecognised shock and delayed recognition of it are a
major and studied source of avoidable harm.**)**

None of this is a reason for a reader to form a view about any particular person, including
themselves. It is an account of why a classification is drawn the way it is, written for someone
who already has the training to use it, and the category names in it are not a diagnostic scheme
that can be applied from a reading of this page. Anyone who is unwell, or worried about someone
who is, needs the local emergency number rather than this answer.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Define shock without mentioning blood pressure. Why is that definition the one that forces the
  classification?
* Write out the chain of terms, and say which term each category breaks.
* Why can distributive shock present with a normal or high flow, and what does that do to a
  pressure-based classification?
* Why is a normal pressure uninformative about category, and why is a low one no better?
* Why is mixed shock the expectation rather than the exception, and what does that imply about
  acting on a category?
* What would make you abandon a category you had committed to?

## Scope and safety

**If someone is unwell right now, call your local emergency number.** This is not for use during
an emergency, and reading this instead of calling for help would be worse than doing nothing at
all.

This is revision material about *why a classification has the categories it has*, written for
someone already trained. It is deliberately not a protocol and not a decision aid: it contains no
management sequence, no thresholds, no doses, no rates, no volumes and no settings, and it is not
something to consult while acting. The definitions and terminology of the shock categories —
particularly the distributive group — differ between countries and between institutions and have
been revised more than once. The reader's own national guidance and local policy are the
authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here describes any real person
or any real case.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanistic classification is stable and is not controversial: shock has been taught as a
failure of one or more identifiable determinants of oxygen delivery for decades, and the four
broad categories are mainstream across standard texts. What is not stable is the vocabulary and
the boundaries. The naming and subdivision of the distributive group, the definitions that attach
to sepsis and septic shock, the markers and thresholds used to recognise inadequate perfusion, and
the degree of emphasis placed on each monitored quantity have all moved within living memory and
will move again. Treat this as an explanation of a principle dated October 2026, and read the
current document from the national body and the local policy that govern where the reader actually
works for anything beyond the principle.
