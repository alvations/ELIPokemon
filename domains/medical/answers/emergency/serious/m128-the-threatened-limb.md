# The clock is the disease, and the examination has no units until the other side supplies them

A limb whose perfusion has failed is unusual among emergency presentations in that almost
everything about it is settled by one variable. The mechanism matters for what is done about it.
The anatomy matters for where. But the quantity that decides what there is left to save is
**elapsed time since perfusion failed**, and it is running before anyone has been told about it.

That has two consequences which look unrelated and are the same consequence twice.

The first is that every step which does not shorten the interval is, in this presentation, a cost
with no offsetting benefit — including steps which in almost every other presentation would be
good practice. The second is that the examination has to be read against the other limb, because
almost none of its findings carry information in absolute terms, and the comparison is the only
thing that converts an observation into a measurement.

## What is actually on the clock, and why it is not one clock

Tissues distal to an occlusion do not fail together. They fail in an order set by their metabolic
demand and their tolerance of anaerobic conditions, and that order is why the examination changes
character as the hours pass rather than simply getting worse.

```
   time since perfusion failed ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────►

   nerve          ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
                  ↑ least tolerant. Sensory fibres before motor.
                    So the EARLIEST objective sign is a change in sensation,
                    and the earliest MOTOR sign already means later.

   muscle                   ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
                             ↑ intermediate. Once this has gone, restoring flow
                               introduces a SECOND problem (the washout of the
                               products of necrotic muscle) rather than only solving
                               the first.

   skin, bone, fat                        ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓
                                           ↑ most tolerant. Intact skin is therefore
                                             NOT evidence that the limb is viable.

   the pulse       ──── absent from minute one, or present until very late,
                        depending ENTIRELY on where the occlusion is and what
                        collateral exists. It is not a clock at all.
```

Three readings of that diagram, each doing work.

* **The order of failure is the order of the signs, so the sign tells you roughly where on the
  clock you are.** A limb with altered sensation and preserved power is at a different point from
  one with neither, and *the second is not a more severe version of the first; it is a later one*.
  (**Mechanism.**)
* **The most reassuring-looking tissue is the most tolerant one.** Skin stays acceptable long
  after nerve and muscle have not. An unremarkable-looking limb is therefore a weak negative in
  the same structural way that a soft abdomen is, which is question m126's argument.
  (**Mechanism.**)
* **Restoring flow is not monotonically good once muscle has died.** Reperfusion of substantially
  infarcted muscle produces systemic consequences of its own, and compartment pressure can rise
  after flow returns. So the decision is not *restore flow as soon as possible, always*; it is a
  decision whose right answer depends on where on the clock the limb already is. (**Consensus.**)

## Why the examination is comparative, and what the comparison is for

Take the findings one at a time and ask what each means on its own.

| Finding | What it means in isolation | What the other side supplies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature of the limb | almost nothing: ambient temperature, the person's circulation and the room all move it | a within-person control measured in the same room at the same minute, so the *difference* and the *level at which the difference begins* are both readable |
| Colour | highly variable between people, and the standard descriptions fail in darker skin — question m018's argument | the same person's own baseline pigmentation and perfusion |
| Capillary refill | variable with age, ambient temperature and site | a simultaneous paired reading |
| Sensation | a report, which needs a reference the person can give | the person's own other side, which is the reference they can actually use |
| Power | depends on baseline strength, pre-existing disease and effort | the person's own usual asymmetry, which is frequently not zero |
| Pulses | present or absent is crude; the useful observation is which level and whether it matches | the contralateral level, which converts presence into a *pattern* |

The pattern in that right-hand column is the whole point. **These findings are differences, not
quantities.** The examination of a limb is a paired measurement in which the contralateral side is
the control, and the control is unusually good: same person, same circulation, same ambient
temperature, same minute, same observer. Almost no other clinical comparison is that well matched.
(**Mechanism.**)

Which means the failure mode is predictable. **When the comparison is unavailable, the examination
loses most of its power and the reader frequently does not notice.** Bilateral disease, a previous
amputation, a prosthesis, pre-existing neuropathy, a previous stroke with residual weakness,
longstanding peripheral arterial disease on both sides, a limb already in a cast: in each of these
the paired reading is gone and what remains is an absolute reading of a quantity that was never
meaningful in absolute terms. The correct response is to say so explicitly and to go looking for a
different reference — the person's own account of their usual state, a previous examination, a
previous record of pulses — rather than to report the absolute number as though it were the paired
one.

## Why the history carries the one thing the examination cannot

The examination can tell you roughly where on the clock the limb is. It cannot tell you **when the
clock started**, and that is the variable everything depends on.

* **Onset is the single most important thing anyone will say about this.** Sudden against gradual;
  a specific moment the person can name against *it has been getting worse for weeks*. These point
  at different mechanisms and, crucially, at different starting times for the same examination
  findings. (**Consensus.**)
* **A gradual onset can mean the collateral supply has had time to develop**, which is why an
  identical examination can be compatible with a limb that has hours and one that has had a
  chronically poor supply for years. The examination alone does not separate those.
  (**Mechanism.**)
* **Somebody else's account frequently supplies the time.** Who last saw the person moving
  normally, when the person last stood, when the pain started relative to something datable like a
  meal or a television programme. This is the same reasoning as question m068's mechanism of
  injury: the information is in the history, it has to be collected at the point where it still
  exists, and it has to be handed on, because the next person cannot recover it.
* **A recorded time, not a recorded duration.** *Four hours* written down at eleven o'clock
  becomes useless by one o'clock, and is routinely re-read as though it were still current. A
  clock time survives being re-read; an interval does not. (**Mechanism.**)

## What follows for how the episode is run

Nothing in this section is a protocol, and nothing in it names an agent, a dose, a technique, a
scoring system or a threshold; all of those are local and revised, and the reader's own guidance
is the authority. What can be said is structural.

* **Parallel rather than serial.** When the dominant cost is the interval, the steps that can be
  done at the same time should be. Assessment, the referral conversation, and preparation for
  whatever comes next do not have to queue behind one another, and a sequential workflow spends
  the only resource that matters. (**Consensus.**)
* **The referral is early and provisional.** The clinician who will act on this needs to know now
  and be updated, rather than to be told a complete story later. Which specialty receives it, how
  it is contacted and what the expected response time is are all (**country-dependent**) and
  institutional.
* **Investigation competes with the clock.** Imaging answers questions about anatomy that
  genuinely change what is done, and it also costs time; the trade-off is real and its resolution
  is local. The general argument about a test with its own harm is question m104's.
* **The thing most often lost is the time of onset.** It is the one piece of information no later
  examination can regenerate, and it is routinely absent from the record of a transfer. Question
  m070 is the argument about what crosses a handover boundary and what does not; this is the
  specific field whose loss is least recoverable here.

## The human stakes, said plainly

The subjective part of this presentation is the part the person will remember, and it is handled
in plain prose here because it should not be made into a mechanism.

Someone with a limb that has lost its blood supply is usually in severe pain, and the pain can be
out of proportion to anything visible. That has two practical consequences and one ethical one.
The practical ones are that the pain is itself information — a disproportionate, poorly localised,
unrelenting pain is a finding, and so is pain on passive movement — and that pain unaddressed
makes the rest of the examination worse, because the comparison the whole assessment depends on
cannot be performed properly on someone who cannot tolerate being touched. The ethical one is that
*because time is the dominant cost* there is a standing temptation to skip the part of care that
does not shorten the interval, and comfort is the usual casualty. Nothing in the clock argument
justifies that, and the specific management of pain is deliberately not described in this answer:
it is agent-specific, dose-specific and local, and it belongs in the reader's own guidance.

Two more things, said plainly because they are usually said badly or not at all.

A person being assessed for this has frequently already worked out what the possible outcomes are,
and they will be trying to read the room. Honest, specific, non-promissory information — what is
being done now, who has been called, what the next decision point is and when — is what they can
actually use. Nothing about outcome or prognosis is described anywhere in this answer, which is
deliberate and is a matter of policy for this directory and not an oversight.

And the account of time usually belongs to someone who is not the patient: whoever found them,
whoever saw them last, whoever they were with. That person is frequently frightened and frequently
blaming themselves for not having noticed sooner. They are also holding the single most valuable
piece of information in the whole encounter, and they should be asked carefully rather than
hurriedly.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Name the variable that decides what there is left to save, and say what that does to every step
  in the workup.
* Tissues distal to an occlusion do not fail together. Give the order and say what sets it.
* Why is a change in sensation an earlier sign than a change in power, and what does that mean
  about the clock?
* Why is intact-looking skin a weak negative here?
* Why is restoring flow not monotonically good?
* Take four findings and say what each means in isolation. Then say what the other limb supplies.
* Give five situations in which the paired comparison is unavailable, and say what should be done
  instead.
* Why does the examination not establish when the clock started, and who usually does?
* Why is a recorded clock time better than a recorded duration?
* Which single field is least recoverable across a handover in this presentation?

## Scope and safety

**If someone is unwell right now, call your local emergency number.** This is not for use during
an emergency, and reading this instead of calling for help would be worse than doing nothing at
all.

This is revision material about *why one variable dominates a presentation and why an examination
is a paired measurement*, written for someone already trained. It is deliberately not a protocol
and not a decision aid: it names no classification system, no score, no threshold, no time window,
no imaging pathway, no technique, no agent and no dose, and it is not something to consult while
assessing anyone. The management of pain is not described, and nothing about outcome or prognosis
appears anywhere, both deliberately. Referral routes, response-time expectations, imaging
availability and surgical arrangements differ sharply between countries and institutions. The
reader's own national guidance and local policy are the authority; this is not, and it has had no
clinical review. Nothing here describes any real person or any real case.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanism is stable and the numbers are not. That nerve is less tolerant of ischaemia than
muscle and muscle less than skin, that sensory involvement precedes motor, that the order of
tissue failure is therefore the order of the signs and so indicates position on the clock, that
intact skin is consequently a weak negative, that reperfusion of infarcted muscle introduces a
second problem, that the pulse examination reflects the level of occlusion and the collateral
supply rather than elapsed time, and that the contralateral limb is an unusually well matched
control are all long-standing teaching and follow from anatomy and physiology rather than from any
single document. What moves is everything quantitative and everything procedural: the time windows
quoted for salvage differ between sources and are still argued about, the classification systems
in use differ between societies and are revised, imaging recommendations move with availability
and with technique, and referral routes, response-time expectations and who performs what are
institutional and change with each reorganisation. Treat this as an explanation of a principle
dated October 2026, and read the current guidance from the vascular society, the college and the
institution that govern where the reader actually works for anything beyond the principle.
