# A barrier does not reduce need, it reduces the part of need you can see

Three quantities get called demand and they are not the same thing (**definitional**). **Need** is
what would benefit a person if it were delivered. **Demand** is what is asked for. **Utilisation**
is what is actually provided. An access system sits between the first and the second, and
everything it does happens there: it changes what is asked for and what is delivered, and it
changes need not at all. A door that is hard to open produces a service with less recorded demand
and exactly as much disease behind it.

Load-bearing claims below are marked with what they rest on: **mechanism**, **definitional**,
(**consensus**) or (**country-dependent**). m050 lists access as one of six mechanisms of the
inverse care law; this answer is about the demand side of the same door, and the equity gradient
is taken from there rather than re-argued here.

## What a hard door does, mechanism by mechanism

A system entered by telephone at a fixed hour, by an online form, by a digital identity check, by
an appointment several weeks ahead, by attendance in working hours, or by a payment at the point
of use, rations on the ability to operate that system. The filter is real, it is reproducible, and
it sorts on literacy, language, confidence, flexibility of employment, transport, childcare, data
access and previous experience of being taken seriously — none of which is clinical, and all of
which are distributed in the same direction as need (**consensus**).

Four consequences, each with a sign:

1. **Displacement, not reduction.** Need that cannot enter here enters somewhere else — urgent
   care, an emergency department, an out-of-hours service — or it enters later, in a worse state,
   through a door that cannot be closed (**consensus**).
2. **The opportunistic contact disappears.** A large amount of useful work in first-contact care
   happens because somebody came in for something else. Raise the cost of coming in and that
   work stops happening, silently.
3. **The measurement illusion.** Recorded demand falls, so the service's own data says need fell.
   A practice list with a hard door looks like a healthier population than the identical list with
   an easy one.
4. **The filter is undeclared.** Nobody wrote down that the criterion was persistence at 8 a.m.
   An undeclared filter cannot be audited, compared or argued with — the single strongest reason
   for stating an access rule explicitly even when the rule is crude.

An easy door converts unmet need into expressed demand, which is the intended effect and is also
the uncomfortable one: measured demand rises immediately while capacity does not. Part of that
rise is need that was always there and was previously absorbed by the patient, and part of it is
contact for problems that would have resolved unaided. Those two parts look identical in the
appointment book. How much total demand expands when a door is opened, and whether it settles, is
an empirical question answered differently in different systems and still argued about
*[consensus that it rises, magnitude and persistence contested]*.

The structural gain is real and is usually understated: when the door is open, the sorting is done
*inside* the system by somebody with clinical information, instead of outside it by whoever can
hold on longest. That turns rationing from an endurance test into a clinical act, which can be
trained, audited and improved.

## The queue is a sort, and the key is a design choice

Every system with more demand than capacity sorts. The only question is on what.

* **First come, first served** sorts on availability and speed of response — a proxy with no
  clinical content, and one that correlates with flexible employment and spare time.
* **Clinical triage** sorts on assessed need, which requires an assessment of everybody,
  including the large majority who need nothing further. The assessment is not free and is itself
  rationed.
* **Most real systems run brackets.** A small number of categories, sorted by urgency, with
  arrival time deciding order within a category. This is the primary-survey structure m031 and
  m033 describe: the bracket is read first, and nothing you do inside a bracket moves anything
  across one (**mechanism**).

Two trade-offs are genuine and neither has a free answer. Same-day access and continuity compete
for the same appointments, and m015 is the argument for why losing continuity is a clinical loss
and not an inconvenience. And access competes with length: more contacts from the same capacity
means shorter contacts, which is exactly the resource that complexity needs.

## The human stakes, said plainly

The person who cannot get in does not appear in any of the service's data, and the service will
therefore not experience them as a problem. That is the whole difficulty: the failure is
invisible from inside, and it is visible from outside only as a worse presentation somewhere else,
months later, attributed to something other than the door.

In the other direction, a service that opens its door and does not get the capacity to match
degrades for everyone, including the people the opening was meant to reach — and the first thing
that degrades is usually the time available per person, which is the thing that matters most to
whoever has the most wrong with them. Neither error is a moral failing of the people working in
it. Both are properties of a design, which is why they are worth designing rather than
apologising for.

And the honest sentence that belongs in the same place: the person waiting on hold is not
necessarily the person with the most need, and everybody in the system knows it.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate separates need, demand and utilisation without being prompted, and then
says which of the three their own practice measures. Then what their access system filters on,
stated as an attribute rather than a process. Then where the displaced demand goes, and how the
practice would know. Then the sort key: what the brackets are, who assigns them, and what
information that person holds. Then the trade-off against continuity, with a position taken. Then
the hard one — what a practice could measure to detect the need that is not arriving at all.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about how an access system behaves, written for someone already
training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and
not for use in making a decision about any person's care. No access standard, waiting-time target
or triage category is named here on purpose: all of them are local, several are contractual, and
all are revised. Nothing here describes how any individual should seek care. If someone is unwell
right now, the relevant action is to contact local urgent care or the local emergency number, not
to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural claims — that a barrier changes demand and utilisation but not need, that every
access system filters on some attribute, and that an open door moves the sorting inside — are
mainstream and stable. What moves is everything operational: access standards, contractual
targets, the balance between telephone, online and in-person entry, and the evidence on what
opening a door does to total workload. Digital-first access has been introduced, evaluated and
partially reversed in several systems over the last decade, and the evidence on its equity
effects is arriving rather than settled. Take access standards and definitions from current local
requirements rather than from here, as of October 2026.
