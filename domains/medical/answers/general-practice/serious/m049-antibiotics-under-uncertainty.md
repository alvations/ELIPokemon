# One decision, two correct answers, and they do not agree

Most prescribing dilemmas dissolve once you have the right information. This one does not. Even
with perfect knowledge of the individual in front of you, the decision that maximises expected
benefit *for that person* and the decision that maximises expected benefit *across the population
over time* can point in opposite directions, because the benefit is private and a substantial part
of the cost is shared. That is not a failure of reasoning or of character. It is the structure of
the problem, and recognising it as structural is the first useful step *[mechanism, not
guideline]*.

The shared cost is selection pressure. Each course of an antimicrobial selects for organisms that
survive it, in the person treated and in their environment, and the resulting resistant
population is available to everybody afterwards — including, later, the same person. The benefit
lands on one identifiable individual now; the cost lands on an unidentifiable set of people later.
Every feature of the dilemma follows from that asymmetry.

## Why the externality cannot be priced in the room

This is the same structural problem as overdiagnosis, and recognising the shape is worth more than
memorising either case. No individual resistant infection can be traced to the course that caused
it; the harm exists as a rate across a population, while the decision exists as an instance in
front of one person. So:

* **The clinician sees one harm vividly and the other not at all.** The person who was not treated
  and deteriorated is a specific, memorable, attributable event, often with a complaint attached.
  The person who acquires a resistant organism years later is a statistic that names nobody. These
  two feed back on prescribing behaviour with wildly different strength, and the asymmetry pushes
  in one direction.
* **Exhortation is the wrong instrument for an externality.** Telling an individual decision-maker
  to internalise a cost they cannot observe, attribute or measure does not work reliably and is
  not a design. Population-level instruments exist because the population-level cost has to be
  carried at population level *[consensus]*.
* **The replenishment is slow and the depletion is fast.** New agents arrive over a timescale of
  years to decades, through a development pipeline that has repeatedly been described as
  insufficient relative to the rate of loss; resistance accumulates continuously. The asymmetry
  between those two rates is why the resource behaves like a finite stock rather than a renewable
  flow *[consensus]*.

## What narrows the gap, in order of how much it buys

Four instruments, and they are not interchangeable:

1. **Anything that moves the prior.** A test, a finding, or a scoring rule that genuinely
   separates the people who will benefit from those who will not reduces the conflict rather than
   trading one harm for another. This is the only lever that improves both ledgers at once, which
   is why it dominates the list.
2. **Duration and spectrum.** Shorter courses and narrower agents reduce selection pressure while
   leaving most of the individual benefit intact, so they move the population ledger at small
   individual cost. Specific durations are condition-specific, actively revised, and genuinely
   contested in several areas *[country-dependent]*.
3. **Converting the decision into a conditional one.** A deferred or back-up prescription, with a
   named trigger, moves the decision to the point where more information exists. It is the
   safety-net applied to prescribing, and it inherits the safety-net's weakness: it assumes the
   person can act on the trigger.
4. **Instruments that operate above the individual decision.** Formulary restriction, reserved
   agents, audit with feedback, and prescribing data made visible. These work by removing the
   externality from the individual's hands rather than by asking them to weigh it.

## The counterweight, stated as strongly as the argument

The case above is not an argument for prescribing less. Under-treatment is a real harm with real
victims, and a policy expressed as a volume target rather than as a rule about cases will produce
them. The population argument licenses a *better rule about which people*; it does not license a
quota, because a quota pushes the harm onto whoever presents once the quota is spent, and who
presents last is not random.

Two further honesty requirements. Withholding with a safety-net assumes a person who can return,
which is not every person, and stewardship that ignores that assumption redistributes risk toward
the people least able to carry it. And resistance patterns are local: the population ledger is not
the same ledger everywhere, so the balance point genuinely differs by country, by region and by
setting *[country-dependent]*.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Two people stand behind this decision and only one of them is in the room. The person present has
a reasonable question — they feel unwell, there is a treatment, and they are being told the
reasons for not using it involve people they will never meet. Treating that as unreasonable, or as
something to be managed with a leaflet, is both unkind and ineffective. The honest version of the
conversation includes the part that is genuinely uncomfortable: that the clinician is weighing a
cost the person is not being asked to bear, and that this is why the decision does not feel like
it is entirely about them.

The other person is real too. A resistant infection in someone who has run out of options is not
an abstraction, and it arrives at the end of a chain in which every individual link was
defensible. Nobody in that chain did anything they could be blamed for, which is precisely why
the failure has to be designed against rather than moralised about.

And the part that is easy to leave out of a stewardship argument. The person who was not treated,
followed the advice, got worse, and came back — or did not come back — has been harmed by a
correct decision. Holding that alongside the population case, without softening either, is the
actual skill. It is also why the conversation at the point of not prescribing has to leave the
door genuinely open rather than closing it politely.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate can state why the two optima differ in structural terms, rather than
asserting that resistance is a problem. Then the attribution point: why the cost cannot be
assigned to any single course, and what that implies about relying on individual judgement. Then
the four instruments, ranked, with a reason for the ranking. Then the counterweight, offered
unprompted. Then the hard one: how a deferred prescription behaves differently in a person who can
easily come back and one who cannot, and what the policy should do about that difference.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about the structure of a prescribing decision, written for someone
already training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision
aid, and nothing here should inform what any individual is given or not given — that belongs with
the prescriber who has examined them and with local microbiology guidance. No antibiotic, class,
dose, duration, spectrum, scoring rule, prevalence or resistance rate is named here on purpose,
because all of them are local, several are contested, and all are revised. The local formulary and
the local antimicrobial guidance are the authority and must be checked before anything reaches a
patient. If someone is unwell right now, the relevant action is to contact local urgent care or
the local emergency number, not to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural claim — that the individual and population optima diverge because the benefit is
private and part of the cost is shared and unattributable — is settled and will not move. What
moves continuously is everything operational: resistance rates, which agents are classified as
reserved, recommended durations for specific infections, and which near-patient tests are
available and funded in primary care. Short-course regimens have displaced longer ones in several
indications over the last decade and that process is ongoing in both directions. Near-patient
testing and host-response markers at first contact are the area where the lever that improves both
ledgers would come from, and the evidence on them is arriving rather than settled. Take agents,
durations and local resistance data from current local guidance rather than from here, as of
October 2026.
