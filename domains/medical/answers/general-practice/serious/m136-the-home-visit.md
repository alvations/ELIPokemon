# A consulting room is a controlled environment, and control is the thing a home takes away and gives back

Three things change when an assessment happens in somebody's house, and they do not change in the
same direction. The information available goes **up**, mostly in categories a history cannot
supply. The resources available go **down**, to whatever was carried through the door. And the
social frame of the encounter inverts, because the clinician is now a guest in a space the other
person controls. A visit assessed as "the same consultation, somewhere less convenient" misses all
three (**mechanism**).

Load-bearing claims below are marked with what they rest on: **mechanism**, **definitional**,
(**consensus**) or (**country-dependent**). Who does home visits, when, with what equipment and
under what contractual obligation differs more between health systems than almost anything else in
this specialty, so the structure here is deliberately separated from the arrangements.

## The information that only exists in the house

A consulting room is a standardised environment, and standardisation is what makes findings
comparable. It also strips out everything about the setting the person actually lives in — which,
for a great deal of primary care, *is* the clinical question (**mechanism**).

What the house supplies, none of which has to be asked for:

* **The layout against the function.** Stairs, where the bathroom is, whether the bed is in the
  living room, what the chair is like to get out of, how far it is from the chair to the toilet.
  Function assessed on a flat floor with a hand rail is function in a different building. Nursing
  m110 makes the same point from the ward side — that an assessment against a corridor is not an
  assessment against a home — and a visit is the one time that gap closes.
* **The medicines as objects rather than as a list.** What is actually in the house, in what
  quantity, in how many duplicate boxes, from how many prescribers, and whether the dates on them
  are consistent with the list on the screen. Discrepancies between the prescribed list and what
  is actually in the house are among the most frequently reported findings of a home medicines
  review, and they are invisible from the consulting room (**consensus**). m014 is the argument
  about what the count itself costs.
* **The other people, and what they are doing.** Who else lives there, who is providing care, how
  tired they look, whether they are themselves a patient. A carer's capacity is a clinical
  variable and it is one of the terms in every plan made at home.
* **Food, warmth, continence, and hazard.** An empty fridge, an unheated room, a smell, a trip
  hazard, a stair with no rail. None of these is a diagnosis. All of them change what a plan can
  assume.
* **The evidence of trajectory.** Unopened post, uncashed prescriptions, a meal from several days
  ago, a fallen picture nobody picked up. The house carries a record of the last few weeks that
  nobody wrote down, and in a presentation where the key question is *change from baseline*, that
  record is the closest thing available to a baseline. m114 is the argument about who can supply
  that information; a visit is the other route to it.

And one population fact that is easy to miss. The group who are seen at home is not a random
sample of the list: they are, by selection, older, frailer, more multimorbid and less able to
leave the house. m011's argument therefore applies at full strength — the same finding carries a
different weight in this group than in the waiting room, because the prior is different
(**mechanism**). The visit changes both the information and the prevalence against which it is
read.

The practical consequence is that a home assessment is weighted towards the things that can be
done with hands, eyes, ears and a conversation, and away from the things that need a machine. That
is not a lesser assessment. It is a different distribution of evidence, and it rewards exactly the
skills — the history, the general appearance, the trajectory, the comparison with how someone was
— that a test-rich environment lets a clinician lean on less (**consensus**).

It also changes the *shape* of the decision. In a consulting room the usual output is a diagnosis
and a plan. At home, the live question is frequently whether the person can be looked after where
they are: what would have to be true for that to be safe, who would have to do it, and what the
route is if it stops working. That is the discharge question of nursing m110 asked in the other
direction, before anyone has been admitted — and the safety-net is the part most often left thin,
because the clinician is about to leave a building they cannot see into afterwards. m012 is the
full argument about what separates a net that works from reassurance.

## The frame inverts, and that is clinically useful

In a consulting room the clinician controls the space, the time, the seating and the ending. In a
house, none of those. The person can refuse entry, can decide which room is used, can keep a
family member present or send them out, and can end the encounter. The usual asymmetry of the
consultation is reduced.

That is largely a gain. People say more in their own kitchen; the account is less compressed; what
matters to them is in the room rather than described. Several things that are difficult to elicit
in ten minutes in a clinic simply present themselves.

It also removes structures that exist for good reasons. Getting a person alone is harder, and
whether someone *can* be seen alone is itself a finding. Examination happens in whatever position
and light the room allows, which lowers the quality of some findings and should be recorded as
such rather than silently. And the usual escalation — ask a colleague, order a test, review this
afternoon — is not available at the moment it is wanted.

Two things that arise in houses are deliberately not treated here. Where a visit raises a concern
about someone's safety, or about whether they are able to make a particular decision, those are
governed by law and by local procedure rather than by clinical reasoning, they differ by
jurisdiction, and they are out of scope for this answer (**country-dependent**). The scope note
says so again.

## What makes a visit a clinical act rather than a courtesy

The same test as m046 applies: a visit is a clinical act when its output is a decision that could
not have been made without it, and administrative when the output was already determined before
the door opened. Three things carry most of that weight.

**Deciding the visit is itself triage.** A request for a visit is a triage decision made on
someone else's information, usually by telephone, often from a third party. m112's argument
applies: the decision does not disappear by being made remotely, it moves earlier, to the point of
least information.

**Writing down what the setting changed.** A record that says what the house was like, who was
there, what the medicines looked like and what the plan assumes is the only way the next clinician
inherits any of it. Nursing m073 is the argument for why the absence of that record will be read
as the absence of the finding.

**Naming who holds the net.** The clinician leaves. Whoever is in the house, or whoever answers
the telephone next, inherits the plan. A visit that ends without a named person, a named change to
look for and a route that answers has done the assessment and skipped the part that makes it safe
(**consensus**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

Being assessed at home is not a neutral experience. For some people it is a relief — not having to
get dressed, get into a car, or be helped into a building. For others it is an intrusion at the
worst possible moment, in a house they are ashamed of, in front of a relative they did not want to
involve. Both reactions are common and neither is wrong.

The thing worth holding onto is that what the clinician sees is a home, and a home is not a
presentation. An untidy kitchen is not neglect. A cold house may be a choice, a tariff, or an
inability to pay, and those are different problems with different answers. Reading poverty as
decline, or decline as poverty, are both errors, and both are made quickly and silently. The cost
of getting it wrong falls on someone who has just let a stranger into the only place they control.

And the asymmetry of the ending matters. The clinician leaves and the day continues. The person
stays in the situation that was just assessed, with whatever was arranged, and usually with less
certainty than the plan implies. Several of the decisions made in houses are about where someone
will live, and those are among the most consequential conversations in the whole of primary care.
They are not improved by being conducted quickly, standing up, in a hallway.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate names all three changes rather than only the loss of equipment, and gives
the direction of each. Then what the house supplies that a history cannot, with the
medicines-as-objects finding offered unprompted. Then the selection argument: that the visited
population is not the listed population, and what m011's reasoning does to a finding read in it.
Then the decision actually in play, with conveyance distinguished from diagnosis. Then the record:
what about the setting has to be written down for it to survive, and why. Then the net, with a
named holder. Then the hard one — how a practice would decide which requests warrant a visit,
given that the request is triaged on the least information anyone will have.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about the structure of an assessment carried out in someone's home,
written for someone already training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and nothing here should inform a decision about whether any
individual is visited, assessed, moved or admitted. No equipment list, visiting criterion,
interval or threshold appears here on purpose: all are local, several are contractual and all are
revised. **Safeguarding and mental capacity are deliberately out of scope** — both are governed by
legislation and local procedure that differ by jurisdiction, and neither can be reasoned out from
first principles. If someone is unwell right now, the relevant action is to contact local urgent
care or the local emergency number, not to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural claims — that information rises and capability falls, that the visited population
is selected rather than representative, that the live decision is often about where care happens
rather than what the diagnosis is, and that the net has to have a named holder — are mechanism and
consensus, and they will not move. What moves is everything about the arrangements: who visits,
whether visiting is contractual, whether it is done by a doctor or by another member of the team,
what portable testing exists, and what hospital-at-home capacity sits behind it. Portable
diagnostics and remote monitoring have expanded what can be carried through a door, and that
expansion is ongoing rather than settled. Take the arrangements from current local guidance rather
than from here, as of October 2026.
