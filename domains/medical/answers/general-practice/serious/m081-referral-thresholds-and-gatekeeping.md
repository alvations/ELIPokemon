# A referral threshold is set by the two error costs, not by how certain anyone feels

The question answered in the room is not *is this serious*. It is *is the probability now high
enough that referring is the better of two imperfect acts*. Those are different questions, and
only the second one can be answered. The first invites a search for certainty that first-contact
care cannot supply; the second is a comparison between two expected harms, and a comparison can
be made under uncertainty *[mechanism, not guideline]*.

Load-bearing claims below are marked with what they rest on: *mechanism*, *definitional*,
*consensus* or *country-dependent*. A claim marked *mechanism* is checkable by reasoning. A claim
marked *country-dependent* needs the reader's own guidance, not the writer's.

The threshold itself falls out of the comparison. Expected harm of referring rises with the
proportion of people referred who do not have the disease; expected harm of not referring rises
with the proportion who do. Set them equal and the probability at which they cross depends on the
*ratio* of the two harms and on nothing else — not on prevalence, not on the strength of a
feeling, not on the clinician's experience *[mechanism]*. Prevalence decides where a given person
sits relative to the threshold. It does not move the threshold.

Two consequences follow immediately. The threshold differs between conditions, because the ratio
differs. And it differs between health systems, because the downstream pathway differs — the same
probability, the same person, can be above the threshold in one country and below it in another
without either clinician being wrong *[country-dependent]*.

## What raising a threshold does, and what it does not do

Raising a threshold always reduces the number referred. Whether it reduces the number of cases
found depends entirely on one thing: whether the region the criterion now excludes contains
cases.

* **A criterion whose excluded region holds no cases is free.** Yield rises, referrals fall,
  nothing is missed. This is the ideal and it is rarer than it sounds.
* **A criterion that cuts across the distribution of cases loses them systematically, not
  randomly.** The same subgroup is excluded every time: the ones who present atypically, early,
  below an age cut-off, without the sentinel feature. A threshold does not lose a random sample
  *[mechanism]*.

That second point is the whole reason a threshold needs a safety-net behind it rather than
confidence in front of it, which is m012's subject and is not re-argued here.

## What the gatekeeper is optimising, which is four things at once

1. **Sequence.** Which test first, and whether this problem needs the expensive instrument now or
   the cheap instrument twice.
2. **The downstream clinic's operating point.** Referral enriches the population the specialist
   sees, which is what makes the specialist's tests perform as published. Loosen the criteria and
   the downstream predictive values fall, through no change in anybody's technique — m011's
   argument, read from the other end.
3. **Residual risk, held rather than transferred.** Not referring is a decision to carry the
   probability, with a plan attached. Referring does not discharge it either, because a wait is a
   period during which somebody has to be watching.
4. **A finite queue.** This is the part that is uncomfortable to say and dishonest to omit. The
   urgent route has capacity; using it for a low-probability case lengthens the wait for a
   higher-probability one. The gatekeeper is not only assessing a person — they are allocating,
   and the people on the other side of the allocation are not in the room *[mechanism]*.

## Urgency and probability are two dials, and conflating them is the common error

Most systems run at least two routes, differing in speed. The choice of route is not a statement
about how suspicious the clinician feels; it is a product of probability and
time-criticality. A moderate probability of something that gets much worse in weeks belongs on
the fast route; a higher probability of something indolent may not. Which routes exist, what they
are called, what they promise and what criteria they require are local and are revised regularly
*[country-dependent]*.

## The thresholds nobody wrote down

A referral decision passes through filters that are never declared. Who presented at all. Who
could attend an appointment in working hours. How much time the consultation had. Where the
clinician's own risk tolerance sits, which varies between individuals far more than any
guideline allows for, and which drifts after a remembered case. Whether the downstream service
rejects or re-triages referrals, which is a second threshold applied by someone who has not met
the person.

A declared threshold can be audited, compared and argued with. An undeclared one can only be
inherited. That is the strongest practical argument for writing criteria down even when the
written version is crude: not that the criteria are right, but that they are visible.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Both errors land on people. A threshold set too high means someone goes back to a waiting room
with a condition that is quietly progressing, and the reasoning that sent them there was
defensible and was wrong about them. A threshold set too low means a different person waits
longer for the assessment that would have changed their management, and nobody will ever be able
to name them, because queue harm is distributed and unattributable. Those two harms are not
commensurable in a feeling, which is exactly why they are worked through as a ratio rather than
weighed in the moment.

There is a second thing that belongs here. The people who are least likely to be referred are
often the least likely to re-present, for reasons that have nothing to do with their
probability of disease — work, language, cost, transport, caring responsibilities, and previous
experience of not being believed. A threshold applied to presentations is not applied to need. It
is applied to whoever managed to be presented, and that is a different population.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate can say what their threshold *is* for a named situation, and then say what
the threshold is made of — which two harms, and whose. Then what prevalence does and does not
change. Then the queue argument, offered without prompting, and the follow-up: how a practice
would ever find out that its threshold is wrong in either direction. Then the hard one, which
separates a good answer from a complete one: what the plan is for the person left below the
threshold, by name, with a date on it.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about the structure of a referral decision, written for someone already
training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and
nothing here should inform whether any individual is referred — that belongs with the clinician
who has assessed them and with current local criteria. No referral criterion, age cut-off,
timescale, probability or threshold value is named here on purpose: all of them are local, several
are contested, and all are revised. The local urgent-referral guidance and the receiving service's
own policy are the authority. If someone is unwell right now, the relevant action is to contact
local urgent care or the local emergency number, not to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural claim — that a referral threshold is a ratio of two error costs and that
prevalence positions a person against it rather than moving it — is settled decision theory and
will not move. Everything operational will: which routes exist, what they are called, what they
promise, which criteria they require, what the downstream capacity is, and which tests are
available before referral rather than after it. Direct-access investigation has moved several
decisions upstream in some systems over the last decade and that process is continuing unevenly.
Take criteria and routes from current local guidance rather than from here, as of October 2026.
