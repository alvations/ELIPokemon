# Continuity is a diagnostic instrument that happens to feel like a kindness

The first move in any good answer is to split the word, because three different things are called
continuity and they come apart *[consensus]*:

* **Relational continuity** — the same clinician over time.
* **Informational continuity** — the same record, legible and complete, whoever is reading it.
* **Management continuity** — the same plan, so that the second contact continues the first rather
  than restarting it.

A practice can have excellent informational continuity and almost no relational continuity, and
the two are frequently traded against each other without anyone deciding to. Naming which one is
meant is most of the argument, because they have different mechanisms and different costs.

## The mechanism, which is the same mechanism as prevalence

The reason relational continuity does diagnostic work is that it supplies a **prior specific to
one person**. An unselected population gives a base rate for a symptom. A person seen repeatedly
over years gives something much sharper: their baseline, their pattern of presenting, what they
are like when they are frightened, what they are like when they are actually ill, and — the signal
with no substitute — *this is not like them*.

```
   WHAT THE RECORD CARRIES AND WHAT ONLY THE RELATIONSHIP CARRIES

   ┌──────────────────────────────┬─────────────────┬────────────────────────────────┐
   │ information                  │ in the record?  │ needs the same person?          │
   ├──────────────────────────────┼─────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┤
   │ diagnoses, medicines, results│  yes            │  no                            │
   │ what was tried and failed    │  usually        │  no                            │
   │ the plan and its triggers    │  if written     │  no                            │
   │ what matters to this person  │  rarely         │  mostly                        │
   │ how they present when unwell │  almost never   │  yes                           │
   │ "this is not like them"      │  not at all     │  yes                           │
   │ enough trust to say the real │  not at all     │  yes                           │
   │ reason for the appointment   │                 │                                │
   └──────────────────────────────┴─────────────────┴────────────────────────────────┘

   The top three rows are an information-systems problem and are solvable by writing
   things down. The bottom four are not, and that is what relational continuity is for.
```

Everything in the lower half changes a pre-test probability, which makes it diagnostic information
in exactly the sense that a test result is diagnostic information. A clinician who knows that this
person has not attended in four years is working from a different prior than one who does not, and
the finding in front of them both is identical.

## What discontinuity costs, concretely

There is no single measurable penalty for discontinuity the way there is for, say, a missed dose,
which is exactly why the costs have to be listed as mechanisms rather than asserted as a loss of
something intangible. Four of them *[mechanism, not guideline]*:

* **Re-investigation.** A clinician who does not know what has already been excluded repeats it,
  and the repeat carries the same false-positive rate it did the first time.
* **The diagnostic clock restarts.** A presentation that is the third of its kind is a different
  presentation from a first, and that fact lives in whether anyone recognises it as the third. If
  it is read as a first, the clock that should be running is not.
* **A safety-net held in one person's head stops existing** the moment somebody else takes the
  consultation, which is precisely the situation the net was built for.
* **The "this is not like them" signal has no substitute.** It cannot be written down in advance
  because nobody knows which change will matter, and it is the signal most often cited after the
  event by the clinician who did catch something early.

## What it buys, stated honestly

The observational literature on continuity is large and consistent in direction: higher continuity
is associated with fewer unscheduled and emergency attendances, better uptake of agreed plans,
higher satisfaction on both sides of the consultation, and in several large datasets lower
mortality *[consensus on the direction]*.

What that literature is not is a clean experiment, and a good answer says so without using it as a
reason to dismiss the finding. The confounding is structural and runs in both directions. People
with stable lives, one regular clinician and the capacity to book ahead get more continuity *and*
are healthier for other reasons. But people who are seriously ill also attend more and may
accumulate continuity as a consequence of illness rather than a cause of health. Randomising
continuity is close to impossible, so the honest position is a consistent association with a
plausible mechanism and a contested effect size, and no specific number is reproduced here for
that reason.

## Why it is an intervention and not a manner

Three tests of whether something is an intervention: it can be designed, it can be measured, and
it can be dosed. Continuity passes all three.

* **It is designed.** Micro-teams, a named accountable clinician, booking rules that prefer the
  usual clinician for a follow-up, and list sizes small enough for the preference to be
  satisfiable. These are organisational decisions, and a practice that has not made them has made
  the opposite one by default.
* **It is measured.** The simplest index is the proportion of a person's contacts that were with
  their usual clinician; others measure how dispersed contacts are across all the clinicians seen.
  The indices disagree with each other and are sensitive to how many contacts someone has, which
  is worth knowing before quoting one.
* **It is dosed.** It is not needed equally by everyone. The strongest argument for designing it
  deliberately is that it can be concentrated where the mechanism bites hardest — complex
  multimorbidity, serious mental illness, frailty, end-of-life care, and anyone whose presentation
  is hard to interpret without a baseline.

## The tradeoffs, which are real

* **Continuity competes with access**, and for a person who needs to be seen today the same-day
  appointment is the better care. A practice that maximises one has usually degraded the other.
* **A longitudinal prior can anchor as well as inform.** The clinician who knows someone best is
  also the one most able to normalise a change, having watched it arrive gradually. Continuity
  makes anchoring and premature closure easier, not harder, and that is the honest cost.
* **Single points of failure.** A plan that lives with one clinician fails when that clinician is
  away, which is the argument for a small team rather than one person.
* **It costs the clinician something**, and arrangements built on individual goodwill rather than
  rota design do not survive.
* **The system shapes what is possible.** Where people are registered with one practice,
  relational continuity is the default that has to be protected; in open-access systems it has to
  be constructed, and the measures mean different things *[country-dependent]*.

## The human stakes, said plainly

The people for whom continuity matters most are generally those least able to arrange it: people
who cannot phone at a fixed time, who cannot wait weeks for a specific clinician, whose first
language is not the system's, or who have previously been disbelieved. If continuity is allocated
by who can navigate a booking system, it goes to those who need it least. Treating it as an
intervention rather than a preference is what makes it allocable at all.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate splits the three kinds without prompting. Then the mechanism in Bayesian
terms, as a person-specific prior rather than a nice atmosphere. Then the confounding, and whether
they can argue it in both directions. Then the tradeoff against access, and who they would
prioritise. Then the awkward one: the harm case, where knowing someone well is the reason a change
was missed.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about how a service is organised and why that organisation has clinical
effects, written for someone already training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical
reference, not a decision aid, and not for use in making a decision about any person's care. No
effect size is quoted, because the evidence is observational and the estimates are contested. How
registration, booking and continuity work differs substantially between health systems, and local
arrangements are the authority on what is possible — this is not. If someone is unwell right now,
the relevant action is to contact local urgent care or the local emergency number, not to read
this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The three-way split of continuity, and the mechanism by which relational continuity carries
diagnostic information, are settled and stable. The association with outcomes is consistent in
direction and contested in magnitude, and that has not changed. What is moving quickly is the
delivery context: remote and asynchronous consulting, triage-first access models, larger
organisational groupings of practices, and the use of records that follow a person between
providers. Several of those improve informational continuity while making relational continuity
harder, which is exactly why the three need separating. Re-check the local arrangements rather
than assuming them. Correct as a description of consensus in October 2026.
