# The last act in a chain is the only one with a name attached, which is why an enquiry can always stop there

A significant event analysis is a structured retrospective review of a single episode — one that
caused harm, one that nearly did, or occasionally one that went notably well — whose output is a
change to the system that produced it (**definitional**). Three things it is not: it is not an
investigation of a person, it is not a disciplinary process, and it is not a statistical
instrument. A single event cannot estimate a rate, cannot demonstrate that anything has improved,
and is not a sample of the decisions a practice makes. Treating it as any of those three is the
commonest way the whole exercise is wasted.

Load-bearing claims below are marked with what they rest on: **mechanism**, **definitional**,
(**consensus**) or (**country-dependent**). Whether such a review is required, what it is called,
who must be told, what must be disclosed and how it interacts with regulation and with any process
about conduct are all set locally and differ substantially between systems; none of that is
specified here.

Five mechanisms, each of which operates without anybody intending to blame anybody.

1. **Hindsight and outcome bias.** Knowing how it ended changes how reasonable the decision looks,
   in a direction that is measurable and that does not go away when people are warned about it
   (**consensus**). The reviewer is reasoning from a position nobody in the event occupied.
2. **Visibility.** The final act has a name, a time and a record. The conditions upstream — a
   default, a rota, a template, an interface, a decision taken elsewhere months earlier — have
   none of those. An enquiry naturally terminates where the evidence is richest, and the evidence
   is richest at the end.
3. **Counterfactual closure.** *If only somebody had checked* is always available and always true,
   and once it is on the table the upstream questions are never asked. A sufficient explanation
   that arrives early prevents an adequate one arriving later (**mechanism**).
4. **It destroys its own input.** The instrument runs on people reporting things, most of which
   nobody else would ever know about. A process that produces sanctions reduces reporting, and
   reduced reporting is invisible: the practice sees fewer events and concludes it has fewer
   (**consensus**).
5. **It predicts recurrence wrongly.** If the conditions that made the action reasonable are
   unchanged, the event recurs with a different name attached. Retraining or removing an
   individual is a change to the actor and not to the chain. That is why "remind everybody to be
   careful" and "the person responsible has been dealt with" sit at the same place in the
   hierarchy of outputs below: both act on who was there and neither touches a condition
   (**mechanism**). How much each class of change actually buys is a question for the
   human-factors literature, not for this answer.

This is the same argument nursing m003 makes about medicines administration — that diligence is
the wrong frame and the system is the right one — applied retrospectively to a single event rather
than prospectively to a process.

## What replaces it, and what it does not mean

It does not mean nobody is answerable. Two words are routinely conflated and the distinction is
the whole of the professional position (**definitional**):

* **Accountability** is the duty to give an account: to say what you did, what you knew and why it
  seemed right. It is owed to the person affected and to colleagues, and nothing in a systems
  frame removes it.
* **Culpability** is fault. It is a judgement about conduct, it has its own procedures, its own
  standards of evidence and its own consequences, and it is decided elsewhere
  (**country-dependent**).

A review that treats every event as the first and never the second is as useless as one that
treats every event as the second. A small number of events do involve conduct, impairment or a
pattern rather than an occasion, and a systems frame is not a licence to ignore them; the local
procedures govern, and recognising which situation is in front of you is part of the judgement.
What makes the instrument work is that the *two processes are not the same meeting*, because a
review that might end in sanction cannot also be the forum where someone says the thing nobody
else knows.

The substitution, then, is a change of question. Not *who failed to do what they should have* but
**what made this action the reasonable one at the time, to a competent person, given what was in
front of them** — and then what about that is changeable (**consensus**).

## Why comparing the event to the written procedure teaches nothing

Every procedure is a description of an intended path. The path actually taken contains
adaptations, shortcuts and local fixes, most of which exist because the intended path does not fit
the conditions — and most of which are *why the service functions at all* on an ordinary day
(**mechanism**).

So a review that compares what happened with what is written finds a deviation. It will always
find a deviation, in the events that went badly and equally in the thousands that went fine,
because the deviation is the normal state. The finding is therefore uninformative, and acting on
it — reasserting the written path — removes an adaptation that was load-bearing and makes the next
failure more likely rather than less.

The useful version of the same question is: why is the actual path the usual path? What does it
get around? If the answer is that the procedure requires something the day does not contain, then
the procedure is the defect, and that is a finding worth having.

## What the review has to produce

A review's output is the only part of it that changes anything, and most outputs are weak. In
rough order of how much they buy:

* **Remove the possibility.** Change a default, a form, a template, a stock arrangement, a route —
  so that the error becomes difficult or impossible rather than merely discouraged. This is the
  only class of change that does not depend on anybody remembering anything.
* **Make the right action the easy one.** Reorder a screen, pre-populate a field, put the thing
  where the hand already goes.
* **Build in a check that something else performs.** A second pair of eyes, an automated flag, a
  reconciliation step with an owner.
* **Change who holds a loop.** Most primary-care events involve an open loop — a result, a
  referral, a follow-up — and closing it requires a *named person and a stated action*, not a
  shared intention. Nursing m110 identifies unowned results as the commonest structural failure in
  the whole transition out of hospital, and the mechanism is identical at this end.
* **Tell people to be careful.** The modal output of real significant event analyses, and the
  weakest. It is not worthless — for a genuinely rare and unforeseeable situation it may be all
  there is — but a review that produces only this has usually stopped at the active failure.

Two more requirements, because without them the change does not exist. It needs a **named owner**,
because a change owned by everybody is owned by nobody; and it needs a **point at which somebody
checks whether it happened**, because the implementation step is where most of these die
(**consensus**). No interval is suggested here; local arrangements set it.

## The honest limits of the instrument

* **It is a sample of one, selected on its outcome.** Events reach review because something went
  wrong, so the reviewed set is not a sample of the practice's decisions and nothing about rates
  can be inferred from it. This is m084's observability asymmetry at the level of a review file.
* **It cannot evaluate.** A single event cannot show that last year's change worked. That requires
  counting something over time, which is a different exercise with different methods.
* **It is vulnerable to the severity of the outcome.** Identical decisions get reviewed
  differently depending on how badly they turned out, and near-misses — which are the cheapest
  learning available, because the information arrives without the harm — are the ones least likely
  to be reported.
* **It is a meeting, with all that implies.** Seniority, who speaks first, who is present and who
  is being appraised by someone in the room all shape what gets said. A structure that makes the
  account independent of the hierarchy is doing real work, not procedural work.

## The human stakes, said plainly

There is a person in the middle of this and it is usually two people.

The patient, or the family, needs an account: what happened, what is known, what is not yet known,
what is being done, and — where it is owed — an apology. In many systems that duty is explicit and
statutory, and what it requires differs by jurisdiction (**country-dependent**). What is
consistent is that a review conducted so that the organisation can show it reviewed something, and
which never reaches the person affected, has failed at the only part of itself that is owed to
anybody.

And the clinician. Being the named person in the last box of that chain is one of the hardest
things that happens in a professional life. People involved in serious events describe a reaction
that outlasts the event by a long way and that affects their subsequent practice, usually in the
direction of more caution and sometimes in the direction of leaving. This is well described and
the support available is uneven and frequently absent. If you are the person in that position, the
sentence that matters is that this is a known and expected reaction to something difficult, not
evidence about your competence, and that support — occupational health, a professional body, a
defence organisation, a colleague who has been there — exists and is worth using early rather than
late. A review process that leaves the person worse is not a rigorous process; it is a broken one,
and it will also be a less informative one, because the next person who is in that position will
have watched this one.

The last thing is harder to say. A systems frame is sometimes experienced by a family as evasion —
as a way of ensuring nobody is held responsible. That reaction is reasonable and it is not
answered by explaining human factors. It is answered by the organisation taking responsibility as
an organisation, in public, for a thing it built, and by the change actually happening. Where it
does not happen, the family's reading was correct.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate states what the instrument is for and names at least two things it is not.
Then the stopping-rule argument — that an enquiry running backwards terminates at the first
nameable sufficient cause — rather than an assertion that blame is unkind. Then hindsight bias,
with the two-column distinction between what the reviewer holds and what the actor held. Then the
reporting-destruction mechanism, and why its effect is invisible. Then accountability against
culpability, with the point that the two processes must not be the same meeting, and that a
minority of events really are about conduct. Then why comparing the event to the written procedure
is uninformative. Then the hierarchy of outputs, with the modal output identified as the weakest
and the named owner and the check attached. Then the hard one — how their own practice would get
near-misses reported, given that the information arrives free and the incentive runs the other
way.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about the structure of a retrospective review, written for someone
already training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision
aid, and it is **not a procedure for conducting an investigation or for handling a complaint, a
claim or a concern about a colleague** — all of those are governed by local policy, by regulation
and in places by law, they differ by jurisdiction, and the local documents are the authority. No
timescale, reporting threshold, grading scheme or disclosure requirement is named here on purpose.
Nothing here describes or judges any real event; every example is constructed. If you are the
person involved in a serious incident, the support routes named in the sources above exist for
that and are worth using early. If someone is unwell right now, the relevant action is to contact
local urgent care or the local emergency number, not to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural claims — that a backward enquiry terminates at the first nameable sufficient cause,
that hindsight bias is not corrected by good intentions, that a blame-bearing process reduces its
own input, that work-as-done differs from work-as-imagined, and that a single event cannot
estimate a rate — are mechanism and mainstream, and they have been stable for decades. What moves
is the institutional layer: what reporting systems exist, what they require, how a review is
graded, what must be disclosed and to whom, and how a safety review is kept separate from
processes about conduct. Several systems have replaced their incident frameworks in the last few
years, generally in the direction of systems-based review and proportionate response, and the
detail differs. The literature on supporting staff after serious incidents is growing and the
provision is not keeping pace with it. Take the framework and the duties from current local
requirements rather than from here, as of October 2026.
