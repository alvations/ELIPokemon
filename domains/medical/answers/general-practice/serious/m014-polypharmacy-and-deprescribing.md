# The count is not a summary of the risk. It is part of the risk.

Polypharmacy is usually described as a count, which makes it sound like a label. It is better
understood as a structural claim: as a regimen grows, the number of ways its components can
interact with each other, with the person's physiology and with their ability to take any of it
grows faster than the number of components, while the benefit of each addition grows more slowly
than the one before it. The two curves have different shapes. Somewhere they cross, and after that
point adding a well-indicated medicine makes things worse *[mechanism, not guideline]*.

This is why the useful distinction is not "many medicines" against "few" but **appropriate**
polypharmacy against **problematic** polypharmacy — a regimen where every component is still doing
work the person would choose, against one that accumulated *[consensus]*.

## Why the count itself carries risk

```
   ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY — a hypothetical, independent 5 % chance per medicine of one
   troublesome effect. This is a round number chosen to show a shape. It is not a
   measured figure for any medicine, and real effects are neither 5 % nor independent.

   medicines   interacting pairs = n(n−1)/2    P(at least one effect) = 1 − 0.95ⁿ
   ─────────   ─────────────────────────────   ──────────────────────────────────
       1                      0                             5.0 %
       2                      1                             9.8 %
       5                     10                            22.6 %
      10                     45                            40.1 %
      15                    105                            53.7 %

   The middle column grows quadratically and does not saturate. The right column
   saturates. Benefit, in almost every real regimen, behaves like the right column —
   each addition adds less than the last — while the opportunity for harm behaves
   like the middle one.
```

Four mechanisms sit underneath that, and naming them is better than naming a number:

1. **Pairwise and higher-order interactions**, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic — including the
   cumulative ones, where several medicines each contribute a modest amount of the same effect and
   no single pair looks dangerous.
2. **Adherence falls as complexity rises**, and it falls with the number of administration times
   more steeply than with the number of medicines, so two regimens with the same count are not
   equally takeable *[consensus]*.
3. **Each medicine is a monitoring commitment**, and monitoring capacity is finite. A regimen can
   outgrow the system's ability to watch it.
4. **The evidence runs out.** Trials predominantly recruit people with one condition and exclude
   the multimorbid and the frail. Guideline-concordant care for five conditions sums to a regimen
   that no trial has ever studied, and the sum is not the thing that was tested *[consensus]*.

## The prescribing cascade

```
   THE SHAPE, not a real sequence.

   medicine A ──► an adverse effect of A
                        │
                        └── read as a NEW problem ──► medicine B
                                                           │
                                                           └── an adverse effect of B
                                                                      │
                                                                      └──► medicine C

   Every step is locally reasonable and nobody made a mistake at any one of them. The
   error is only visible from outside the sequence — which is why a review has to start
   from the list and ask what each item is for, rather than starting from the complaint.
```

## What makes stopping a different kind of decision

```
   ┌──────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┐
   │                      │ starting                   │ stopping                    │
   ├──────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┤
   │ evidence base        │ trials designed to answer  │ far fewer trials of         │
   │                      │ exactly this question      │ withdrawal, and they are    │
   │                      │                            │ smaller and shorter         │
   │ recorded reason      │ an indication is coded at   │ frequently nothing records  │
   │                      │ the moment of starting      │ why, or for how long        │
   │ default when unsure  │ continue                   │ continue                    │
   │ who carries blame    │ diffuse, often historical  │ the person who stopped it   │
   │ early signal         │ improvement, attributed to │ symptom return or rebound,  │
   │                      │ the medicine               │ attributed to the stop      │
   │ follow-up required   │ sometimes                  │ always                      │
   └──────────────────────┴────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┘
```

The asymmetries in that table are the whole answer, and three are worth drawing out.

**Withdrawal effects and rebound are routinely mistaken for the original disease returning.** For
several widely used classes, stopping produces a transient worsening that is a property of the
stopping rather than of the underlying condition. If that is not anticipated and described in
advance, the natural interpretation is that the original problem was real and the stop was wrong —
and the medicine goes back permanently on the strength of an artefact *[consensus]*.

**A stop is an intervention, so it needs a plan, not just a decision.** A defensible deprescribing
decision specifies what is being withdrawn, over what period, what to watch for, who is watching,
when the review is, and — the part most often missing — the condition under which the medicine
would be restarted. A stop without a restart condition is a gamble; a stop with one is a trial of
withdrawal, which is a legitimate clinical experiment with an n of one.

**The person who stops is rarely the person who started.** Starting happens in the setting where
the condition was diagnosed, often in a clinic, often years earlier. Stopping happens where the
whole list is visible, which is usually primary care. That means the stopper is working without
the reasoning that produced the start, and the honest first move is frequently to find out what
the intention was rather than to adjudicate it.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Some medicines produce their benefit over a long horizon. Where the benefit accrues over years and
a person's circumstances have changed, the balance that justified starting may no longer hold, and
noticing that is part of good care rather than a withdrawal of it. This is not a judgement about
anyone's worth or a rationing argument, and it is not a conversation that should arrive as a
surprise in a medication review — which is exactly why it belongs in an anticipatory discussion
held while there is time for it, with the person deciding what matters to them *[consensus]*.

The other half of the same section, which is easier to forget because nobody reports it. Taking
fifteen medicines is itself a daily experience — the timing, the counting, the swallowing, the
collecting, the cost where there is one, and the constant reminder of being a person with fifteen
conditions. That burden is borne by the person and is largely invisible to whoever writes the
prescriptions, because it never appears in a consultation as a complaint about medicines. It
appears as tiredness, or as not taking them, and both are routinely read as something else.

## The thing not to stop

The counterweight to all of this: the most common medication error in a frail, multimorbid
population is still **under**-treatment of something that would have helped, and enthusiasm for
shortening a list can produce exactly that. A review that only removes is as unexamined as one
that only adds. The two questions — what is no longer earning its place, and what is missing — are
the same review.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate distinguishes appropriate from problematic polypharmacy rather than treating
the count as the diagnosis. Then the cascade, with an example. Then the specific content of a
withdrawal plan, including the restart condition, which is the part most candidates omit. Then
whether they would check what else depends on the medicine before withdrawing it, and how. Then
the conversation itself, and who is deciding.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about how a prescribing decision is structured, written for someone
already training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision
aid, and nothing here should inform what any individual takes or ceases to take — that belongs
with the prescriber and the pharmacist who hold the person's full list. No medicine, class, dose,
interval or withdrawal schedule is named here on purpose. The arithmetic is a hypothetical round
figure illustrating a shape and describes no real medicine. Formularies and interaction data
differ by country and change; the local formulary is the authority and must be checked before
anything reaches a patient. If someone is unwell right now, the relevant action is to contact
local urgent care or the local emergency number, not to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument — that interaction opportunity grows faster than component count while
marginal benefit shrinks — is settled and is not expected to move. The framing of deprescribing as
an intervention requiring a plan and a restart condition is now mainstream rather than novel,
which was not true a decade ago. What continues to move is the evidence base for withdrawing
specific classes, the content of the published screening tools, and the structure of the funded
medication review, all of which differ by country. Take those from current local sources rather
than from here. Correct as a description of consensus in October 2026.
