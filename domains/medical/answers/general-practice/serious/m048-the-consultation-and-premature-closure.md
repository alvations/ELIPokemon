# The opening question draws the sample. Everything afterwards is conditioned on that draw

A consultation is a search over a space of problems, and the opening question determines which
part of that space gets sampled. An open opening draws from the distribution as the person holds
it, in their ordering. A closed opening draws from the distribution as the clinician already
suspects it to be. Both produce a sample; only the first one is a sample of the thing you are
trying to estimate *[mechanism, not guideline]*.

That is why the opening is not etiquette. It is the sampling step, and no amount of careful
reasoning downstream can recover information that the first question excluded from the sample.

## Three facts about what people bring, and what follows from each

1. **Agendas are multi-item, and the ordering is not by importance.** People commonly arrive with
   more than one concern, and the one named first is frequently not the one they are most worried
   about — the worrying one is often held back until the clinician appears to have finished, or is
   raised at the point when the consultation is visibly ending. That this pattern exists is not
   controversial; its frequency differs by setting and by how it is measured *[consensus]*.
2. **An uninterrupted opening statement is short.** Shorter than clinicians expect, and much
   shorter than the time its interruption goes on to cost. Specific averages have been published
   by several groups and they differ; the useful version of the finding is the direction, which is
   that the cost of listening is smaller than the cost of not.
3. **Interruption truncates the list, and it does not truncate it at random.** It stops at
   whatever the person happened to say first — so the items most likely to be lost are the ones
   that were hardest to say.

## Why premature closure is adaptive, which is why it is hard to drop

The first hypothesis is usually right. In an unselected population most presentations are common
and self-limiting, so a clinician who commits early is correct most of the time and faster every
time. The efficiency gain is real and it is what makes a working day possible. Three consequences
follow, and the third is the one people skip *[mechanism, not guideline]*:

* **The benefit is spread across every consultation; the cost is concentrated in a few.** So the
  practice feels good from the inside and the harm is invisible in the aggregate until somebody
  audits the few.
* **The error is not the hypothesis. It is the stopping.** Premature closure means the search
  terminated, not that the first guess was poor. A clinician who forms a hypothesis in ten seconds
  and keeps sampling has not made the error.
* **The commitment is partly made for you.** Once an impression exists, the questions that come to
  mind are the ones that fit it, and the words the person uses get mapped onto it. You do not
  experience this as choosing to confirm; you experience it as the obvious next question.

## What actually reduces the cost

* **An explicit second pass.** Some form of *what else* — asked once the first item has been
  heard, not at the end — reliably surfaces items that would otherwise have been lost. It is the
  cheapest intervention in the whole consultation.
* **Say the differential out loud, including the one you do not believe.** Naming an alternative
  converts a private impression into a thing that can be argued with, by you and by the person.
* **Re-read the opening.** The person's own words, before any hypothesis was applied to them, are
  the only uncontaminated sample you will get.
* **A deliberate pause before committing** — the equivalent of a timeout — is worth most in the
  presentations where the first impression arrived fastest, which is the opposite of where it
  feels needed.
* **And the structural fix is a safety-net.** The reason an opening pass does not have to be right
  is that the plan contains a named trigger and a route back. Diagnostic reasoning under
  uncertainty is far less dependent on closing correctly when the consequence of closing wrongly
  has been designed for.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Being interrupted while describing something frightening is not a minor procedural failure. It
tells someone that what they are saying is not what the room is for, and people do not generally
try twice. A great many consultations end with the person having not said the thing, and almost
none of those are recorded as anything at all.

The distribution of that harm is not even. The people most likely to be interrupted, and least
likely to re-raise an item once it is passed over, are those whose speech the system finds
hardest to accommodate: anyone working through an interpreter, anyone with a communication
disability, anyone in distress, anyone who has previously not been believed, and anyone who has
learned that the appointment is short and they should not take up space in it. A consultation
style built around a confident, fluent, available person is not a consultation style for them,
and the fix is structural rather than attitudinal — time, interpreters booked properly, and not
making the person compete with the clock.

And the part that is not about diagnosis. For the clinician the consultation is one of many; for
the person it is an event they will remember and repeat to their family. Both of those are true
at once, and the second one is not a sentimental addition to the first. How the opening went is
most of what determines whether the person comes back, which means it is also a safety mechanism.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate treats the opening as the sampling step rather than as rapport. Then the
distinction between forming a hypothesis early and stopping early, with an example of each. Then
the information value of a confirming question, said plainly. Then what *what else* is actually
for, and why it belongs near the start. Then the hard one: a consultation that went wrong without
a single incorrect inference in it, and where in the sequence the loss occurred.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about clinical reasoning and consultation structure, written for someone
already training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision
aid, and not for use in making a decision about any person's care. No published average for the
length of an opening statement, and no diagnostic-error rate, is reproduced here, because the
figures differ between studies and settings and the principle does not depend on any of them. If
someone is unwell right now, the relevant action is to contact local urgent care or the local
emergency number, not to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The reasoning here — that the opening determines what is sampled, and that premature closure is a
failure of stopping rather than of hypothesis generation — is long settled and is not expected to
move. What is moving is the context. Remote, telephone and asynchronous consulting change the
opening in ways that are only partly understood, and text-based first contact in particular
changes who is able to state an agenda at all. Automated history-taking and summarisation tools
are being introduced into first contact in several systems; their effect on what gets sampled and
on whose words survive into the record is an open question and should be treated as one. Take
local consultation-model teaching and local access arrangements from current sources rather than
from here, as of October 2026.
