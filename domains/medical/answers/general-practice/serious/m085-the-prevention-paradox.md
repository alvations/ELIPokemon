# Most cases come from the many at modest risk, so the best population measure is imperceptible

The paradox is not a paradox once the arithmetic is written down, and the arithmetic is the whole
answer. Cases in a population are the sum, over every level of risk, of the number of people at
that level multiplied by their risk. The number of people falls off much faster than risk rises as
you move out toward the tail. So the majority of cases arise in people whose individual risk is
unremarkable — and an intervention that shifts the whole distribution a little therefore prevents
more cases than one that fixes the tail completely, while offering each individual a benefit far
too small to feel (**mechanism**). The distinction between a population strategy and a high-risk
strategy, and the observation that a measure doing great collective good may offer little to each
participant, is usually credited to Geoffrey Rose.

Load-bearing claims below are marked with what they rest on: **mechanism**, **definitional**,
(**consensus**) or (**country-dependent**).

## The arithmetic, with the sum done

```
   ILLUSTRATIVE NUMBERS ONLY — round figures chosen to show the mechanism. They are
   not the risk distribution of any real condition and nothing clinical should be
   read out of them.

   100 people, three strata of risk over some stated period:

     stratum           people   risk each   cases from this stratum
     ───────────────   ──────   ─────────   ───────────────────────
     low                  80      1 %           0.80
     moderate             18      5 %           0.90
     high                  2     25 %           0.50
                                              ───────
     total                                      2.20 cases

   WHERE THE CASES ACTUALLY COME FROM

     the high-risk 2 people      ████                       23 %
     the moderate 18             ███████                    41 %
     the low-risk 80             ██████                     36 %
                                 └─ 77% of cases are in the 98 people nobody
                                    would call high risk

   NOW TWO STRATEGIES, PRICED

     A. HIGH-RISK: halve the risk of the top 2 people
        prevented = 2 × 12.5%  = 0.25 cases   =  11 % of all cases
        and even ABOLISHING their risk entirely caps out at 23 %

     B. POPULATION: shift every stratum's risk down by a fifth
        prevented = 0.20 × 2.20 = 0.44 cases  =  20 % of all cases
        delivered to 100 people, of whom 98 were never at much risk

   AND THE SAME SUM FROM THE INDIVIDUAL'S SIDE

     a person in the low stratum, under strategy B
        their risk goes from 1 % to 0.8 %
        absolute change: 0.2 percentage points
        → 998 in 1 000 such people get no event either way, and the two who
          do cannot be distinguished from the ones who would have anyway
```

Both columns of that table are correct. They are sums over different things, which is the same
structure m049 describes for the antibiotic decision, and recognising the shape is worth more than
memorising either case.

## Four consequences, and the third is the one that changes practice

1. **The number needed to treat is large by construction, not by failure.** If the benefit is
   spread over the many at modest risk, then most people who take the intervention were never
   going to have the event. That is not a sign the intervention is weak; it is what a population
   strategy *is* (**mechanism**).
2. **Safety and cost dominate everything else.** The harms are distributed over the same large
   denominator as the benefits, so a small per-person harm is multiplied by the same number as the
   small per-person benefit and can cancel it. This is why the threshold of acceptable harm for a
   population measure is far stricter than for a treatment given to someone at high risk — the
   same harm buys far less for each recipient (**mechanism**).
3. **Imperceptible benefit will not sustain individual effort, so the measures that work tend to
   be structural.** Asking a hundred people to do something daily for a 0.2 percentage-point
   change in their own risk is asking for an act whose reward nobody can experience. Measures
   that change the default — what is in the food, what the built environment makes easy, what the
   price is, what happens unless you opt out — do not depend on anybody feeling the benefit
   (**consensus**).
4. **Nobody can be identified as the beneficiary.** The 0.44 prevented cases are real and they
   are not anyone in particular. No participant can be told that they were helped, and no
   participant can be shown that they were not. That makes the case for a population measure
   impossible to argue one person at a time, which is exactly why it keeps being argued one person
   at a time and keeps losing (**mechanism**).

## The high-risk strategy is not the mistake, and saying so matters

The high-risk strategy is efficient per person treated, it offers each recipient a benefit large
enough to be worth their trouble, it uses the clinical relationship that already exists, and for
conditions where risk really is concentrated — a strong single-gene determinant, a very high
measured risk, an established event already — it is the only strategy that does anything useful
for the people who have most to lose (**consensus**).

Its limit is arithmetic rather than moral: it cannot reach cases that are not in the tail, and
most cases are not in the tail. It also requires a screening or case-finding step to identify the
tail, which brings its own costs and the harms m013 describes. The two strategies are
complements. The error is running one and claiming the coverage of both.

## Why first-contact clinical work makes this hard to see

The population in front of a clinician is selected for having presented. Prevented cases do not
present, do not thank anybody, and are not recorded; the person who had the event despite taking
the intervention is in the room and is memorable. This is the same observability asymmetry m084
describes for overdiagnosis, pointing in the same direction: what is visible teaches, what is
invisible does not, and no amount of conscientiousness corrects for a missing signal
(**mechanism**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

Someone can take a medicine, or make a change, every day for twenty years, derive nothing
detectable from it, and have been entirely right to do it. That is a genuinely difficult thing to
be told and it should not be dressed up. The expected value was positive; the realisation for that
individual was zero; both statements are true and neither cancels the other. Being clear about
this in advance — that the offer is a small shift in a probability and not a promise — is more
respectful than implying a benefit that cannot be delivered to a named person.

The second thing is about who bears the burden. A population measure delivered as individual
advice asks for daily effort, money, time and attention, and those are not equally available.
Advice is cheapest to follow for people who already have the most room to follow it, so a
population strategy run entirely through exhortation can widen the gap it was meant to close —
m050's argument, applying here with full force. Structural measures are preferred partly because
they are effective and partly because they do not charge the people with the least capacity the
most to comply.

And the honest closing caution: none of this means an individual's own decision is unimportant or
that a clinician should press a population measure on a person who has weighed it and declined.
That decision belongs with the person, which is m083's subject.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate can do the sum — people times risk, summed over strata — rather than
asserting the conclusion. Then the ceiling on the high-risk strategy, stated as a number derived
from their own distribution. Then why the acceptable harm threshold differs between the two
strategies, which is the part that most often goes missing. Then the attribution point, and what
it implies about consent conversations for population measures. Then the counterweight, offered
unprompted. Then the hard one — how they would explain to one person that the measure is worth
taking while being honest that it will probably do nothing detectable for them.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about the structure of preventive reasoning, written for someone
already training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision
aid, and nothing here is advice to any individual about whether to take, continue or stop a
preventive intervention — that belongs with the clinician who knows them. No risk factor,
threshold, effect size or number needed to treat is named here on purpose: those are specific to
the intervention and the population, several are contested, and all are revised. The worked
figures above are hypothetical round numbers illustrating a sum and are not the risk distribution
of anything real. If someone is unwell right now, the relevant action is to contact local urgent
care or the local emergency number, not to read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The arithmetic is arithmetic and will not move, and the population-against-high-risk distinction
has been stable for decades. What moves is everything applied: which interventions are offered as
population measures, at what thresholds, with what evidence of absolute benefit, and whether
delivery is structural or advisory. The evidence base for default-changing and
environmental measures has grown substantially over the last decade and continues to; the balance
between population and high-risk approaches for specific risk factors is argued about actively and
differs by country. Take specific thresholds, effect sizes and programme details from current
local guidance rather than from here, as of October 2026.
