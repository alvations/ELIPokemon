# Reactive care samples a population at the moments it chooses to be sampled

A consultation happens because somebody decided to come. That decision is driven by symptoms, by
worry, and by the practical possibility of attending. For a condition that produces symptoms, this
is an efficient sampler: the thing that needs attention generates the contact that provides
it. For a condition that produces no symptoms until it has already done its damage, it is close
to the worst sampler available, because the probability of being sampled is near-independent of
the variable that matters *[mechanism, not guideline]*.

A structured review changes one thing: who initiates. The contact is generated by a register and a
clock rather than by a symptom. Everything else about a review — the template, the measurements,
the ordering, the recall letter — is implementation of that single change. This is also why the
word *structured* is doing real work and not decoration: the value is in the specification, not in
the length of the appointment.

## What a review specifies that an opportunistic contact cannot

Four mechanisms, and none of them is about the clinician trying harder *[mechanism, not
guideline]*:

1. **The sampling times are chosen rather than observed.** In reactive care the interval between
   looks is a property of the person's symptoms and circumstances. In a review it is a parameter,
   set against how fast the thing changes and how fast a decision would change with it.
2. **The variable list is fixed in advance, so a gap is visible as a gap.** An opportunistic
   contact records what the presenting complaint led to. A review has a denominator, which is what
   turns "not recorded" from an absence into a finding.
3. **The register is the intervention; the appointment is a consequence.** A service that reviews
   whoever happens to attend has reinvented reactive care with a template on top. The active
   ingredient is the list of people who have not been seen.
4. **The record is a cached value, and the cache is only refreshed on contact.** Every number in
   the notes is a reading of the moment it was taken. Reactive care lets the refresh interval be
   set by chance; and because attendance falls as people become less able to attend, the interval
   tends to be longest exactly where the accumulated burden is greatest.

Two errors sit on either side of that choice, and they are not symmetrical.

* **An interval shorter than the rate of change** buys very little information per look and a lot
  of noise. Repeated measurement of a variable that moves within its own measurement error
  generates apparent trends, and each apparent trend invites an action.
* **An interval longer than the rate of change** means the value at the point of decision is
  stale, and the staleness is invisible: the number on the screen looks like a number, not like a
  number from eighteen months ago.

Neither failure announces itself, which is why the interval belongs in a protocol rather than in
each clinician's judgement, and why the protocol should say what the interval is *for*.

## Where a review stops being a clinical act

This is the part that matters more than the mechanism, because the mechanism is easy and the
failure is common.

* **The measurement is not the intervention. The decision is.** A completed template with no
  decision in it has produced a record and no care. The test of a review is whether anything would
  have been different had a value come back differently.
* **Incentivised indicators change recorded activity, and what is measured displaces what is
  not.** That direction is not seriously disputed; the size of the effect, and whether it survives
  the removal of the incentive, are contested and differ between systems
  (**country-dependent**).
* **A review built around a single disease collides with the next person who has four.** Four
  annual reviews is four appointments, four templates and four sets of targets, assembled by
  nobody. That is a separate question and it has its own answer.
* **Recall reaches whoever the recall system can reach.** A letter to a fixed address, a telephone
  call in working hours and an online booking form are each a filter, and each filters hardest on
  the people carrying the most disease. A review programme measured on mean coverage cannot see
  that happening.
* **The consultation inside the review is still a consultation.** The structure guarantees that
  the right variables are collected; it guarantees nothing about whether anyone asked what the
  person came to say.

## The human stakes, said plainly

A review invites someone who feels well to come and be told about something they cannot feel. That
is a genuinely odd thing to ask of a person, and it is worth not glossing over. The offer is an
offer: attending, and acting on what is found, are decisions for the person, and a service that
treats non-attendance as non-compliance has misunderstood its own position.

The burden is real and is mostly invisible to whoever books the appointment. Time off work,
transport, childcare, the cost where there is one, the blood test the week before, the waiting
room, and the cumulative experience of being a person who is managed. For someone with several
conditions this is not one morning a year; it is a recurring part of life that nobody has added
up. When that burden becomes the reason someone stops coming, it is usually recorded as
disengagement.

And the thing the structure cannot supply. Being given the name of a condition changes how a
person understands their own body, and it does so permanently. Someone who arrives well and
leaves as a patient has had something taken as well as given, even when the exchange is clearly
worth it. Saying so out loud, in the appointment, is not a softening of the message; it is part of
the accuracy of it.

## What an examiner digs into next

Whether the candidate identifies the sampling mechanism as the thing that changes, rather than
listing the contents of a template. Then the interval: what it should be set against, and what
goes wrong in each direction. Then the register, and why a review service without one is not a
review service. Then the honest part — what proportion of completed reviews contain a decision,
how anyone would know, and what the answer would imply.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about how a clinical process is designed, written for someone already
training in or qualified for the field. It is not a clinical reference, not a decision aid, and
not for use in making a decision about any person's care. No review interval, target value or
indicator threshold is reproduced here on purpose: those are local, they are revised, and the
authoritative version is the one issued for the reader's own setting. If someone is unwell right
now, the relevant action is to contact local urgent care or the local emergency number, not to
read this.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The sampling argument — that patient-initiated contact is a biased sampler for anything
asymptomatic, and that a register plus a clock removes that bias — is settled and is not expected
to move. What moves, and quickly, is the content of review templates, the intervals themselves,
and the incentive frameworks attached to them; several systems are moving from single-disease
annual reviews toward combined reviews for people with more than one condition, and the evaluation
of that shift is still arriving. Remote and asynchronous review is now routine in places where it
was experimental, and its effect on the access gradient is actively contested. Take intervals,
variables and indicators from current local guidance rather than from here, as of October 2026.
