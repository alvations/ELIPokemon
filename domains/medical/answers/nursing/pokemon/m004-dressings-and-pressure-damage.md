# A Damp Rock does not make rain. It makes the rain last eight turns instead of five.

That sentence is the whole of what a dressing is. **Rain Dance** sets the weather for five turns.
A **Damp Rock** held by the Pokémon that sets it makes the same weather last **eight**. The rock
adds no power, changes no type match-up and heals nobody. Its single function is to **hold a
condition for longer**, and the games have an entire family of items that do nothing else:

```
   the item               what it holds                   what it does NOT do
   ─────────────────────  ──────────────────────────────  ────────────────────────────────
   Damp Rock              Rain Dance, 5 turns → 8          set the rain, or heal anything
   Heat Rock              Sunny Day, 5 → 8                 make the sun stronger
   Icy Rock               the hail, 5 → 8                  change what hail does
   Smooth Rock            Sandstorm, 5 → 8                 protect its holder from it
   Light Clay             Light Screen and Reflect, 5 → 8  improve either screen
   Terrain Extender       the terrains, 5 → 8              choose which terrain
```

The repair is the Pokémon's own, and the games are explicit about that too. **Regenerator**
restores a third of a Pokémon's maximum HP when it leaves the field. **Shed Skin** gives it a
one-in-three chance, every single turn, of clearing its own status. **Natural Cure** clears the
slot the moment it switches out. None of that is the weather's doing. The weather only decides
whether the Pokémon gets the chance.

## Moisture balance, and why the target is the middle

Both ends of the weather are a cost, and they are different costs — which is exactly why there are
rocks for both.

```
   TOO MUCH SUN                      right                        TOO MUCH RAIN
   ───────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────
   Solar Power raises     │  the condition you chose,  │  Swift Swim doubles the wrong
   Special Attack and     │  held for as long as you   │  Pokémon's Speed
   costs its holder HP    │  needed it and no longer   │  Fire moves weakened to half
   every single turn      │                            │  Thunder and Hurricane now
   Dry Skin takes EXTRA   │                            │  never miss
   damage in harsh sun    │                            │  Dry Skin holders gain HP
   ───────────────────────┴────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────
   what you are choosing:  bring moisture IN  ◄──────────►  take moisture OUT

   And there is a field that is simply hostile: Sandstorm takes a sixteenth of maximum
   HP off everything on the field every turn EXCEPT Rock, Ground and Steel types. A
   wound bed left in the wrong environment is in a Sandstorm.
```

So nobody picks the rock first. You decide which condition the Pokémon needs, and then you pick
the item that holds that condition — which is the right order for every dressing decision there
has ever been. And you look at the field before you decide: what tissue is there, whether anything
is inflamed, how wet it is, what the edges are doing.

## The other problem is load multiplied by turns

The binding moves are the sharpest mechanic in the games for this, because they are not about
damage per hit at all.

**Whirlpool**, Wrap, Bind, Fire Spin and Clamp all do the same three things: they take a share of
the target's HP at the end of every turn, they last several turns, and **the target cannot switch
out**. That last clause is the injury. Not the hit — the inability to get away from it.

And the games have an item for each half of the equation:

```
   Grip Claw       pins the hold at SEVEN turns          ◄── the TIME half
   Binding Band    deepens the share taken each turn     ◄── the PRESSURE half

   pressure
     ^
   high │ ■            a Binding Band bind, briefly
        │   ■
        │      ■
        │          ■ ■
        │                 ■  ■  ■            a Grip Claw bind, for seven turns
    low │                          ■  ■  ■  ■
        └─────────────────────────────────────────────────►  turns
                                                            held

   Both points are ON the curve. Hard and short, or soft and long, is the same damage.
   "It was only a few turns" is not an argument, and neither is "it was barely pressing".

   Three things make it worse than the arithmetic says:
     SLIDING    being dragged rather than lifted. Layers move against each other
     RUBBING    Rough Skin, Iron Barbs and a Rocky Helmet all hurt whatever makes
                CONTACT, regardless of how hard the hit was. Contact is its own damage
     THE FIELD  a Pokémon already sitting in the wrong weather tolerates less of
                everything else
```

**And the damage can land after the turn it was done.** **Future Sight** is used now and strikes
at the end of the second turn *after* it was used. In between, the field looks completely
untouched — no animation, no message, nothing on the HP bar. **Doom Desire** behaves the same way.
An attack has already been made, the arithmetic is already settled, and the screen will not show
you for two turns. That is the single most important thing to know about load injury: a patch of
skin that is discoloured but unbroken is a Future Sight that has already been used.

**Anatomy decides where it lands first.** **Onix** has a base Defence of **160** and a base HP of
**35**. Enormously hard on the outside, and almost nothing underneath to lose. Anywhere bone sits
close to the surface is an Onix, and that is where the first damage appears — the heels, the
sacrum, and every point under something you attached on purpose.

## Why clearing the field is the wrong idea

**Defog** removes entry hazards, removes **Light Screen** and **Reflect**, lowers the opponent's
evasion, and in the later games clears the terrain as well. It clears the field of effectively
everything you can see on it. It does **not** loosen a bind by a single turn.

That is the whole objection to treating load injury as a hygiene problem. The things you can see
and tidy are not the thing holding the Pokémon in place. What ends a bind is **Rapid Spin**, or
switching out — getting the load off. Nothing else in the game does it, and no amount of clearing
the visible field substitutes.

Which does not mean the field is irrelevant. A Pokémon in a **Sandstorm** is losing a sixteenth a
turn on top of the bind, so the weather genuinely makes the bind worse — it is simply not the
bind.

And the thing attached on purpose is often the thing doing harm. A **Life Orb** costs its holder a
share of maximum HP every time it attacks. **Black Sludge** damages anything that is not a
Poison-type. An **Iron Ball** halves Speed. Every one of those was equipped deliberately, for a
reason, and every one needs looking under.

How many turns you can leave a Pokémon in before it is too long is the one question this answer
will not answer, because it depends on the Pokémon — and in the clinical case the equivalent
interval is genuinely contested rather than settled.

## Where the metaphor stops

The two mechanisms above are mechanisms. What they are attached to is not.

Pressure damage hurts. It is slow, it can be deep, it can take months to close, it can be the
reason someone does not get home, and a large share of it is preventable. It is also one of the
few harms that is mostly a property of the care system rather than of the illness, which is why it
is counted and reported the way it is.

And the other half, which matters too: some of it happens despite entirely appropriate care, in
people whose perfusion or tissue tolerance cannot be fixed by anybody. Treating every instance as
somebody's failure is both untrue and corrosive to the reporting that prevention depends on.

## What Nurse Joy is listening for

Which direction a given item is moving the moisture, and what it looks like when the choice was
wrong in each direction. Why changing a dressing often is a cost and not a virtue. The difference
between damage from load and damage from being wet. Why an intact but discoloured patch over a
bony prominence is urgent rather than reassuring. Which of the things attached to this patient
need looking under. And what the local risk tool scores — and what it misses.

## Scope and safety

This explains two mechanisms, for someone already training in or qualified for clinical practice.
It is not a protocol, not a decision aid, and it names no dressing product and no repositioning
interval, deliberately — the only durations in it are a video game's. Dressing selection, risk
assessment, staging language and repositioning frequency are set by local policy and the local
formulary, which are the authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here
is for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care, including a decision about
anyone's own wound. If someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the correct
response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanisms are stable: keeping a wound in the right moisture range has been the foundation of
wound care for decades, and load injury has been understood as pressure multiplied by time for
longer. What dates is everything procedural — staging terminology, risk-assessment tools, support
surface classifications, repositioning recommendations, and above all the formulary, which changes
whenever procurement does. The reader's own policy is the authority for all of it. The rocks, by
contrast, have held five turns to eight since Sinnoh.
