# Spikes are not on the Pokémon. They are on the ground, and whoever laid them has gone.

**Spikes** is a move that does no damage when it is used. It damages every Pokémon that **switches
in** on that side of the field afterwards, for the rest of the battle, and the Pokémon that laid
it may have been recalled four turns ago. In the Advance games it stacks to three layers and the
arithmetic is printed in the code: one layer takes a maximum of an **eighth**, two layers a
**sixth**, three layers a **quarter**, and it does not touch a Flying-type or anything with
**Levitate** because they never land on the ground where it is.

Everything about infection prevention is in that one move. The hazard is in the **environment**,
not in the Pokémon. It fires at a **moment of transition**, not continuously. It is paid for by
whoever comes next, not by whoever created it. And it is invisible: nothing on the battle screen
in those games tells you how many layers are down. You either watched it being set or you are
guessing.

## The hazards are a reservoir, and each one needs a different answer

The games built out a whole family of these, and the differences between them are the whole
lesson:

```
   hazard           what it does to whatever switches in        who it skips
   ───────────────  ──────────────────────────────────────────  ─────────────────────────
   Spikes           a share of maximum HP — an eighth, a        Flying-types and
                    sixth or a quarter by layer count          Levitate: never grounded
   Toxic Spikes     poisons it. TWO layers and it badly        anything not grounded —
                    poisons it instead, which is the           and a grounded POISON-type
                    climbing counter, not the flat one         ABSORBS them and clears
                                                                the field as it arrives
   Stealth Rock     damage scaled by TYPE EFFECTIVENESS, so    nobody. It is the one
                    the same rock costs different Pokémon      that reaches everything
                    wildly different amounts
   Sticky Web       drops Speed by one stage on arrival.       anything not grounded
                    No damage at all — it changes what the
                    Pokémon can do, not what it has
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Four hazards on the same patch of ground, and NOT ONE TOOL CLEARS ALL OF THEM the
   same way. Rapid Spin clears your OWN side. Defog, whose printed description is
   "Removes obstacles and lowers evasion", clears both sides — and takes Light Screen,
   Reflect, Mist and Safeguard with it, so it removes things you wanted alongside things
   you did not. And a grounded POISON-type absorbs Toxic Spikes on arrival while doing
   absolutely nothing about the Spikes or the Stealth Rock sitting beside them.
```

That last line is the point about product choice. The tool has to match the hazard. A cleaning
move that is right for three of the four and useless against the fourth is not a general solution,
and the Pokémon that walks in assuming it was is the one that pays.

## The route is contact, and the games are explicit about it

A large group of moves are flagged as making **contact**, and a matching group of effects fire on
contact and on nothing else. **Rough Skin** and **Iron Barbs** both damage whatever touches their
holder; a **Rocky Helmet** does the same from the item slot; a **Sticky Barb** moves to the
attacker outright. None of them cares how powerful the hit was. Contact is the whole trigger.

Which makes **Long Reach** the most interesting ability in the games for this subject. It does one
thing: the Pokémon's moves **stop making contact**. It does not armour anybody. It removes the
route. That is the difference between a control that protects a person and a control that breaks a
chain, and the second kind is rarer and better.

## The equipment protects the wearer. Read the item text.

```
   Heavy-Duty Boots      "Protects from the effects of traps set on the field."
   Protective Pads       "Protects from effects triggered by making contact."
```

Both of those are the real printed descriptions, and both say **the holder**. The Boots walk a
Pokémon through three layers of Spikes without a scratch and leave all three layers **exactly
where they were** for the next one. The Pads stop the Rocky Helmet hurting their holder and do not
stop the contact happening. Neither item removes anything from the field. Neither item helps the
Pokémon behind you.

That is gloves, and it is the commonest confusion in the subject. Equipment on one party is not a
break in the chain. **Rapid Spin** is a break in the chain. The Boots are a pair of boots.

## Why it is a systems problem and not a reading problem

Every player knows what Rapid Spin does. The hazards still sit there for whole battles, and the
reasons are structural.

**The cost scales with exactly the thing that makes you switch.** Hazards fire on switch-in. You
switch more when you are in trouble — a bad match-up, a status you need to clear, something you
need off the field. So the hazard bill rises precisely in the battles where you can least afford
it. The more dependent the position, the more crossings, and every crossing is charged. Nothing
about the player's diligence changes that arithmetic.

**The tool has a budget.** **Rapid Spin** carries **40** PP, the most generous in this whole set,
and **Defog** carries **15**. If clearing the field is something that must happen dozens of times
across a long fight, the constraint is not whether you remember — it is whether the resource is
there when the moment arrives. A defence you have to walk a long way to reach is a defence with a
smaller budget than the one printed on it.

**Whoever sets the hazard pays nothing.** The side that laid the Spikes is not standing on them.
The cost falls entirely on the other side of the field, turn after turn, long after the Pokémon
that set them has been switched out and forgotten. Every structure in which the person creating
the hazard is not the person paying for it behaves this way.

**The field count is not on screen.** In the Advance games the number of Spikes layers is held in
a side timer and displayed nowhere. You are reading your memory of the field, not the field. Any
figure you quote about how clean a field is, is a figure about your measurement.

**And one move does not carry the battle.** Rapid Spin clears hazards from your own side and frees
the user from a bind. It does nothing to the weather, nothing to the terrain, nothing to the
opponent's stat stages, nothing about a **Toxic Orb** a Pokémon is holding, and nothing about a
**Leech Seed** on the Pokémon in the back. Loading the whole field onto the one move you can name
is how a side loses while the thing it measured looked fine.

## Where the metaphor stops

The mechanism above is a mechanism, and a game is a fair way to explain it. What it is attached to
is not.

People acquire infections in healthcare settings that they did not arrive with. Some are mild,
some cost weeks of recovery and a lost discharge date, and some kill. A meaningful share is
preventable, which is the whole reason this apparatus exists and the reason it is audited rather
than merely encouraged.

And the person who missed a moment at three in the morning, on a short-staffed ward, at an empty
dispenser, is not the cause of an outbreak. Treating them as the cause is both untrue and
expensive: it removes the information the institution needed and leaves the empty dispenser
exactly where it was. Both are true at once, and holding both is the point of teaching this as a
system rather than as a virtue.

## What Nurse Joy is listening for

Which of the four hazards a given tool actually clears, and which it leaves. Why the moment of
crossing onto the field is the trigger, and why the moment that counts for the *surroundings* is
the one everybody skips. What the Boots do and, more importantly, what they leave behind for
whoever switches in after you. Why the hazard bill is biggest in exactly the battles you are
already losing. How many layers are down on this field, and whether that is knowledge or a guess.
And what else was on the field the last time somebody went through the whole thing properly.

## Scope and safety

This explains why one control measure is designed and implemented the way it is, for someone
already training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a decision aid,
and it names no product, no contact time and no compliance target, deliberately — the only numbers
in it belong to a video game. The infection prevention policy of the institution where someone
works is the authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here is for use in
an emergency or for a decision about any person's care. If someone is unwell right now, the local
emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The chain of transmission and the patient-zone reasoning are stable and have been for a long time.
What dates is everything around them: product formulations and contact times, which organisms
trigger soap and water, glove indications, the dress code, respiratory precautions, audit
methodology and the compliance figures themselves. Several of those moved substantially during and
after the coronavirus pandemic, so the local policy is the authority rather than anyone's memory
of a lecture. Spikes, meanwhile, has been sitting on one side of the field since Johto, it has
stacked to three layers since the Advance games, and the layer count has been invisible on screen
the whole time.
