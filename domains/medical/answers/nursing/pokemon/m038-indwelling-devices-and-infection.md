# A Pokémon holds exactly one item, and nothing in the games ever asks whether it still needs it.

One slot. That single design decision is what makes every held item a **trade** rather than a
bonus, because the question is never "is this item good" — it is "is this item better than the
slot being empty, and better than every other item, **for as long as it stays on**." And the games
give you no prompt to ask it again. You equip the item, the battle ends, the item is still there.
Three badges later it is still there.

## The item that is both the benefit and the harm, by design

**Sticky Barb**, whose printed description in the game is worth reading exactly as it stands:

```
   Damages the holder each turn. Latches on from contact.
```

Two clauses and both are the subject of this question. The code puts the first at **an eighth of
maximum HP at the end of every turn**, every turn it is held, exempting only a holder with **Magic
Guard**. And the second fires when an attacker makes **contact** and is **holding nothing** — at
which point the Barb simply moves across and becomes theirs.

That is a device. A standing per-turn cost to whoever has it, and a transmission route to whoever
handles it without their own protection. Nothing about the Barb is a one-off event. It is a rate.

```
   WHERE THE COST COMES FROM, and why the second column is the one that grows
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   THE MOMENT IT GOES ON              EVERY TURN AFTERWARDS
     one event, one decision            an eighth of maximum HP, end of turn,
     either the right item for this      every turn, for as long as it is held
     Pokémon or the wrong one           and a contact route to the next
                                         Pokémon that touches it
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Grip Claw pins a bind at SEVEN turns.  Binding Band deepens the share taken
   each turn. Same two axes as the pressure problem — TIME and INTENSITY — and
   for a held item the one you actually control is the number of turns.
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
     a fixed cost      vs      a rate × turns
   The first is paid once by whoever equipped it. The second is paid by everyone
   who has the Pokémon afterwards, and it grows while nobody does anything at all.
```

## The item that is a real gain and takes three options away

**Choice Band**, again in the game's own words: *"Boosts Attack, but permits only one move."*
**Choice Specs** and **Choice Scarf** do the same for Special Attack and Speed. The boost is real
— these are among the strongest items in competitive play. And the moment the Pokémon attacks,
three of its four moves are gone for as long as the item is held.

That is the shape of the harm nobody writes down. The cost of a device is not only what it does to
the body; it is **what it stops you being able to do**. **Iron Ball** says it outright — *"Cuts
Speed and makes Ground moves hit the holder"* — a device that slows you down and changes what else
can reach you. A **Macho Brace** *"Promotes growth, but reduces Speed in battle"*: a straight
trade of present capability for future capability, named in the description.

## Whether it helps depends entirely on who is holding it

**Black Sludge** restores HP to a **Poison-type** holder and damages everything else. Same object,
opposite sign, decided by the pair and not by the item. An indication is a property of the match,
not a property of the thing — which is why "a Black Sludge is a good item" is not a sentence that
means anything on its own.

And the counter-example that shows what a good decision looks like: an **Everstone** is a held
item whose entire function is to **prevent** something. It is deliberate, it is the right call in
the situations it is the right call for, and it sits in the item field where the next Trainer will
see it. A documented decision reads as a decision. An undocumented one reads as an oversight, and
the Pokémon that has been carrying a Sticky Barb for a hundred turns because nobody checked looks
exactly like the Pokémon that is carrying it on purpose.

## Nothing prompts the review, so the review has to be the habit

There is no screen in these games that asks whether a Pokémon still needs its item. The slot shows
what is in it and never editorialises. Removal is an **action somebody has to take**: **Knock
Off** *"Deals more damage to a foe with an item and removes it"*, **Trick** *"Tricks the foe into
trading held items"*, or simply going into the party screen and taking the thing off. All of those
require somebody to have had the thought.

So the failure mode is never a bad decision. It is an **inherited** one. Item slots do not get
reviewed because reviewing them was nobody's turn, and the per-turn cost in the diagram above
keeps accruing on something that was equipped for a reason that stopped applying four towns ago.

## The abnormal reading that needs no response at all

**Pokérus** puts a marker on the status screen. It cannot be cleared at a **Pokémon Center**, it
looks like something has gone wrong, and what it is actually doing is doubling what the Pokémon
gains from every battle.

That is the shape of colonisation. A positive finding, on the screen, on something that has been
carrying a device for weeks — expected, not the same as the thing that hurts, and acting on it as
though it were is how the real problem goes unexamined. Reaching for the bag because a marker is
present is how a Trainer spends a turn on the marker and loses to whatever the Pokémon was
actually struggling with.

## Where the metaphor stops

The mechanisms above are mechanisms. What they are attached to is not.

Device-associated infections are common, they are a substantial share of all healthcare-associated
infection, and some of them are fatal. They are also among the most preventable, which is why they
are counted and reported in a way most hospital-acquired harm is not.

Alongside that: for some people an indwelling device is what makes life tolerable, and a long-term
catheter can be the right answer rather than a failure of care. A culture that treats every device
as a defect gets the easy half right and the hard half wrong. And the discomfort, the indignity
and the loss of independence that a device imposes are experienced by a person, not recorded as a
rate — which is exactly why they get left out of the ledger that decides whether it stays in. That
omission is worth naming without a costume on.

## What Nurse Joy is listening for

Which half of the cost is paid at the moment the item goes on and which half is paid every turn
after. Why the number of turns is the term that grows, and what the stopping question was at the
moment it was equipped. What a Choice Band takes away as well as what it adds, and who that costs.
Why the same object has opposite signs on two different Pokémon. Which item on this Pokémon is an
Everstone — a decision someone recorded — and which is just still there. And what a Pokérus marker
does and does not justify spending a turn on.

## Scope and safety

This explains why a class of device carries the risk it does, for someone already training in or
qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a decision aid, and it names no review
interval, no insertion technique and no infection rate, deliberately — the only numbers in it
belong to a video game. The catheter and vascular access policies of the institution where someone
works are the authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here is for use
in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care, including any decision about anyone's
own device. If someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanisms are stable: a breach, a surface, a per-day rate. What dates is the procedural layer
— which bundle elements are mandated, the review interval and whether it is prompted
electronically, device materials and coatings and the repeatedly disappointing evidence for them,
phlebitis scoring tools, and the local surveillance definitions that decide what counts as a
device-associated infection at all. Those definitions matter more than they look, because a
reported rate is only comparable to a rate collected under the same definition. The one-item rule,
meanwhile, has held since held items first existed in Johto, and there has still never been a
screen that asks whether the item is still needed.
