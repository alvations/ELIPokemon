# A traded Pokémon arrives with its whole record and none of your history with it.

When a Pokémon changes hands, the handover is not a conversation. It is a **transfer**, and the
game is explicit about what transfers: the species, the level, the four moves and their PP, the
held item, the status slot, the **Original Trainer**'s name and the **ID No.** it was caught
under. A fixed set of fields, in a fixed order, on pages the receiving Trainer can turn without
being told what to look for. That fixed order is the entire point of a structured handover — not
that it remembers for you, but that it will not let you skip a heading.

What does **not** transfer is the reason a threshold exists. **A Pokémon from a trade obeys only
up to a written level ceiling set by your badges**, and the game works this out by comparing the
**ID No.** on the Pokémon against the **ID No.** on your **Trainer Card**. If they differ, it is
an outsider. You have the creature. You do not have the relationship, and the game will not
pretend otherwise.

Two of those deserve expanding.

**The interpretation is the limb that gets skipped, and it is the valuable one.** **Magikarp**
evolves into **Gyarados** at level 20 — that is a fact about the species, checkable, and the
summary screen does not say it. A Trainer who hands over "it's a Magikarp, it knows **Splash**"
has told you the truth and nothing else. A Trainer who says "it's five levels off a Gyarados, keep
it alive" has handed over their reading, and their reading is the part you cannot reconstruct from
the screen. The sender might be wrong about how many levels. They are still more use than silence.

**The ask has to name the action and the moment.** **Helping Hand** is the cleanest example in the
games of a request that fails unless it is specific: it boosts an ally's move, but only if it is
used on the same turn, by the right partner, in the right direction. A request with the timing
left out is not a weaker request. It is a different thing that does not work.

**And the loop closes before the transfer completes.** A trade shows both Pokémon to both
Trainers, and both have to confirm. Nobody hands a Pokémon over on the strength of having said its
name.

## Handing over a party and shouting for help are different problems

Handing over six Pokémon at once is **broad and unhurried**: the risk is that one of the six gets
missed entirely, and the defence is a list you work through. Shouting that the Pokémon on the
field is in trouble is **narrow and urgent**: the risk is that the severity does not transmit, and
the defence is leading with it. Using the party-handover habit for the second one is how a **Badly
Poisoned** Pokémon at counter six gets mentioned fourth.

## Why the criteria are numbers

The games write the ceiling down. Not "a strong Trainer's commands are respected" — an actual
ladder. This is the **Kanto** one, and every player who ever picked up those games got the same
numbers:

```
   badges you hold                     a traded Pokémon obeys up to
   ──────────────────────────────────  ──────────────────────────────────────────────
   none                                level 10
   the Cascade Badge                   level 30
   the Rainbow Badge                   level 50
   the Marsh Badge                     level 70
   the Earth Badge                     everything, to the level cap of 100

   and note the shape of it: ABOVE the ceiling the Pokémon MAY disobey, on a roll.
   The threshold does not promise an outcome. It governs when the risk starts.
   Later regions set their own numbers — which is the point, not an objection.
```

That ladder does five things a Trainer's judgement cannot.

It is **the same for everyone** — a **Youngster** on **Route 1** and a Champion read the identical
number, so confidence stops being an input. It is a **mandate rather than a request**, which
matters because the real barrier is social: a **Youngster** does not want to be the one who
bothers **Lance**, and a written ceiling means they do not have to justify it. It **specifies the
response**, not merely that something is wrong. It is **checkable afterwards**, because the badge
case and the level are both recorded. And it is **teachable on the first day**, which is the whole
point — a new Trainer can be safe before they are experienced.

Published limits are everywhere in the games for exactly this reason. Stat stages run from −6 to
+6 and no further; the level cap is 100; a Pokémon holds one status condition and not two. Nobody
negotiates any of these, and nobody has to.

## Where thresholds fail

**They are written for the general case and applied to a particular Pokémon.** The fix is a
documented exception attached to the Pokémon itself, and the games have exactly this: an
**Everstone** is a held item whose function is to prevent evolution. It is a decision, made
deliberately, recorded in the item field where the next Trainer will see it. An undocumented
exception is indistinguishable from a mistake.

**"Under the ceiling" gets read as "fine".** The ladder says when a Pokémon *may* stop obeying. It
says nothing at all about whether this one is in trouble. A level 8 Pokémon with no badges will
obey perfectly while being **Badly Poisoned** at counter nine.

**A format can be performed instead of used.** Turning all three pages and saying nothing is worse
than a shambolic handover, because it looks finished. The test is whether the receiving Trainer
can say what the plan is and what the worry is.

## Where the metaphor stops

The mechanism above is a mechanism, and a game is a fair way to explain it. What it is attached to
is not.

Reviews of patients who deteriorated unexpectedly keep finding the same thing, and it is not that
nobody noticed. Somebody noticed. The concern existed, in the building, in a person who was
worried about it, and it did not reach the person who could act while there was still time. Every
structured handover format and every written trigger value in clinical practice is a response to
that one failure. A format that feels like bureaucracy at the end of a thirteen-hour shift is the
accumulated weight of the times it did not happen, and that is worth saying without a costume on.

## What Nurse Joy is listening for

Which page of the record carries the sender's reading, and what to say there when unsure. The
difference between handing over a party and shouting about the one on the field. What the ladder
at this Center actually specifies — who comes, and how fast. How to keep pushing when a worry is
waved off. And how an Everstone-shaped exception gets written down so the next Trainer sees a
decision rather than an oversight.

## Scope and safety

This explains why two communication tools are shaped the way they are, for someone already
training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol and not a decision aid, and
it deliberately names no clinical trigger values, because escalation criteria and response times
are set institutionally and are revised. The handover standard and escalation policy where someone
works is the authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here is for use in
an emergency or for a decision about any person's care. If someone is unwell right now, the local
emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The reasoning is stable: transfers lose information, fixed fields defend against specific losses,
and a written number transfers between people where judgement does not. What dates is the
lettering — clinical handover formats are revised, renamed and extended, trigger values and
response ladders are reissued, and electronic tools change what is automatic and what must still
be said out loud. The authority is the card on the reader's own ward. The Kanto badge ladder,
unlike the card, was written down once and anyone can still read exactly what it says.
