# The chart is a ledger of measured volumes. The patient is not a ledger.

A fluid balance chart records the volumes somebody measured going in — oral and enteral intake,
intravenous fluids, intravenous drugs and their diluents, line flushes, blood products — and the
volumes somebody measured coming out: urine, vomit or nasogastric aspirate, stoma output, drain
output, measured stool. It subtracts the second from the first and carries a cumulative balance
forward. *What belongs in each column is consensus across major guidance; the design of the chart
and how the cumulative total is presented are local.*

Two things follow immediately and are worth saying before anything else. **The chart is a trend
instrument, and its unit of meaning is days, not shifts.** And **every quantity on it is a
measured volume**, which means the chart's relationship to the patient's actual fluid state is
mediated by everything that was not measured.

```
   ┌──────────── MEASURED, and therefore on the chart ───────────────────────────┐
   │  IN                                   OUT                                   │
   │  oral and enteral intake              urine                                 │
   │  intravenous fluids                   vomit / nasogastric aspirate          │
   │  drugs and their diluents             stoma output                          │
   │  line flushes                         drain output                          │
   │  blood products                       measured stool                        │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                 │                                      ▲
                 │                                      │
   ┌─────────────▼──────────────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────┐
   │  NOT MEASURED, and therefore invisible to the arithmetic                    │
   │                                                                             │
   │  INSENSIBLE LOSSES — respiratory and transcutaneous water, continuous,      │
   │    and INCREASED by fever, tachypnoea, a warm environment, large open       │
   │    wounds and burns. The sicker the patient, the larger the thing the       │
   │    chart cannot see                                                         │
   │  UNRECORDED INTAKE — sips, a relative's cup of tea, a drink at a            │
   │    bedside nobody charted                                                   │
   │  UNRECORDED OUTPUT — sweat, incontinence, a leaking dressing or drain,      │
   │    diarrhoea that was not measured                                          │
   │  DISTRIBUTION — see below. This is the big one                              │
   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Distribution: why a balanced chart is not a reassuring chart

The chart counts fluid crossing the boundary of the body. It says nothing whatever about **where
inside the body** that fluid now is, and that is the question clinical decisions actually turn on.

```
   total body water                       the compartment that matters
   ┌──────────────────────────────┐       ┌──────────────────────┐
   │                              │       │  INTRAVASCULAR       │
   │   interstitial  ████████████ │       │  ░░░                 │  ◄── perfusion
   │   oedema, ascites, pleural   │       │                      │      comes from
   │   fluid, fluid in the gut    │       │  depleted            │      HERE only
   │                              │       └──────────────────────┘
   │   intravascular  ░░░         │
   └──────────────────────────────┘
          overloaded in total                 and empty where it counts

   A patient can be SIMULTANEOUSLY fluid-overloaded and intravascularly depleted, and
   it is common — capillary leak in sepsis, low plasma oncotic pressure, accumulation
   in a third space. The chart shows a positive cumulative balance. The circulation is
   underfilled. BOTH readings are correct, because they are measuring different things.
```

This is the central reason the chart cannot be used alone: *that overload and intravascular
depletion coexist is consensus and is mechanism rather than guideline*, and no arithmetic on
measured volumes can distinguish the two.

**Weight is the better integrator.** Daily weight, on the same scales, at the same time, in
comparable clothing, captures everything the ledger missed, because mass is conserved and water is
heavy — over days, a change in weight is overwhelmingly a change in water. *This is consensus.*
Its limits are honest ones: different scales disagree, it is impractical for some patients, and
over weeks it starts to include real tissue mass change. Over days it is the strongest single
measurement of fluid state available at a bedside.

## Urine output as a circulation signal

The kidney receives a large share of cardiac output and is highly sensitive to a fall in
perfusion, so **urine output works as an early surrogate for circulatory adequacy** — not as a
measure of it. When perfusion falls, whether because the circulation is underfilled or because the
heart is not delivering, urine output tends to fall before blood pressure does. *This is consensus
across major guidance, and it is why urine output is monitored at all rather than merely
totalled.*

What breaks the inference, and every one of these matters:

```
   observation                        what it might mean instead of hypovolaemia
   ─────────────────────────────────  ──────────────────────────────────────────────
   output is low                      a BLOCKED OR KINKED CATHETER. Mechanically the
                                      commonest explanation on a ward, and the first
                                      thing checked before anything is concluded
                                      about the kidney
   output is low                      intrinsic kidney injury, or obstruction above
                                      the bladder; also normal antidiuresis in a
                                      post-operative patient
   output looks adequate              an osmotic diuresis — high blood glucose, or
                                      contrast — so volume is being lost, not
                                      conserved
   output looks adequate              a diuretic has been given, which decouples
                                      output from perfusion entirely
   output looks adequate              impaired concentrating ability, common with age
                                      and in chronic kidney disease, so a normal
                                      volume carries less information
   output rises sharply               post-obstructive diuresis, which is a finding
                                      to act on rather than a reassurance
```

*Thresholds for oliguria are expressed per kilogram per hour and are set by local guidance;* they
are deliberately not stated here, because the numbers differ between definitions and between
institutions and the principle does not depend on them.

## Where the chart fails as a measurement

**Completion.** *Audits of fluid chart completeness repeatedly find them incomplete,* and the
cumulative total inherits the error of the worst-documented shift. A chart with one blank column
is not a chart with slightly less information; the cumulative balance is simply wrong from that
point forward, and silently.

**Granularity.** Volumes are recorded in round numbers from vessels of approximate capacity. The
chart is accurate to something much coarser than the figure it prints.

**It is read as a verdict rather than a trend.** A single shift's balance is noise. The slope
across days is the measurement.

**It is not in the early warning score.** Fluid state is absent from the standard composite
observation scores, which is a design limitation of those scores and not an indication that it
does not matter. The two instruments are read together.

So the chart belongs inside a composite clinical assessment, alongside heart rate, blood pressure
and pulse pressure, capillary refill time, peripheral temperature, mucous membranes, jugular
venous pressure, consciousness, and the relevant biochemistry. *Skin turgor is unreliable in older
people and this is consensus* — it is one of the first bedside signs taught and one of the least
dependable.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Getting this wrong harms people in both directions, and the second direction is the one that gets
underestimated.

Under-replacement causes hypoperfusion, kidney injury, delirium, falls, and in the frail older
patient a cascade that is hard to reverse. Over-replacement causes pulmonary oedema,
breathlessness, prolonged ventilation, poor wound healing and an oedematous patient who cannot
mobilise. A fluid chart is a tedious piece of paperwork that is, in practice, one of the few
continuously collected measurements of whether a patient's circulation is being managed correctly,
and it is among the most commonly incomplete documents on a ward. Both of those facts are true at
once and the second is the reason the first is worth insisting on.

## Scope and safety

This explains what a measurement is and is not, for someone already training in or qualified for
clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a decision aid, and it contains no volumes, no
replacement regimens and no thresholds, deliberately. Fluid prescribing, oliguria definitions and
escalation points are set by local guidance, which is the authority; this is not, and it has had
no clinical review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's
care, including any decision about how much anyone should drink. If someone is unwell right now,
the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physiology does not move: compartments, insensible losses, the kidney's sensitivity to
perfusion, and the conservation of mass that makes weight the better integrator. What dates is the
guidance wrapped around it — fluid therapy recommendations, the choice and composition of
replacement fluids, acute kidney injury definitions and their urine output criteria, and the chart
design itself, which is increasingly electronic and in some places automatically totalled. The
reader's own policy and chart are the authority for every number.
