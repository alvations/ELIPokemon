# Nobody fails hand hygiene because they did not know.

Ask a clinical team to name the moments for hand hygiene and most of them will get it right. Then
measure what they do, and the number is lower — reliably, everywhere, in every country that
publishes it. That gap is the whole subject. If the deficit were knowledge, teaching would close
it, and decades of teaching have not. What closes it is changing the conditions under which the
behaviour is performed, which is a different kind of work and belongs to a different kind of
person than the one standing at the bedside.

Markers used below: **mechanism** means checkable by reasoning; **definitional** means it is what
the word means; (**consensus**) means mainstream agreement across major guidance;
(**country-dependent**) means the reader's own policy decides.

## The thing hand hygiene is one link in

Transmission is a chain, and every control measure is an attempt to break one link. The chain is
(**definitional**) — it is the framework the discipline is taught in — and the controls attached
to it are (**consensus**):

Two things follow immediately. Hand hygiene sits on the **commonest** mode of transmission, which
is why it is described as the single most effective measure — that framing is (**consensus**). And
it is **one** link of five, which is why an outbreak is never explained by it alone, and why an
institution that responds to one by retraining staff has usually left the actual cause in place.

## Why the moments are defined the way they are

The structure most of the world teaches is the World Health Organization's five moments, which are
anchored to **the patient zone** rather than to tasks: before touching a patient, before a clean
or aseptic procedure, after exposure to body fluid, after touching a patient, and after touching
the patient's surroundings. *Definitional, and the authority is the WHO document itself.*

The design decision worth understanding is the last one. Including the patient's surroundings
makes the bed rail, the pump, the notes and the chair part of the reservoir, which is a claim
about where organisms live — and it is the moment most often missed, because nothing was *touched*
in the way people intuitively mean. Anchoring to a zone rather than to tasks also means the count
of indicated moments is a property of the care, not of the clinician. A dependent patient
generates more of them per hour than an independent one (**mechanism**).

Which produces the first systems problem, and it is arithmetic. **The number of required moments
rises with dependency and with how many patients one person is covering, while the time available
per moment falls.** Compliance therefore degrades fastest in exactly the conditions under which
transmission is most likely. No amount of individual conscientiousness changes that. Staffing
does.

## The rest of the systems problem

**Placement.** A moment that requires walking competes with the next task, and loses. Handrub at
the point of care — within reach without leaving the patient's side — is the most consistently
identified facilitator in the implementation literature, and it is a procurement and estates
decision rather than a clinical one. Sink number, sink siting and whether sinks are used for
anything else are the same kind of decision (**consensus**).

**Gloves, which are the commonest confusion in the topic.** Gloves protect the wearer. They do not
protect the next patient. They become contaminated exactly as hands do, they can be perforated,
and hands can be contaminated during removal — so hand hygiene is required after glove removal,
not instead of it. And glove use is repeatedly associated with *lower* hand hygiene compliance,
which makes unnecessary gloving an infection-control problem rather than a neutral extra.
*Consensus, and the association is from observational research rather than guideline assertion.*

**Product against organism.** Alcohol-based handrub is the default because it is fast, effective
against most relevant organisms, and kinder to skin than repeated washing. It is **not** effective
against bacterial spores, so soap and water is required where spore-forming organisms are in
question — and for visibly soiled hands, where the mechanism is physical removal rather than
microbial kill. *Mechanism, and consensus as to which organisms.* Which organisms and situations
trigger the switch locally is (**country-dependent**).

**Skin.** Repeated hand hygiene, dozens to hundreds of times a shift, causes irritant dermatitis;
dermatitis is painful, reduces compliance, and increases bacterial carriage on damaged skin.
Emollient provision is therefore an infection-prevention intervention rather than a staff perk
(**mechanism**).

**Dress and nails.** Nail length, artificial nails and nail extensions, and hand and wrist
jewellery all interfere with hand hygiene, and restrictions on them are widely agreed. The broader
bare-below-the-elbows requirement is policy in some countries and not in others, and the evidence
for the sleeve component specifically is weaker than the evidence about nails. *Country-dependent
— name which it is rather than asserting one.*

**Hierarchy.** Audited compliance is often lowest in the most senior clinical groups, and juniors
copy what they see. A student who cannot safely prompt a consultant has a communication problem,
not a knowledge problem, and the fix is the one that fixes escalation: a written mandate, so the
junior does not have to justify themselves.

**Measurement, which is weaker than it looks.** Direct observation is the reference standard and
is subject to the Hawthorne effect — people perform better while watched, so observed compliance
overstates actual compliance. Automated systems count dispenser activations, which is not the same
as counting indicated moments, so the denominator is estimated. A published compliance figure is a
statement about a measurement method as much as about a ward. *Consensus; this is the claim here
most worth reading the primary literature on.*

## The failure mode that matters most

Loading the whole chain onto one behaviour. Hand hygiene cannot substitute for environmental
cleaning, for removing a device that is no longer needed, for not sharing a tourniquet, for
isolating appropriately, for ventilation, or for antimicrobial stewardship. When an outbreak is
investigated and the finding is a hand hygiene compliance percentage, the investigation has
usually stopped one layer too early — because that number is available and the others take work.

## The human stakes, said plainly

People acquire infections in healthcare settings that they did not arrive with. Some are mild,
some cost weeks of recovery and a lost discharge date, and some kill. A meaningful share is
preventable, which is why this apparatus exists and why it is audited rather than merely
encouraged.

And the person who missed a moment at three in the morning, on a short-staffed ward, at an empty
dispenser, is not the cause of an outbreak. Treating them as the cause is both untrue and
expensive: it removes the information the institution needed and leaves the empty dispenser
exactly where it was. Both statements are true at once, and holding both is the point of teaching
this as a system.

## What an examiner digs into next

Which five moments the local policy names and whether it adds any. Why the moment after touching
the patient's surroundings exists, and why it is the one most often missed. What gloves do and do
not do, and what is required after removing them. Which situations locally require soap and water
rather than handrub, and why. How compliance is measured on the reader's own ward, and what that
method over- and under-counts. And what else was in the chain the last time an outbreak was
investigated there.

## Scope and safety

This explains why one control measure is designed and implemented the way it is, for someone
already training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a decision aid,
and it names no product, no contact time and no compliance target, deliberately. The infection
prevention policy of the institution where someone works is the authority for all of it; this is
not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision
about any person's care. If someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the correct
response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The chain of transmission and the patient-zone reasoning are stable and have been for a long time.
What dates is everything around them: product formulations and their contact times, which
organisms trigger soap and water, glove indications, the dress code, respiratory precautions,
audit methodology and the compliance figures themselves. All of those are reissued, and several
moved substantially during and after the coronavirus pandemic — a reader taught one version should
treat the local policy as the authority rather than their memory of a lecture.
