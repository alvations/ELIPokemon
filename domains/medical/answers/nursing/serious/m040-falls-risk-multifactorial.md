# The factors multiply. That is why removing one of them moves almost nothing.

The reason falls prevention looks like a field full of disappointing trials is arithmetic, and
stating the arithmetic first makes the rest of the topic obvious.

A fall happens when the demand on balance exceeds the capacity to maintain it, at a particular
moment, in a particular place. Both sides of that are products of several independent terms.
Vision, lower-limb strength, gait, postural blood pressure, cognition, continence and urgency,
footwear, the drug chart, the acute illness, and the environment each multiply the hazard rather
than adding to it. *Mechanism, and this is the framing that makes the trial literature make
sense.*

If risk is a product, then removing one factor divides the risk by that factor's contribution and
leaves everything else untouched. A single-intervention study therefore reports a small effect and
a wide confidence interval — not because the intervention did nothing, but because it could only
ever have changed one term. *Mechanism; the empirical finding that single interventions
underperform multifactorial ones in most settings is consensus.*

Markers used below: **mechanism** means checkable by reasoning; **consensus** means mainstream
agreement across major guidance; *contested* means the field genuinely disagrees;
(**country-dependent**) means the reader's own guidance and law decide.

## Why the score is less useful than the assessment

This is the part that examiners press on, and the honest answer is uncomfortable.

Falls risk prediction tools have modest discrimination, and on a ward of frail older people almost
everybody scores as high risk — so the score separates nobody from anybody and consumes time that
the assessment needed. *Consensus; the direction is well established and the magnitude varies by
tool and population.* Several national guidance documents have moved away from recommending
numerical risk scoring in inpatient settings and toward treating older inpatients as at risk by
default and going straight to the multifactorial assessment. *Country-dependent — this is exactly
the kind of recommendation that differs between national bodies.*

The useful output of a falls assessment is not a number. It is a **list of modifiable things, with
a name against each**. A score tells you that risk exists, which you already knew. The list tells
you which term in the product you can actually divide.

## The interventions, and what each one can and cannot touch

The evidence base here is genuinely uneven and a good answer says which parts are solid:

* **Exercise with a strength and balance component** has the most consistent effect of any single
  component, in community-dwelling older people (**consensus**). Its evidence in short-stay
  inpatient settings is weaker, which is a different question rather than a contradiction.
* **Medication review** addresses the channel that is most modifiable and most frequently left
  alone — psychotropics, opioids, anything causing orthostatic hypotension, and polypharmacy
  itself (**consensus**).
* **Multifactorial assessment followed by individually targeted intervention** is the approach
  that national guidance generally recommends, for exactly the arithmetic reason above.
  *Consensus; effect sizes in hospital settings are contested.*
* **Vitamin D, hip protectors and flooring** each have an evidence base that is contested in
  magnitude, in population, or both. Naming them as contested is more useful than asserting either
  way. *Contested.*
* **Bed and chair alarms** have repeatedly disappointed. They shift work, they add to alarm load,
  and the alarm-fatigue argument from medicines safety applies here unchanged. *Contested, leaning
  negative.*

## Why preventing a fall can be the injury

Immobilising someone reduces observed falls. It is also, past a certain point, the harm.

**Deconditioning is fast.** Bed rest in an older person produces measurable loss of muscle mass,
strength and aerobic capacity within days, not weeks, and the lost strength is slower to rebuild
than to lose. A person kept safely in a chair for a week can leave less able to walk than when
they arrived — and now with a higher falls risk than the one that was being managed. *Mechanism,
and consensus.*

**Restraint, including bed rails used as restraint.** Rails do not reliably prevent falls and are
associated with injury when a person climbs over them, with agitation, with pressure damage and
with immobility. Beyond the clinical picture, restraint is a legal and capacity question: whether
a restriction is lawful depends on the person's capacity, on best-interests determination and on
the deprivation-of-liberty framework of the jurisdiction. *Country-dependent, profoundly so, and
not something to decide from a nursing textbook.*

**And the blame asymmetry, which shapes behaviour more than any guideline.** A fall is an incident
and gets investigated. A person discharged to residential care because they lost the ability to
walk during their admission is not an incident and gets investigated by nobody. The consequence is
predictable: the pressure runs one way, toward restriction, and the staff member who encouraged
someone to walk feels exposed in a way that the staff member who did not does not. Any falls
programme that does not explicitly protect mobility will drift toward immobility, because that is
where the incentives point. *Mechanism, as a matter of how institutions behave.*

## After a fall

The post-fall review has three jobs and the first one is urgent. **Detect injury**, including the
two that hide: intracranial bleeding in someone taking an anticoagulant, which can present late
and with a benign initial examination; and hip fracture, where some people can still weight-bear.
Then **find the cause**, which means going back through the channel list rather than recording
"unwitnessed fall". Then **change the plan**, because a review that produces no change has only
documented the event. *Consensus; the observation schedules and the imaging thresholds are
country-dependent.*

## The human stakes, said plainly

For a frail older person a fall in hospital can be the event that ends independent living, and the
fracture is only part of it. The fear afterwards is itself disabling, and the activity restriction
it produces makes the next fall more likely — so the psychological consequence is also a
physiological one. People describe losing confidence in their own body, and that is not a soft
outcome.

And the second thing, which is said less often: the person who was never allowed out of the chair
also lost something, and nobody filled in a form about it. Both harms are real, they pull in
opposite directions, and the resolution is not to pick one. It is to attack the modifiable
channels so that the person can move with less risk, rather than to reduce the risk by reducing
the moving.

Finally: a staff member present when someone falls often carries it personally, and institutions
that respond to a fall by looking for the individual who was insufficiently vigilant produce
exactly the restrictive, defensive practice that harms the next patient.

Why a product of factors behaves differently from a sum, and what that predicts about

channel is the most modifiable and why it is the most often skipped. Where lying-and-standing

framework where the reader works says about restriction and restraint, and who decides. What the
post-fall review locally requires for someone taking an anticoagulant. And how the local programme
protects mobility rather than only counting falls.

## Scope and safety

This explains why a class of risk resists a class of intervention, for someone already training in
or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a decision aid, not a risk
assessment tool, and it names no score, no threshold, no observation interval and no imaging
criterion, deliberately. It is explicitly **not** guidance on restriction or restraint, which is a
legal and capacity matter governed by the law where the reader works. The falls policy and the
legal framework where someone works are the authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical
review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care,
including any decision about whether a particular person should walk. If someone is unwell right
now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The arithmetic and the physiology are stable: multiplicative channels, and deconditioning that
starts within days. What has moved, and is still moving, is the guidance layer — specifically the
retreat from numerical falls risk scoring in inpatient settings, the evidence on bed and chair
alarms, the vitamin D recommendations, and the legal framework governing restriction, which
changes with legislation and case law rather than with evidence. The local policy and the local
law are the authority for all of it, and the legal half is the part most likely to be out of date
first.
