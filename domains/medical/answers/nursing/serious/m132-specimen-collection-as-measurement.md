# The measurement finishes at the bedside. The laboratory only reads what the technique fixed.

A specimen is usually taught as the thing that gets a test done, which puts the measurement inside
the laboratory and the collection outside it. That is the wrong way round. **The analysis is the
most reliable and most reproducible step in the whole chain.** Nearly everything that can make a
result wrong has already happened by the time the container is sealed, and no instrument
downstream can undo any of it (**consensus**; the pre-analytic phase is the dominant source of
laboratory error in the published audit literature, which is observational rather than
mechanistic).

Markers used below: (**mechanism**), (**definitional**), (**consensus**),
(**country-dependent**). No volume, interval, temperature, transport time or diagnostic threshold
appears anywhere here. All of those are set by the receiving laboratory's own handbook, which is
the authority and differs between laboratories in the same city.

This is also the cleanest way to say what a point-of-care test is and is not. A specimen carries
the answer away from the patient; a point-of-care test carries the question to them and computes
the answer where they are. The pre-analytic steps that remain are fewer — no transport, no
additive ratio to get wrong, frequently no labelling step at all — and the ones that remain are
the same ones: identity, compartment, contamination and technique (**mechanism**). What changes
is not that collection stopped mattering but that fewer of its failures are detectable afterwards
by anybody, because there is no second laboratory in the chain to reject anything
(**consensus**).

Identity sits at the top for a reason that is mechanical rather than procedural. A sample labelled
with another person's details is internally consistent: every check downstream passes, the result
is plausible, and it is filed and acted on. This is why labelling at the bedside from the person
in front of you — rather than from a pre-printed sheet, a memory, or a container labelled in the
sluice afterwards — is the one step of the whole process that admits no shortcut
(**consensus**).

## Which compartment, which is the question people skip

A result is a statement about the population that was sampled, and the technique decides which
population that was. The clearest example is the wound swab: a swab run across the surface of a
chronic wound samples the organisms living on the surface of a chronic wound, which is a
population that exists in almost every chronic wound and is not, by itself, evidence of infection
(**mechanism**). Tissue from the wound bed, or fluid from the deeper compartment, samples
something closer to the question being asked. The same logic separates a specimen voided into a
pot from one taken from a sterile compartment, and sputum from saliva — and the laboratory's first
act on a respiratory sample is frequently to judge whether it is sputum at all.

## Yield is mostly a property of the collection, not of the laboratory

For culture, the probability of recovering an organism that is genuinely present is driven by
three things, all of them decided at the bedside: **how much was taken**, **when it was taken
relative to antimicrobial therapy**, and **how many independent samples were taken**
(**consensus**). Volume is the dominant term for blood cultures specifically, because the
organism may be present at very low density and what is sampled is a fraction of a circulating
volume. None of those is a judgement the laboratory can make on your behalf, and none is
recoverable by analysing harder.

Timing relative to treatment is the term most often lost in a hurry. Once antimicrobials have
started, the sample answers a different question, and the answer tends toward the uninformative.
m037 is the full argument about the bundle that puts those two acts in a deliberate order and
about what that ordering trades away — because waiting for a specimen is itself a harm when
treatment is time-critical, and the resolution of that tension is local protocol rather than
reasoning (**country-dependent**).

## Colonisation, contamination and infection are three claims about one growth

```
   claim            what it asserts                             who can make it
   ──────────────── ─────────────────────────────────────────── ──────────────────────────────
   contamination    the organism came from the technique or      partly the lab, from the
                    the environment, not the patient            organism's identity and the
                                                                 pattern across samples
   colonisation     the organism is present on or in the        nobody, from the specimen
                    patient and is not causing disease           alone
   infection        the organism is causing this illness        the clinician, from the
                                                                 specimen AND the person
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   One growth, three possible claims, and the specimen on its own cannot choose between the
   last two. This is a definitional point and it is the commonest misreading of a microbiology
   report in practice: the report says what grew, which is not the same statement as what is
   wrong with the patient.
```

Stating it as a definition (**definitional**): a positive culture is a statement about the
presence of an organism. Infection is a clinical diagnosis that uses that statement as one input.
A device swab, a chronic wound swab and a screening swab produce positives at rates that make this
distinction load-bearing rather than pedantic (**consensus**).

## The failure modes

**Labelling away from the bedside.** Already named, and it is first because it is the only one on
this list that can kill somebody who was never unwell.

**The specimen with no question.** A sample taken because it is routine, or because the line is
there, or because somebody might want it, produces a result that has to be acted on by whoever
sees it next, with no record of what it was for. A positive from a question nobody asked generates
treatment, and sometimes harm, with no counterfactual available.

**The sample taken after the decision.** Collected correctly, transported correctly, reported
after the course has started and the patient has gone home. The chain was intact and the result
changed nothing.

**The repeat that answers nothing.** Repeating a rejected sample is correct. Repeating a sample
because the result was not the expected one, with the same technique and the same compartment,
reproduces the measurement rather than improving it (**mechanism**).

**Transport treated as logistics.** Time and temperature in transit are part of the measurement,
not part of the portering. Cell counts, cell integrity, fastidious organisms and the overgrowth of
robust ones by delicate ones are all time-dependent, and the direction of each error is
predictable (**mechanism**; the limits are set by the receiving laboratory and are
(**country-dependent**)).

**Carryover and fill.** Where a container holds an additive, the ratio of sample to additive is
part of the method, and the order in which containers are filled matters because additive can
travel between them. The specific order, like everything else procedural here, belongs to the
laboratory's handbook.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Every specimen in this answer came out of somebody, and most of the ways they are obtained are
unpleasant. Venepuncture is the commonest invasive procedure in healthcare and it is not nothing
to the person having it: needle fear is prevalent, it is usually treated as a personality trait
rather than a clinical finding, and it is a documented reason people avoid care that would help
them. Children, people with learning disabilities, people with dementia and people who have had
many samples taken over years are the ones for whom a repeat is most costly, and they are
disproportionately the people a badly collected sample is repeated on. A sample taken twice
because the first was mislabelled is a real harm done to a real person by an avoidable system
failure, and it is usually recorded, if at all, as an administrative incident.

Consent for sampling, and what is done with a sample afterwards — whether it is retained, used for
research, or re-analysed later — sit inside a legal and ethical framework that differs profoundly
between countries and is not a bedside judgement. It is named here rather than explained here. The
reader's own legal framework, research governance and local policy are the only authority on it.

Two smaller things that belong to the person rather than to the process. A sample implies a
question, and people reasonably want to know what it is; "we're sending some bloods" answers
nothing and is the version most often given. And results arrive after people have gone, which
makes the follow-up of a pending result part of the duty of care rather than an administrative
tail — a specimen taken and never looked at is a harm with the paperwork of a kindness.

And the context: specimen quality tracks staffing, interruption and the time available to do a
procedure properly, in that order. An institution that answers a rise in rejected samples with a
reminder about technique, having changed none of those three, has identified the wrong variable.

## Scope and safety

This explains why a specimen's collection determines what a result can mean, for someone already
training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a specimen collection procedure, not a
laboratory handbook and not a guide to interpreting any particular report. It deliberately states
no volume, no fill level, no additive order, no transport time or temperature and no diagnostic
threshold, because every one of those belongs to the receiving laboratory and to local policy,
which are the authority. It is not a basis for deciding whether any person is infected, nor for
starting, changing or withholding any treatment. Nothing here has had clinical or laboratory
review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care. If
someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanisms do not date: a sample is a statement about a compartment at an instant, and no
analysis recovers what collection destroyed. The arithmetic of yield does not date either. What
moves is everything procedural — container types, additive chemistry, transport requirements and
rejection criteria are revised by laboratories continuously and without announcement, which is
why this answer names none of them. What is moving faster is the technology layer: rapid molecular
and point-of-care testing changes the trade-off between taking a specimen and waiting for it,
detects organisms that are present without being the cause, and shifts the colonisation-versus-
infection problem from a culture plate to a sequence. Expect the interpretive difficulty to grow
rather than shrink as sensitivity improves. The receiving laboratory's handbook and the reader's
local policy are the authority throughout.
