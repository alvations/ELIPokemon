# Fatigue has no readout, and the rota sets its value before anyone arrives.

The claim that matters is not that tired people make more mistakes. It is that **fatigue is
produced by the shift pattern rather than by the person working it**, that its effects are
systematic rather than random, and that the one capacity it degrades earliest is the capacity to
notice it is degraded. Those three facts together are why it is a design problem. *This framing is
mechanism; the individual effects are consensus from the sleep and human-factors literature.*

Markers used below: *mechanism*, *definitional*, *consensus*, *country-dependent*.

Three largely independent inputs, and the night shift loads all three at once:

   PRESSURE                 since waking, discharged only by     waking, not from clocking on.
                            sleep                                A night shift often begins
                                                                 after a full day awake

   TIME ON TASK             decrement accumulating within the    the last hours of a long shift
                            shift itself, independent of the     coincide with the circadian
                            other two                            trough
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   They are ADDITIVE, not alternatives. A person at the circadian trough, twenty hours awake,
   eleven hours into a shift is carrying three loads at once, and the chart records none of
   them. Daytime sleep after a night shift is both shorter and more fragmented, because it is
   taken at the wrong circadian phase — so consecutive nights accumulate a debt rather than

*The three-input account is consensus, and the shorter, more fragmented daytime sleep is one of
the most robust findings in the area. No number of hours, no number of consecutive nights and no
shift length is given here: those are regulated or negotiated and differ by country.*

## What degrades, in what order

Not uniformly, and the order is what makes it dangerous:

```
   degrades EARLY                               holds up LONGER
   ──────────────────────────────────────────── ───────────────────────────────────────────
   sustained attention and vigilance            over-practised motor routines
   detection of an infrequent, unexpected       well-rehearsed procedures done often
     signal — exactly the deterioration case
   working memory, so multi-step tasks lose     brief, interrupted tasks that fit in one
     their middle                                 held thought
   error monitoring: the mistake is made AND    speed, which may even be maintained — at
     not caught                                    the cost of accuracy
   risk appraisal, which shifts toward the
     easier option
   SELF-ASSESSMENT of impairment
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   The last line is the one that makes exhortation useless. The faculty you would need to
   decide "I am too tired for this" is one of the faculties that has gone. Someone who feels
   fine at 04:00 has lost a measurement, not a symptom.
```

*The dissociation between measured performance and self-rated alertness is consensus; it is the
central reason fatigue is managed structurally rather than by asking people to self-report.*

## Why the handover at the end of a night shift is the worst one

m002 covers what handover is doing and why escalation criteria are written as numbers; m070 covers
what is lost at a boundary. This is a narrower point about **where in the day the boundary sits**:

```
   the handover at the end of a night shift is simultaneously:
     the LARGEST information transfer    everything that happened overnight, for every
                                         patient, much of it unwitnessed by the day team
     at the OUTGOING party's worst       end of shift, past the circadian trough, longest
       moment                            time on task, and about to drive home
     at the INCOMING party's worst       least context, no continuity with the night's
       moment for context                events, and often numerically fewer people present
                                         overnight to have witnessed them
     under TIME PRESSURE in both         one party wants to leave; the other has a day's
       directions                        work starting
     with the fewest cross-checks        overnight staffing is typically lower, so the
                                         number of people who could correct an error in the
                                         account is at its minimum
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Nothing about the structure of handover changes at 07:30. Every variable feeding into
   it is at its least favourable value at once, which is what "worst hour" means.
```

And a second, less obvious failure: the outgoing clinician is motivated toward a **tidy** account.
A night that resolves into a clean summary is easier to give and easier to receive, and the thing
most likely to be smoothed out of it is the unresolved worry — the observation that did not fit,
the family member who was concerned, the patient who was "a bit off" without crossing a threshold.
That is precisely the content with the highest value to the incoming team. *Consensus from the
handover literature; mechanism for why it happens.*

## What is actually done about it

Not a list of recommendations — a list of the levels at which the problem exists, because the
level determines who can fix it:

**Rota design.** Direction of rotation, number of consecutive nights, shift length, rest between
shifts, and how the first and last shifts of a block are placed. This is the level with the
largest effect and it is not available to the individual. *Country-dependent: much of it is
regulated by working-time law or collective agreement, which is the authority.*

**Breaks, and whether they can actually be taken.** A break policy that cannot be delivered at the
observed staffing level is not a control. *Mechanism.*

**Napping.** Short naps during night shifts have supportive evidence in several settings, and
institutional policies on them range from provision to prohibition. *Consensus that there is
evidence; entirely country- and institution-dependent whether it is permitted, and local policy
governs.* Sleep inertia on waking is a real and well-described cost, which is part of why nap
duration and timing are specified where napping is sanctioned.

**Task scheduling.** Where there is any discretion, moving the tasks that depend most on sustained
attention away from the trough, and adding an independent check where it cannot be moved. m003 is
the argument for why an independent check is the control that survives tiredness.

**The commute.** Driving after a night shift is one of the best-characterised risks in the whole
area, and it is a risk to the staff member rather than the patient, which is exactly why it gets
least attention. *Consensus.*

**Not telling people to be more careful.** Fatigue is not responsive to effort, and the faculty
that would allow someone to compensate is among the faculties impaired. An institution that
answers a night-shift error with a reminder about vigilance has chosen the one intervention the
mechanism rules out. *Mechanism.*

## The human stakes, said plainly

This one is unusual in the set because the person harmed is often the staff member. Long-term
shift work is associated with real health effects and with effects on family life that do not
appear in any incident report, and the person who drives home after twelve hours and a night of no
breaks is taking a risk nobody has assessed.

It also lands on patients, and it lands unevenly. The patient who deteriorates at 04:00 is being
looked after by the fewest, most tired people of the day, and their deterioration has to be
noticed by exactly the faculty that is most impaired. That is not a reason for pessimism; it is
the argument for why the thresholds, the escalation criteria and the checklists exist. They are
the parts of the system that do not get tired.

And it has to be said that someone who makes a mistake at the end of a long night and is treated
as the cause of it will not report the next one. The reporting that makes the pattern visible
depends on the error being treated as a property of the shift. An institution that cannot do that
loses the data it needs to fix the rota.

## What an examiner digs into next

The three inputs, and why they are additive rather than alternative. Why hours awake are counted
from waking and not from the start of the shift. Why daytime sleep does not reset the debt. Which
capacities fail first, and why the failure of self-assessment is the one that matters. Why every
variable in a handover is at its worst value simultaneously at the end of a night shift. What gets
smoothed out of a tidy summary. Which of the controls above are available to an individual and
which are not. And what the local rules are on shift length, rest periods and napping, because
those are the authority and they differ.

## Scope and safety

This explains why fatigue is managed as a system property, for someone already training in or
qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a fatigue risk assessment and not
advice about anyone's own working pattern, sleep or health, and it deliberately states no shift
length, no number of consecutive nights, no rest interval and no nap duration — those are set by
law, negotiated agreement and local policy, which are the authority. It is not occupational health
advice and has had no clinical or legal review. Anyone whose own sleep, health or safety at work
is affected needs their occupational health service and their own clinician, not a revision note.
Nothing here is for use in an emergency; if someone is unwell right now, the local emergency
number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physiology is stable and old: the three inputs and the order in which capacities fail are not
in dispute at the level stated here. What moves is everything around it. Working-time law, rest
requirements and the regulatory treatment of fatigue as an employer duty differ sharply by country
and change with legislation; institutional napping policy is changing in several health systems
and in opposite directions; and the long-term health effects of shift work are an active and
genuinely contested observational literature where the direction is agreed and the magnitude is
not. The reader's own regulations and local policy are the authority for all of it.
