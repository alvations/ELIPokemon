# Lying still is not the absence of a treatment. It is a treatment, and its dose is time.

Bed rest has real indications, and nothing below argues otherwise. The argument is narrower and
harder to dismiss: **bed rest acts on every major system, the effects are dose-dependent in hours
and days, and none of them is written on the order.** A prescription for a drug carries its
adverse effects with it. A decision that someone stays in bed carries the same class of
information and carries it nowhere. *This framing is mechanism rather than guideline; the
individual effects below are consensus.*

## The asymmetry, which is the practically important half

The loss is fast and the recovery is slow, and the two are not symmetrical in either mechanism or
timescale.

*That recovery is slower than loss is consensus, and is one of the clearest findings in the
literature on older inpatients. Any specific ratio is not stated here because the studies do not
agree on one.* The effect is larger in older people and in anyone with low reserve before
admission, which is why the same number of days in bed is a different intervention for two
different people.

## Why it accumulates without being noticed

Four structural reasons, and none of them is anybody's carelessness:

**Nothing records the negative.** A fall generates an incident form, a drug error generates a
report, and a day spent sitting generates nothing. The harm with no document is the harm that does
not get counted. *Mechanism, and the same argument as m073 makes about pertinent negatives.*

**Every local pressure points one way.** Getting someone up takes two people, time, equipment and
a tolerance of risk; leaving them takes none of those. Where staffing is short, the default wins
without any decision being made. *Consensus from the implementation literature; the staffing
context is country-dependent.*

**The order outlives the reason.** "Bed rest" written for a specific indication on day one is
rarely rescinded when the indication resolves, because rescinding requires somebody to notice an
instruction that is no longer doing anything. *Mechanism; the same structure as m038's device
review.*

**Risk aversion reads in one direction only.** A person who falls while walking produces a visible
event with a name attached. A person who never walked and cannot now stand produces a slow decline
with nobody's name on it. *Consensus; m040 makes the same argument from the falls side, and the
resolution there is the resolution here.*

## What early mobilisation is, and what the evidence actually supports

Early mobilisation means progressive activity begun as soon as it is safe, graded against the
person's condition, rather than deferred until they are better. It is not a single intervention:
it ranges from sitting out of bed to active cycling in critical care, and the evidence is not
uniform across that range. *Consensus that prolonged immobility is harmful; the evidence for
specific early mobilisation protocols differs by setting, and in some settings — notably very
early mobilisation after acute stroke — trial evidence has argued against the most aggressive
versions.* That last point matters: "earlier is always better" is not what the literature says,
and the honest position is that the default should be movement, titrated, with named exceptions
rather than a blanket.

What that looks like operationally is boring and effective: a documented mobility status, a
documented plan with who does it and how often, the equipment at the bedside rather than down the
corridor, and the question "why is this person in bed?" asked on every round. *The specifics are
set by local policy and by the physiotherapy and occupational therapy assessment, which are the
authority on what any individual should be doing.*

## The human stakes, said plainly

The person most harmed by this is the one least able to object: an older person admitted for
something else, who walked into hospital and leaves unable to manage their own stairs. The cause
is not the illness. It is a fortnight in which nobody's job was to get them up, and the
consequence can be the loss of their home.

It is worth being equally plain about the other side. Getting someone up carries real risk, takes
real staffing, and sometimes is not what the person wants that morning — and someone who declines
is exercising a choice, not committing an error. The resolution is neither to force nor to leave,
but to treat immobility as something with a cost that has to be weighed out loud, in the same
sentence as the risk of moving.

And this is a staffing question as much as a clinical one. Telling individual staff to mobilise
more, on a ward where it cannot be done safely with the people present, moves the blame without
moving the patient.

## What an examiner digs into next

Which systems immobility affects and by what mechanism in each. Why strength falls faster than
muscle bulk. Why orthostatic intolerance appears so early. Why the two slopes in the diagram have
different units, and what follows for discharge planning. Why nothing on the chart records the day
nobody walked. Which professional sets the mobility plan locally, and what the escalation route is
when it is not being delivered. And where the evidence for very early mobilisation is contested
rather than settled.

## Scope and safety

This explains why immobility is an active harm and why its reversal is slower than its onset, for
someone already training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a
decision aid and not a mobility assessment, and it states no rate, no interval and no
thromboprophylaxis threshold, deliberately. Whether a particular person should be mobilised, how,
and with how many people is set by the local policy and by the therapy assessment for that person;
those are the authority and this is not, and none of it has had clinical review. Nothing here is
for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care, including a decision about
whether someone should get out of bed. If someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number
is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physiology is stable and has been for decades: the directions of effect in the table are not
in dispute, and neither is the asymmetry between losing capacity and regaining it. What is
genuinely live is the dose-response question — how early, how much, for whom — and it is live in
opposite directions in different settings, with the stroke literature the standing reminder that
"as early as possible" is a hypothesis and not a principle. The procedural layer dates fastest:
mobility documentation, therapy staffing models, moving-and-handling policy and thromboprophylaxis
guidance all differ by country and are revised on a cycle. The local policy is the authority for
all of it.
