# A dressing does not heal anything. It holds a condition in which healing can happen.

This is the single most useful sentence in wound care, and it reframes every product question that
follows from it. Repair is done by the wound: by migrating keratinocytes, by fibroblasts laying
down matrix, by angiogenesis, by the inflammatory cells that clear debris first. A dressing
contributes none of that. **It maintains an environment**, and the environment it maintains is the
thing being chosen when a dressing is chosen. *This is mechanism rather than guideline, and it is
consensus across all major wound-care guidance.*

What the environment consists of:

```
   what a dressing does                     why it matters to the wound itself
   ───────────────────────────────────────  ─────────────────────────────────────────────
   holds moisture in the useful range       cells migrate across a moist surface; they
                                            cannot migrate across a dry crust
   manages exudate                          too much floods the peri-wound skin; too
                                            little dries the bed
   keeps temperature stable                 cellular activity falls when the wound is
                                            cooled, and every change cools it
   protects from mechanical trauma           new epithelium is one cell layer thick and
                                            is removed by a wipe
   acts as a bacterial barrier               not sterility — a barrier
   carries information                       wear time, strike-through, what the removed
                                            dressing shows
   does no harm coming off                   an adherent dressing removes what grew
                                            under it; this is a real cost of a change
```

*Which product does which of these is local.* Formularies differ by country and by institution,
and product selection is a decision for the local formulary and the assessing clinician — the
class-level reasoning below is the part that generalises.

## Moisture balance, and why the target is the middle

The historical shift in wound care was from drying wounds out to keeping them moist, and the
mechanism is the whole argument: epithelial cells migrate across a moist surface and have to
tunnel beneath a dry scab. But the opposite extreme is also damage — excess exudate macerates the
peri-wound skin, the barrier fails, and the wound gets *bigger*. So the target is not "wet" and it
is not "dry":

```
   TOO DRY                            right                             TOO WET
   ────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────┬────────────────────────────
   crust forms             │  surface moist, peri-wound   │  peri-wound skin white,
   epithelium has to       │  skin intact, exudate        │  soggy, breaking down
   burrow                  │  contained                   │  wound margin extending
   healing slows           │                              │  dressing leaking
   pain on movement        │                              │  odour, change needed often
   ────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────
   the dressing's job:     DONATE moisture  ◄────────────►  ABSORB moisture

   which is why the classes exist at all, and why they are not interchangeable:
     gels and hydrogels   donate to a dry bed
     foams and alginates  absorb from a wet one
     films                retain, and add nothing
     hydrocolloids        do some of both, within limits
```

The teaching framework for the assessment that precedes the choice is usually some version of
TIME — tissue, infection or inflammation, moisture, and the wound edge. *That framework is widely
taught internationally; it is a teaching structure rather than a regulation.* Its value is the
same as any structured assessment: it stops the moisture question being answered before the tissue
question has been asked.

## Pressure damage is load multiplied by time

Pressure damage is an **ischaemic injury**. Sustained mechanical load deforms soft tissue and
occludes capillary perfusion and lymphatic drainage; oxygen delivery falls, metabolic waste
accumulates, cells are damaged both by the ischaemia and by the deformation itself, and
reperfusion when the load comes off causes further injury. *That mechanism is consensus across
major guidance.*

The relationship that gives the whole topic its shape:

```
   pressure
     ^
   high │ ■
        │   ■
        │      ■
        │          ■ ■
        │                 ■  ■  ■                  everything on or above the
    low │                          ■  ■  ■  ■  ■   curve causes damage
        └──────────────────────────────────────────────────►  time
          minutes                                    hours

   HIGH pressure for a SHORT time, or LOW pressure for a LONG time, do the same thing.
   This is why "he was only in the chair for the afternoon" is not reassurance, and why
   a hard edge for twenty minutes is not either.

   Three things move the curve DOWN — i.e. make damage happen sooner:
     SHEAR      tissue layers slide over each other; vessels are stretched and kinked
                as well as compressed. Sliding down the bed is the classic source
     FRICTION   surface damage from rubbing, which also removes the barrier
     MICROCLIMATE  moist, warm skin at the interface tolerates less load than dry,
                cool skin. This is where hygiene genuinely does matter
```

Two consequences are worth stating explicitly.

**The damage often starts deep.** Load concentrates at the interface between soft tissue and bone,
so the deepest tissue can be injured while the skin surface still looks intact. This is why an
area of intact, discoloured skin — deep purple or maroon — is treated as a serious finding rather
than a bruise, and why such an area can deteriorate quickly once it becomes visible. *The staging
system in use locally will name this category; the categories and their numbering are set by that
system, so the reader's own classification document is the authority for the words.*

**Anatomy predicts the sites.** Where there is little soft tissue between bone and surface, and
where perfusion is poorest, damage comes first — the heels and the sacrum in bed, the ischial
tuberosities in a chair, and the many sites under devices: oxygen tubing behind the ears, mask
edges, casts, splints, tube fixings, compression. *Device-related pressure damage is consensus as
a category and is the one most often missed, because the device is there for a reason and so
nobody looks under it.*

## Why cleanliness is the wrong frame

Because the injury is ischaemic. A scrupulously clean, immobile patient develops pressure damage.
A repositioned one largely does not. Hygiene matters to the microclimate and to infection risk
once skin is broken, and both are real — but they are secondary to load and duration.

There is also a genuine diagnostic distinction here, *and it is consensus*: moisture-associated
skin damage, from incontinence or sweat or exudate, is a different entity from pressure damage,
with a different distribution, different appearance and different management. Confusing the two is
common, and it leads to the wrong intervention in both directions.

Prevention is therefore about load: structured risk assessment with a scored tool *and* clinical
judgement, skin inspection, repositioning, support surfaces, management of moisture, attention to
nutrition and hydration because they alter tissue tolerance and repair, and looking under every
device. *How often to reposition is deliberately not stated here: it is individualised, and the
evidence for any particular interval is genuinely contested in the literature rather than
settled.* The honest answer to "how often" is that it depends on the person, the surface and the
skin response, and that the local policy sets the starting point.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Pressure damage hurts. It is slow, it can be deep, it can take months, it can become the reason
someone cannot go home, and a large share of it is preventable. It is also one of the few harms
that is mostly a property of the care system rather than of the illness — which is why it is
measured, reported and audited the way it is.

That cuts both ways and the second half matters too. Some pressure damage occurs despite
appropriate care, in people whose perfusion or tissue tolerance cannot be fixed, and treating
every instance as negligence is both untrue and corrosive to the reporting that makes prevention
possible.

## What an examiner digs into next

What a particular dressing class is doing to moisture, and what the signs are that the choice was
wrong in each direction. Why the frequency of dressing change is a trade-off rather than a virtue.
The difference between pressure damage and moisture-associated skin damage. Why a discoloured but
intact area over a bony prominence is urgent. Which devices on a given patient need skin inspected
under them. And what the local risk-assessment tool scores, and what it does not.

## Scope and safety

This explains two mechanisms, for someone already training in or qualified for clinical practice.
It is not a protocol, not a decision aid, and it names no product, no dressing brand and no
repositioning interval, deliberately. Dressing selection, risk assessment, staging language and
repositioning frequency are all set by local policy and the local formulary, which are the
authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency
or for a decision about any person's care, including a decision about anyone's own wound. If
someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanisms are stable: moist wound healing has been the foundation of wound care for decades,
and pressure damage has been understood as load multiplied by time for longer than that. What
dates is everything procedural — staging terminology and its numbering, risk-assessment tools,
support surface classifications, repositioning recommendations, and above all the formulary, which
changes whenever procurement does. The reader's own policy and formulary are the authority for all
of it.
