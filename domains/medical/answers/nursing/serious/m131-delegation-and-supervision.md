# Delegation moves the task and the authority. It does not move the accountability for deciding.

Almost every argument about delegation is really an argument about which of three things has been
handed over, because they travel separately and people assume they travel together. The task is
the act — the observation, the wash, the dressing, the feed. The authority is permission to
perform it in this setting on this person. The accountability is answerability for the decision
that it was safe to hand over, and for the supervision that followed. **The first two transfer.
The third does not** (**consensus**; the precise legal and regulatory construction is
(**country-dependent**)).

Markers used below: (**mechanism**), (**definitional**), (**consensus**),
(**country-dependent**). No interval, ratio, staffing level or competency threshold appears
anywhere here, because every one of those is set locally.

Get that separation right and the hard cases answer themselves. Get it wrong and you produce the
two commonest failures in the field at once: a registrant who believes they have discharged
responsibility by saying the words, and a support worker who believes they have acquired
protection by being told to do it.

The delegate's accountability for *declining* is the part most often left out of teaching, and
it is the part that protects patients. A delegate who cannot refuse is not a delegate; they are a
pair of hands, and the system that produced them has removed its last check (**mechanism**).

## What makes a task delegable, and why it is never simply a list

Institutions publish lists, and they have to, because a list is auditable. But the list is
downstream of five conditions, and a task on the list fails if any of them fails
(**consensus**):

* **It is within the delegate's assessed competence.** Assessed, with a record, rather than
  inferred from length of service or from having been seen to do it. Competence is
  task-specific and does not generalise: someone assessed in one procedure has no demonstrated
  competence in an adjacent one (**mechanism**).
* **Its outcome is predictable enough to be recognised by the person doing it.** This is the
  condition that does the real work. A task is delegable when the thing that would go wrong is
  something the delegate has been taught to recognise and report.
* **The person's condition is stable enough for that predictability to hold.** The same task is
  delegable in one patient and not in another on the same day, which is why a task list cannot
  be the whole answer (**mechanism**).
* **The delegate has accepted it,** having understood what is being asked and what to report.
* **Supervision is available at a proximity matched to the task** — and "available" means
  reachable and answering, not rostered.

What never delegates is the thing that is not a task at all: **assessment, the interpretation of
findings, the formulation of a plan, and the decision to escalate.** Those are acts of
professional judgement, and the distinction between them and the tasks that look like them is the
single most useful thing to be able to state (**consensus**).

```
   the task that may be delegated          the judgement that may not
   ─────────────────────────────────────── ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   record this set of observations          interpret them, and decide what the trend means
   report if the wound looks different      assess the wound and decide whether the plan
                                            changes
   assist with this meal                    assess swallowing. m039 is the full argument for
                                            why this one is not a judgement call at the bedside
   reposition on the agreed schedule        decide the schedule, and decide whether the skin
                                            response means it should change — m133
   tell me if he seems not himself          decide whether this is new confusion, and what to
                                            do about it
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Notice that the left column and the right column are frequently described by the same
   verb in conversation. "Can you check his obs" and "can you check on him" are a task and
   an assessment, and the second is not delegable however casually it is asked.
```

## Supervision is a dial, and it is checked at the wrong moment

Supervision is conventionally graded by proximity — direct, where the supervisor is present;
indirect, where they are in the area and immediately available; and remote, where they are
contactable (**definitional**; the terminology varies by country and profession). The grade
required is a function of the task's risk and of how new the delegate is to it, not of how busy
the shift is.

The structural weakness is that delegation is almost always checked **at the point of
initiation** and almost never afterwards. A judgement is made once, at handover, about a
patient's condition at that moment; the task then recurs for the rest of the shift, or the week,
against a condition that has moved. Nothing in the routine prompts a re-decision
(**mechanism**).

Two consequences follow, and both are observable on any ward. The first is **drift**: a task
delegated as "record these" becomes, by repetition and good intentions, "deal with these". The
second is **standing delegation by precedent**: nobody delegated the task today, and nobody
will, because it was delegated three weeks ago and has been done ever since.

## The failure modes, which are mostly structural

**Delegation by absence.** The commonest failure is not a bad delegation but a missing one: the
task is left undone or picked up by whoever notices, with no decision, no competence check and no
report-back. An unsafe delegation is at least visible. This one is not (**mechanism**).

**The delegate who cannot decline.** Bank and agency staff, students, newly qualified staff and
anyone whose next shift depends on being easy to work with are structurally less able to refuse.
Where the ability to decline is the safety mechanism, it fails first in exactly the people most
likely to be asked (**mechanism**).

**The report-back that has nowhere to go.** Delegation has two halves and the second is routinely
unresourced. If the person who was told to report cannot find the person to report to, the
information stops, and the record then shows a task performed with no finding attached — which
reads as a normal finding. m073 is the full argument about why that blank is not neutral.

**The record that holds the act and not the ask.** Two accountabilities held concurrently are
separable afterwards only if the record holds both halves: what was asked, by whom, of whom, and
with what to report back, alongside what was done and what was found. Most clinical records have
one box, and what goes in it is the act (**mechanism**).

**Delegating across a professional boundary without a shared vocabulary.** "Keep an eye on him"
means different things to different disciplines, and the instruction that feels most collegial is
the least specific.

**Task-shifting dressed as delegation.** Where a role has absorbed work permanently because of
establishment gaps, each individual handover may be defensible while the aggregate is a change in
who does the work, made without training, assessment or agreement. That is a workforce decision
being taken one shift at a time, and calling it delegation hides it (**mechanism**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

Two groups of people are affected by this, and only one of them is usually discussed.

The patient is on the receiving end of a decision they cannot see. Most direct care in most
systems is given by people who are not registrants, and that is not a deficiency — it is often
the most skilled, most attentive and most continuous care a person receives, and support workers
frequently know a patient better than anyone with a clinical qualification. What a patient cannot
know is whether the person at the bedside was given the task with a decision behind it or because
nobody else was available. The honest version of that is also worth saying: a system that
delegates well is not a system that delegates less.

The other group is the person delegated to. Being asked to do something beyond your competence is
frightening, and refusing it costs something real — the shift goes badly, the relationship with
the team is damaged, and the person asking is often senior to you. Teaching that treats "you can
always decline" as a complete answer puts the weight of a systems failure on the least protected
person present. What makes declining actually possible is the surrounding structure: a named route
to raise it, a culture where it is heard without cost, and seniors who respond to a refusal by
fixing the gap rather than finding someone more compliant. Where those are absent, telling someone
they are accountable for declining is close to a trap.

And the context, because every one of the failure modes above gets worse along the same axis.
Delegation decisions are made under staffing constraints that the person making them did not set
and cannot change on the shift. An institution that responds to a delegation incident by
retraining individuals, without examining the establishment that made the delegation necessary,
has identified the wrong variable.

## What an examiner digs into next

The separation of task, authority and accountability, and which of the three transfers. The three
concurrent accountabilities, stated as three objects rather than as a hierarchy. The five
conditions, and specifically why the predictability of the outcome is the one that does the work.
Why assessment, interpretation, planning and escalation do not delegate, with a worked pair of
examples that share a verb. The grades of supervision, and why the grade is set by the task rather
than by the staffing. Why drift and standing delegation are the structural failures rather than
one-off bad decisions. What the delegate's accountability to decline requires in order to be real.
And what the reader's own regulator and employer say, since that is the document that binds them
personally and it differs by country and by profession.

## Scope and safety

This explains what delegation transfers and what it does not, for someone already training in or
qualified for clinical practice. It is not a delegation policy, not a scope-of-practice
determination and not legal advice, and it deliberately names no task as delegable or
non-delegable in the reader's setting — that list is set by the reader's regulator and employer,
which are the authority and which differ by country and by role. It states no staffing level,
ratio or supervision interval for the same reason. Nothing here has had clinical or legal review,
and nothing here should be relied on in a complaint, an investigation or any disciplinary or legal
process. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care. If
someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The separation of task, authority and accountability is structural and does not date, and the
five conditions have been stable in professional guidance for a long time. What is moving, and
moving fast, is the regulatory status of the roles being delegated to: several countries are
introducing or extending registration, protected titles and national competence frameworks for
support and assistant roles, and each change alters what may be delegated and who answers for it.
The related debate about skill mix and establishment is active and politically contested, and the
evidence in it is observational. Everything procedural and legal here — the local task list, the
competence records, the supervision grades and the liability position — is set by the reader's
regulator, employer and jurisdiction, which are the authority throughout and which should be read
rather than inferred from this answer.
