# Continence is a volume held at low pressure and released on command. Every word there can fail.

Continence is taught as a care task and it is a control problem. Something has to hold a rising
volume without rising pressure, something has to stay shut while it does, something has to report
how full it is, something has to permit or defer the release, and the release itself has to be two
coordinated movements in opposite directions at the same instant. Then, entirely outside the body,
there has to be somewhere to go, a way of getting there, hands that work and enough time.

Six or seven independent requirements, one observable outcome, and the outcome is binary. That is
why a continence assessment is an assessment and not a question, and it is why "incontinent" is a
useless entry in a record: it names the output and not one of the inputs.

Markers used below. (**mechanism**) means it follows from physiology and is checkable by
reasoning; (**definitional**) means it is what the word means; (**consensus**) means mainstream
agreement across major guidance; (**country-dependent**) means the reader's own guidance decides.

## Storage and voiding are two states of one system, and they are not symmetrical

```
   STORAGE — the long state, hours at a time        VOIDING — the short state, seconds
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────  ──────────────────────────────────────────
   detrusor RELAXED, and staying relaxed as the     detrusor CONTRACTS
   volume rises — this is compliance, and it is
   why pressure does not track volume
   outlet CLOSED — urethral sphincter mechanism     outlet OPENS
   and pelvic floor
   sensation REPORTS filling, gradually             the two movements above happen TOGETHER
   cortex INHIBITS the reflex, i.e. defers          cortex PERMITS
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────  ──────────────────────────────────────────
   Storage is a state actively held. Voiding is a coordinated event. The system is not a tap
   that is open or shut: it has to switch cleanly between two opposite configurations, and the
   switching is a separate function from either of the states it switches between.
```

Storage is dominated by sympathetic and somatic activity, voiding by sacral parasympathetic
activity, with cortical control sitting above both as a permission rather than as a drive.
(**mechanism**) The practical consequence is that a failure of the *switch* — the detrusor
contracting while the sphincter is also contracting — is a distinct problem from a failure of
either component, it is typically neurological, and it produces high pressures and incomplete
emptying rather than simple leaking. (**mechanism**, **consensus**)

## The patterns, and the one that is the opposite of what it looks like

The conventional patterns are a classification of *mechanism*, which is the only reason they are
worth memorising: each one implies a different first intervention, and two of them implicate
treatments that make the others worse. (**definitional** for the categories, (**consensus**) for
the management logic.)

| Pattern | When it leaks | The mechanism | What it needs first | What the wrong treatment does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Stress** | On effort — cough, laugh, lift, stand | Raised abdominal pressure exceeds the closing pressure of the outlet. No detrusor contraction is involved at all | Supervised pelvic floor muscle training, as the first-line intervention in most national guidance | A bladder-relaxing drug has no mechanism to act on here |
| **Urgency** (overactive bladder) | With a sudden compelling need, sometimes on the way | Involuntary detrusor contraction *during* storage. The warning interval between the signal and the event collapses | Behavioural work — bladder retraining, fluid and caffeine review, timed voiding — before anything else | Nothing, immediately; but the drug classes used here carry a side-effect burden that matters |
| **Mixed** | Both patterns, in one person | Both mechanisms, in proportions that have to be established | Treating the more troublesome component first, which is the person's judgement and not the clinician's | Assuming one pattern and treating only that |
| **Overflow** — chronic retention with overflow | Continuously or unpredictably, often small volumes, often at night | The bladder **does not empty**. Leaking is a consequence of failure to empty, not of failure to hold | Establishing the residual volume and relieving the retention | Treating it as urgency with a bladder-relaxing drug worsens the retention. This is the error that matters |
| **Functional** | When the route fails, not when the bladder does | Store, outlet, sensation and coordination are all intact. Mobility, dexterity, cognition, clothing, call-bell response or distance to the toilet is what failed | Changing the environment, not the person | Investigating the bladder of someone whose problem is a walking frame on the wrong side of the bed |

**Read the overflow row twice.** It is the single most consequential distinction in the subject,
because the symptom is the opposite sign of the cause. A person who is wet because they are not
emptying looks, at the bedside and in a free-text entry, exactly like a person who is wet because
they are contracting too soon — and the treatment for the second makes the first worse.
(**consensus**) The same inversion exists in the other tract: faecal loading with overflow
presents as liquid stool, and treating that as diarrhoea with a constipating agent is the same
error with the same shape. (**consensus**)

## What has to be excluded before any of this is a continence problem

New-onset incontinence is a symptom with a differential, and some of that differential is not
about the bladder. (**consensus**)

* **Retention**, because of the row above. A bladder scan measuring post-void residual is the
  usual instrument; the threshold that counts as significant is (**country-dependent**) and local.
* **A reversible precipitant.** Constipation, a new infection with new urinary symptoms, delirium,
  reduced mobility after an admission, and medicines — diuretics changing the volume and timing,
  sedatives and opioids changing the awareness and the mobility, and agents affecting outlet tone.
  The medicines review is part of the assessment, not an afterthought. (**consensus**)
* **Features that are not a continence problem at all.** Visible haematuria, pain, a palpable
  bladder, and above all new neurological signs — saddle sensory change, lower limb weakness, new
  bowel involvement — which belong to a different and urgent pathway. The specific escalation
  route is (**country-dependent**) and is the reader's local one.

## Why the assessment is of a chain, and why the chain is mostly not the bladder

The mechanism list at the top ends outside the body, and for inpatients that is usually where the
problem is. Someone continent at home becomes incontinent in hospital because the distance to the
toilet changed, the bed rails are up, the call bell was answered late, the clothing has a gown's
fastenings, a drip stand has to come too, and an intravenous infusion is running. None of that is
a change in the bladder. (**mechanism**)

That makes continence one of the clearest cases of a problem whose causal channels are
individually small and jointly decisive, which is the argument m040 makes about falls — and the
two intersect directly, because the commonest reason a frail person gets up at night in an
unfamiliar room is to pass urine. A toileting plan is a falls intervention. (**consensus**)

Two consequences from elsewhere in this specialty follow and are worth naming rather than
re-deriving. m038 is explicit that catheterisation is **not** an indicated management for
incontinence: it converts a continence problem into a device problem with a per-day infection
hazard, and it stops the continence assessment happening at all, because nobody can assess
continence in a catheterised person. (**consensus**) And m004 separates moisture-associated skin
damage from pressure damage: they are different entities with different causes, they are commonly
conflated, and continence is the usual source of the first. (**consensus**)

## The measurement problem, which is where the dignity and the mechanism meet

Continence data is self-reported, and the reporting is systematically suppressed. People do not
raise it, often wait years, and frequently do not raise it even when asked in a way that makes
raising it awkward. (**consensus**) So any prevalence figure in any record is a floor and not an
estimate, and a service's own numbers under-describe its own population.

```

   ─────────────────────────────────────── ───────────────────────── ────────────────────────────
   asked, by someone, and the answer was   "continent"               a finding. Usable.
   no

   asked badly, or asked within earshot     nothing                   nothing
   of four other people, and denied

   The last two produce the same blank, and the fourth is produced BY the circumstances of the
   asking. This is m073's pertinent-negative collapse with an extra term: the instrument that
   records the finding is also one of the things that suppresses it.
```

And containment has the same property. A pad is sometimes exactly the right answer and is the
person's own preference more often than services assume. But once it is in place the episodes stop
being counted, because there is no longer an event to record — so the decision to contain is also
a decision to stop measuring, and it needs a review point for that reason and not only for a skin
one. (**mechanism**)

## The human stakes, said plainly

This is a subject people do not raise, and the reasons are not mysterious. Continence is among the
first things a child is taught, it is tied to adult status in almost every culture, and losing it
is experienced by many people as a loss of standing rather than as a symptom. People manage it
alone for years. They restrict what they drink, which causes its own harm. They stop going to
places where they do not know where the toilet is, and the resulting isolation is often more
disabling than the symptom. Some stop leaving the house.

In hospital the stakes are immediate and concrete. Being wet and waiting is one of the most
distressing experiences people report of inpatient care, and it is distressing out of all
proportion to its clinical weight. Being changed by a stranger, being changed in a bay with a
curtain that does not reach, being told someone will come back — these are the parts people
remember years later, and they are also the parts least likely to appear in any record of the
admission.

Three things follow for practice and none of them are soft. First, the question has to be asked,
and asked in a way and a place that makes an honest answer possible, which means privately and in
plain words. Second, the language matters: "incontinent" used as a description of a person rather
than of an event travels with them through every subsequent encounter and changes how they are
treated. Third, and most often got wrong: a plan is only a plan if someone can actually deliver
it. A two-hourly toileting plan written on a ward that cannot staff it is not a plan — it is a
record of an intention, and the person still waits.

For carers at home, continence is one of the commonest reasons a care arrangement becomes
unsustainable and one of the commonest triggers for a move into residential care. That makes it a
problem with consequences far larger than its clinical description suggests, and it is worth
treating as such.

## What an examiner digs into next

The storage-and-voiding division, and why coordination is a third function rather than a property
of the two. Each pattern's mechanism, and which first-line intervention follows from it. Overflow
in both directions: why the symptom inverts the cause, and what the wrong treatment does. What has
to be excluded before this is called a continence problem, and which features leave the pathway
entirely. Why the chain is mostly outside the bladder in an inpatient. Why catheterisation is not
a continence management — m038 is the full argument. The difference between moisture-associated
skin damage and pressure damage, from m004. And how the local service is organised: who assesses,
who scans bladders, what the referral route is, and what the local continence service will and
will not see, all of which are institutional.

## Scope and safety

This explains the mechanisms behind continence and what a continence assessment has to separate,
for someone already training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a continence
assessment tool, not a care plan and not a guide to managing anyone's symptoms. It names no
residual volume, no drug, no dose, no toileting interval and no referral threshold, because all of
those are set by the reader's national guidance and local policy, which are the authority. Nothing
here has had clinical review. New incontinence, incontinence with pain or visible blood, and
incontinence with any new neurological symptom — weakness, altered sensation around the perineum,
or new bowel symptoms — need assessment through the reader's local urgent pathway and are not
matters for a revision answer. If someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the
correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The control-problem framing, the storage-and-voiding division and the overflow inversion are
physiology and do not date. The pattern classification is stable in substance, though the
preferred terminology has shifted more than once — "overactive bladder" against "urge
incontinence", and the retirement of older labels — and a reader should expect the words in their
local documents to differ from the words here. What moves faster is drug treatment for urgency,
where the balance between agent classes and the weight given to anticholinergic burden in older
people has changed and is still changing; and the device and procedural options, which differ
considerably by country and by what the local service commissions. The position on catheterisation
for containment has hardened over the last two decades in the same direction everywhere, but the
specific local bundle is institutional. The reader's national guidance and local continence
service are the authority throughout.
