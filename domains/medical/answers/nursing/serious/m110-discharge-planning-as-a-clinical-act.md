# Nothing about the illness changes at the door. Everything about who would notice does.

A person crossing the threshold out of a hospital loses, in one step, continuous observation, a
call bell, someone who administers their medicines, a kitchen that produces food whether or not
they feel like cooking, and an escalation route that answers. Their disease is identical on both
sides of the door. The system around it is not, and the whole of discharge planning is the work of
making the second side safe enough for the first.

That is why it is a clinical act and not an administrative one. The decision is a prediction: that
this person's trajectory, in *that* environment, with *that* support, is one they will come
through — and if any term in the prediction is wrong, the failure appears as a readmission, a
fall, a missed result or a deterioration nobody saw.

Markers used below. (**mechanism**) means it follows from the structure of the transition;
(**definitional**) means it is what the word means; (**consensus**) means mainstream agreement
across major guidance; (**country-dependent**) means the reader's own system decides. No
timescale, interval or threshold appears here; discharge processes are among the most
country-dependent things in this directory.

## Why it has to start on admission, which is a mechanism and not a slogan

The reason is a difference in time constants. (**mechanism**)

An acute illness can resolve in days. Arranging a package of personal care, an equipment delivery,
an adaptation, a care-home placement, a community nursing referral or a family conversation takes
longer than that, and the steps are largely sequential and performed by other organisations. If
the arrangement only begins when the clinical problem is resolved, the total stay is the illness
*plus* the arrangement, and every day of the difference is an inpatient day with its own hazards —
deconditioning (m072), hospital-acquired infection (m036), delirium, a device nobody reviewed
(m038) and a falls risk in an unfamiliar room (m040).

This is why guidance in most systems asks for an expected date of discharge and a provisional plan
early in the admission, revised as the picture changes, rather than a decision made on the day.
(**consensus**; the specific mechanism, the named roles and the documentation are
(**country-dependent**).) The purpose of an early date is not to predict accurately. It is to make
the arrangement run in parallel with the treatment instead of after it.

## The seven things that have to be true

```
   1  THE TRAJECTORY       the acute problem is on a course that does not need inpatient-level
                           observation — or the alternative level of support is NAMED and in place.
                           This is a judgement about direction, not about a number being normal
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   2  THE MEDICINES        reconciled against what they took before; every change explained and
                           the REASON recorded; a supply that exists; and an honest answer to
                           "can this person, or their carer, actually administer this?"
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   3  THE FUNCTION         assessed against the ACTUAL environment — their stairs, their toilet,
                           their chair, their household — and not against a flat ward floor
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   4  THE DESTINATION      exists, is agreed, and is ready BEFORE departure. A referral made on
                           the day of discharge is not a destination
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   5  THE OUTSTANDING      every pending result has a NAMED owner and a stated action. This is
      RESULTS              the commonest single structural failure in the whole process
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   6  THE FOLLOW-UP        booked, with its purpose understood by the person, plus explicit
      AND SAFETY-NET       safety-netting: what to look out for, what to do, and who to contact.
                           m012 is the full argument about what separates a safety-net that works
                           from reassurance that sounds like one
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   7  THE INFORMATION      has arrived with the person AND with whoever takes over. A summary
                           that arrives after the first community contact has failed, however
                           good it is
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

Two of those deserve expansion because they are where the harm concentrates.

**Medicines.** Admission and discharge are the points at which medication discrepancies cluster —
omissions, duplications, drugs stopped during the admission that silently restart, and drugs
started for the admission that silently continue. (**consensus**) m003 is the full systems
argument and m043 the adherence one; the discharge-specific point is that the list changes *and*
the person administering it changes *at the same moment*, so a change that was safe under
supervision may not be safe unsupervised. The question "who is going to give this, from what, and
how will they know it changed?" is a clinical question.

**Outstanding results.** A pending investigation at discharge is an open loop, and the loop closes
only if a specific person is named with a specific action. Unowned results are a recognised and
repeatedly reported source of serious harm, including missed malignancies, and the mechanism is
structural rather than careless: the team that ordered it has dispersed, the receiving clinician
did not order it and may not know it exists, and the system that files it does not distinguish
"seen and normal" from "never opened". (**consensus**) That is m073's collapse again, in the
results inbox.

## The failure modes, which are mostly structural

**"Medically fit for discharge."** The phrase does real work and also does harm, because it
quietly reclassifies everything remaining — mobility, continence, cognition, who is at home,
whether there is food — as *not clinical*. Those are the terms that determine whether the person
comes back. A discharge is safe or unsafe as a whole; the medical sub-component being resolved is
necessary and not sufficient. (**mechanism**)

**Timing.** Discharges late in the day and at the end of the week are associated with worse
outcomes in several health systems, and the mechanism is not mysterious: pharmacy, equipment,
transport, community services and the person's own general practice are variously closed, and the
first few hours at home are the least supported ones. (**consensus**; the size of the effect and
the local arrangements are (**country-dependent**).)

**Bed pressure as a force on a clinical decision.** It is real, it is not acknowledged in most
written processes, and pretending otherwise makes it less visible rather than less powerful. The
honest version is that the decision carries a competing interest and the defence against it is a
documented plan that states what was arranged, not an assertion that the pressure does not exist.

**Readmission used as a quality metric.** Readmission is a genuine signal of planning failure and
is also confounded by illness severity, by how good local access is, and by coding. A service with
a low readmission rate may have better discharge planning or may have a community service that
cannot admit anyone. (**consensus** that it is confounded; the metric's local definition is
(**country-dependent**).) It is a question-generating number, not an answer.

**The transfer of responsibility that nobody stated.** The commonest version is a plan that
assumes a family member will do something that nobody asked them about, and the second commonest
is each of two services believing the other owns the next step. m002 and m070 both turn on this: a
handover is a transfer of responsibility and it needs a point at which it demonstrably happened.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Discharge is a day with an enormous amount riding on it and almost no clinical drama, which is
precisely why it gets delegated downward and compressed into an afternoon.

Most people want to go home, and want it strongly enough to understate how they will manage. "I'll
be fine" is said by people who will not be fine, and it is said sincerely, because home is where
they want to be and because being a burden is something most people will accept risk to avoid.
Taking that answer at face value is not respecting autonomy; it is declining to do the assessment.
Taking it seriously means asking about the specifics — the stairs, the bathroom, the shopping, who
is actually there on a Tuesday — which produces a different and more useful answer.

Families and carers are frequently the plan without having agreed to be. A relative who is told
what has been arranged, rather than asked what they can do, often says yes in the room and cannot
sustain it, and that is where arrangements break down in the first fortnight. Carers are also
entitled to an assessment of their own in many systems, and the proportion who get one is low.

There is distress attached to both directions. Being kept in when you are ready is its own harm
and people experience it as being trapped. Being sent home before the support exists is
frightening and is commonly experienced as having been pushed out. People are discharged to
residential care after an admission that began as something small, and that is a permanent change
to someone's life decided in a week by people they met a fortnight ago — which is a reason for the
conversation to involve them properly and early, not a reason to avoid the decision.

Where someone wishes to leave against advice, or where there is a question about whether they can
make that decision, the subject moves into capacity, consent and in some cases safeguarding. That
is a legal framework, it differs profoundly between countries, it is not resolved at the bedside
by a revision answer, and it is the reader's own law and local safeguarding route that applies.
The practical point that does generalise: someone leaving against advice still needs the plan, the
medicines and the safety-netting, and withholding them as a consequence of the decision is
indefensible.

And the context: discharge planning fails mostly for structural reasons — the plan lives in
several systems, the community services are commissioned separately, the summary is written by the
person with the least time, and nobody owns the week after. An institution that responds to a
readmission by asking ward staff to plan better, without changing any of those, has identified the
wrong variable.

## What an examiner digs into next

What changes at the door, function by function. The time-constant argument for starting on
admission, and why an early expected date is a parallelism device rather than a prediction. The
seven conditions, with medicines reconciliation and outstanding results expanded. Why "medically
fit" is necessary and not sufficient. Why late-in-the-day and end-of-week discharges carry
additional risk. Why readmission is a confounded metric. What a usable discharge summary contains
and when it has to arrive. Who the plan assumes will do things, and whether they were asked. And
the local answers: who coordinates discharge, what the local process and documentation are, how
outstanding results are owned, what a carer is entitled to, and what the legal framework is for
someone leaving against advice.

## Scope and safety

This explains why discharge is a clinical decision and what has to be true before it is safe, at
the level of someone already training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a discharge
checklist, not a local process and not a basis for deciding whether any particular person is ready
to leave. It states no timescale, no interval and no criterion, because discharge processes,
documentation standards and the roles involved differ more between countries than almost anything
else in this directory. Nothing here has had clinical review. Decisions about capacity, about
discharge against advice and about where someone will live belong to the reader's own legal
framework and local safeguarding route and are named here rather than explained. Nothing here is
for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care. If someone is unwell right now,
the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument does not date: six functions are withdrawn at once, the time constant of
arranging support is longer than that of an acute illness, and an unowned result is an open loop.
Those are properties of transitions and not of any era's policy. Almost everything else here
moves, and moves differently in different countries. Where the boundary between hospital and
community sits, who commissions and pays for post-discharge support, whether discharge-to-assess
or similar models are in use, the standards for summary content and timeliness, and the role
titles of the people who coordinate it have all changed repeatedly in the last decade and will
continue to. The electronic layer — shared records, automated summaries and
results-acknowledgement systems — is moving fastest, and its own failure modes are not yet as well
characterised as the paper ones were. The reader's national transitions guidance, institutional
discharge policy and legal framework are the authority throughout.
