# A device is a breach in the body held open on purpose, and the organisms do not know why.

Intact skin and mucosa are the body's principal barrier against the organisms that live on and
around it. Every indwelling device — a urinary catheter, a peripheral cannula, a central venous
catheter, a tracheostomy, a gastrostomy, a surgical drain, any implanted prosthetic material —
crosses that barrier and **keeps it crossed**. That is not a side effect of the device. It is what
the device is. *Mechanism.*

Markers used below: *mechanism* means checkable by reasoning; *definitional* means it is what the
word means; *consensus* means mainstream agreement across major guidance; *country-dependent*
means the reader's own policy decides.

## Two mechanisms, not one

The breach is the obvious half. The **surface** is the half that explains why device infections
behave differently from other infections.

Organisms adhering to an indwelling surface form a biofilm: an adherent community in a
self-produced matrix, within which they are physiologically different from free-floating organisms
— slower-growing, and far less susceptible both to host immune effectors and to antimicrobials
that would clear the same species in the bloodstream. *Mechanism, and consensus in the
microbiology literature.*

The clinical consequence follows directly and is the single most useful thing to take from this
topic: **for an established device-associated infection, the definitive treatment is often removal
of the device rather than more or better antimicrobial.** An antimicrobial course given around a
colonised surface treats what has shed from it and leaves the reservoir in place. *Consensus.*

```
   the two routes, which need different defences
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   AT INSERTION — one event, one operator, one chance
     organisms carried in from the skin or the periurethral flora at the moment
     of insertion. Defence: aseptic technique, skin preparation, sterile field,
     the right device chosen for the expected duration, an operator who does it
     often enough to do it well
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   DURING DWELL — continuous, every operator, every shift, for as long as it is in
     organisms ascending the outside of the device, entering at a broken closed
     system, introduced at a hub or a sampling port, or carried on hands.
     Defence: hand hygiene before handling, a closed drainage system that stays
     closed, securement so the device does not move in the tract, the bag kept
     below bladder level and off the floor, aseptic access to hubs, the dressing
     intact, and no routine irrigation or routine replacement "to be safe"
   ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Insertion risk is a fixed cost. Dwell risk is a RATE, and a rate multiplied by
   time is the term that grows without anybody doing anything.
```

## Why duration is the dominant term

Because dwell risk is a rate. Risk of catheter-associated urinary tract infection accrues per
catheter-day; the same shape holds for vascular access. *Consensus.* Multiply a per-day hazard by
a number of days and the arithmetic is unforgiving — which means the interventions with the
largest effect are not better products or better technique. They are **not inserting the device**
and **taking it out sooner**. Everything else is a refinement on a term that only grows.

And that is why avoidable dwell-days are where the whole topic concentrates. Nobody decides to
leave a catheter in for five weeks. It stays because the decision to insert it was made once and
the decision to keep it was never made at all — it was inherited, shift after shift, by people who
assumed somebody upstream had a reason. The named defences all attack that specific failure: a
documented indication recorded at insertion, a documented review of continued need at a defined
interval, a prompt or stop-order that forces the question, and removal as the default rather than
continuation as the default. *Consensus that these are the defences; the interval and the
mechanism are country-dependent.*

## The trade is real in both directions

A urinary catheter is genuinely indicated for some things: urinary retention, accurate output
measurement in critical illness, selected surgery and selected postoperative situations, some
wound and skin management where urine is causing harm, some situations in end-of-life care, and
the management of some urological conditions. *Consensus; the local list is the authority.*

It is not indicated for the management of incontinence, for the convenience of the people
providing care, or because fluid balance would be easier to chart — and those three account for a
large share of the catheter-days that should not exist. *Consensus.* The honest version of the
teaching is not "catheters are bad". It is that the benefit is specific and bounded while the cost
accrues daily, so the two have to be re-weighed, not weighed once.

And the harm ledger is wider than infection, which is routinely underweighted:

* **Trauma and discomfort** — urethral injury, bypassing, bladder spasm, and the pain of a
  traumatic removal.
* **Immobility.** A catheter and a bag make getting out of bed harder, which feeds deconditioning,
  pressure damage and falls risk.
* **Continence assessment stops.** Nobody can assess continence in a catheterised person, so the
  decision defers itself indefinitely and some people go home with a catheter they did not need.
* **Delirium.** Devices and tethering contribute, and a catheter is a recognised precipitant.
* **Dignity**, which is not a soft consideration and is the reason many people give for refusing
  one.

## The colonisation trap

Catheterised patients become bacteriuric. Over time this approaches universal, it is colonisation
rather than infection, and treating it in an asymptomatic person is one of the largest single
sources of unnecessary antimicrobial use in hospital and in long-term care. *Consensus.* Two
corollaries follow and both are examined:

**Urine dipstick is close to useless in a catheterised patient**, and of limited value in frail
older people generally, because a positive result is expected and does not distinguish
colonisation from infection. *Consensus.*

**"Confused, so send a urine sample" is a reasoning error**, and a consequential one. Delirium in
an older person has a long differential — the drug chart, pain, constipation, retention, hypoxia,
dehydration, a new infection anywhere, an unfamiliar environment — and a positive culture from a
catheterised bladder is weak evidence for any of it. The sample does not rule out the other
causes, and acting on it as though it does is how the real precipitant gets missed. *Consensus.*

## Vascular access, briefly, because the structure is identical

A peripheral cannula that nobody can justify is the same problem in a different tract: a breach, a
surface, a rate, and a device that nobody revisits. The device is chosen for the **expected
duration** and the **properties of what goes through it**, which is why a peripherally inserted
central catheter and a short cannula are not interchangeable. Phlebitis scoring exists so that the
decision to resite is triggered by a recorded observation rather than by whoever happens to look.
Central access carries insertion and maintenance components in exactly the same way, and the
dressing and the hubs are the maintenance half. *Consensus; the scoring tool and the review
intervals are country-dependent.*

## The human stakes, said plainly

Device-associated infections are common, they are a substantial share of all healthcare-associated
infection, and some of them are fatal. They are also among the most preventable, which is why they
are counted and reported in a way that most hospital-acquired harm is not.

Alongside that: for some people an indwelling device is what makes life tolerable, and a long-term
catheter can be the right answer rather than a failure of care. A culture that treats every
catheter as a defect gets the easy half right and the hard half wrong. And the discomfort,
indignity and loss of independence that a device imposes are experienced by a person, not recorded
as a rate — which is precisely why they get left out of the ledger that decides whether it stays
in.

## What an examiner digs into next

What the local list of indications actually says, and which three reasons are explicitly not on
it. How continued need is reviewed locally, by whom, and how often. Why removal is sometimes the
treatment rather than the aftermath. Why a dipstick is unhelpful in a catheterised patient. What
the whole differential for delirium in an older person contains, so that a urine result does not
close it. And which part of the local care bundle is about insertion and which part is about every
day afterwards.

## Scope and safety

This explains why a class of device carries the risk it does, for someone already training in or
qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a decision aid, and it names no review
interval, no insertion technique and no infection rate, deliberately. The catheter and vascular
access policies of the institution where someone works are the authority for all of it; this is
not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision
about any person's care, including any decision about anyone's own device. If someone is unwell
right now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanisms are stable: a breach, a surface, a per-day rate. What dates is the procedural layer
— which bundle elements are mandated, the review interval and whether it is prompted
electronically, device materials and coatings and the repeatedly disappointing evidence for them,
phlebitis scoring tools, and the local surveillance definitions that decide what counts as a
device-associated infection in the first place. Those definitions matter more than they look,
because a reported rate is only comparable to another rate collected under the same definition.
The reader's own policy and surveillance definitions are the authority.
