# Handover is a transfer of accountability. The format exists to stop it leaking.

Structured handover formats exist because handover is not a conversation, it is a **transfer**,
and transfers lose things. The most widely taught format is SBAR — Situation, Background,
Assessment, Recommendation. *That SBAR is in mainstream use is uncontroversial; which variant is
in force is not.* Institutions run ISBAR with an identification step, ISBAR3 with risk,
responsibility and a read-back, SBARD with an explicit decision, and other local lettering
entirely, so a reader should expect the format on their own ward's card to differ in its limbs
from the one they were taught.

Escalation criteria are written as thresholds for a different reason, and it is not that
thresholds are more accurate than judgement. **It is that a threshold transfers between people and
judgement does not.** A first-day registered nurse and a nurse of twenty years, at three in the
morning, with the same patient in front of them, should produce the same escalation. Only a
written criterion can do that.

## What handover is actually doing, and the four things it loses

Handover is one of the highest-risk moments on a ward because three things change at once: who
holds the information, who holds the responsibility, and who the patient can turn to. *That
handover is a recognised high-risk transition is consensus across major patient-safety guidance.*

one of them:

Two of those deserve expanding.

**The Assessment limb is the one that gets skipped.** A less experienced clinician commonly
presents observations and leaves the interpretation to the receiver, partly out of appropriate
caution about diagnosing and partly because stating an assessment is exposing. But the bedside
clinician holds information the receiver cannot get on the phone: what the patient looked like an
hour ago, how fast this changed, whether this is like them. "I think she is becoming septic" may
be wrong; it is still the single most useful sentence in the call, because it tells the receiver
what to rule out first. *This is human-factors reasoning drawn from the patient-safety literature,
not a guideline instruction.*

**The ask has to be explicit, including the timeframe.** "Can you come and look at her" and "I
need you here within ten minutes" are different requests, and the second one can be refused in a
way that is visible. A request that is never stated cannot be declined, which sounds convenient
and is in fact how a patient ends up with nobody coming.

**And the loop has to close.** Repeating critical information back — the name, the number, the
plan — is a standard defence against mishearing, and it is consensus across major guidance for
telephone and verbal communication of critical results. The sender confirms; the receiver repeats;
the record says who now holds it.

## Shift handover and an escalation call are different problems

Treating them as one tool is a common error. Shift handover is **broad and low-urgency**: many
patients, limited time, and the dominant risk is omission across a list. An escalation call is
**narrow and high-urgency**: one patient, and the dominant risk is that the severity does not
transmit. Many institutions run different structures for each, with the shift version supported by
a written or electronic summary rather than being purely verbal. *Whether handover happens at the
bedside, who must attend, and what written record is required are institutional decisions that
vary widely.*

## Why the criteria are numbers

```
   a threshold does five things judgement alone cannot
   ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   1  it is the SAME for everyone     seniority, confidence and personality stop
                                      being inputs to whether help is called
   2  it is a MANDATE, not a request  "the protocol says I have to call you" removes
                                      the need to justify calling, which is the real
                                      barrier most of the time
   3  it specifies the RESPONSE       not just that someone is told, but who, how
                                      fast, and what happens to monitoring meanwhile
   4  it is AUDITABLE afterwards      a chart plus a policy makes "should this have
                                      been escalated" answerable, which is how the
                                      system learns
   5  it is TEACHABLE on day one      a new starter can be safe before they are
                                      experienced, which is the point
```

Item 2 is the one that is usually underestimated. *The patient-safety literature is consistent
that the barrier to escalation is more often social and hierarchical than cognitive* — the junior
member of staff who has noticed, and who does not want to wake a senior colleague, be wrong in
front of them, or be the person who called unnecessarily. A written threshold converts a social
act into an administrative one. That is most of its value.

Many institutions also provide structured language for persisting when a concern is not acted on —
a graded sequence of phrasings ending in an explicit statement that the speaker is uncomfortable
and is escalating further. *That such tools exist is widespread; their exact wording is local.*

## Where thresholds fail

**They are calibrated on populations, and they are applied to individuals.** The remedy is a
documented individualised plan rather than informal local adjustment — the same remedy as for an
abnormal baseline on an observation chart, and for the same reason: an undocumented exception is
indistinguishable from an oversight.

**"Not triggering" gets used as reassurance.** A threshold states a condition under which help
*must* be called. It says nothing whatever about conditions under which help should not be. Every
mainstream escalation policy carries a route based on the concern of the person at the bedside,
independent of any number, and that route is the answer to this failure.

**A format can be performed rather than used.** A handover that recites four headings in order
while transferring nothing is worse than an unstructured one, because it looks complete. The test
is not whether the limbs were said; it is whether the receiver can state the plan and the worry.

## The human stakes, said plainly

The reason this apparatus exists is that reviews of patients who deteriorated unexpectedly keep
finding the same thing: somebody noticed, and the concern did not arrive. Not nobody noticed —
somebody noticed. The information existed, in the building, in a person who was worried, and it
did not get to the person who could act on it in time. Every structured format and every written
trigger value is a response to that specific failure. It is worth holding onto when a handover
format feels bureaucratic at the end of a long shift.

## What an examiner digs into next

Which limb of the local format carries the assessment, and what to say in it when uncertain. The
difference between a shift handover and an escalation call. What the local policy specifies at
each band — the person, the time, and the monitoring in between. How to persist when a concern is
not acted on, and what the local escalation-of-concern route is. And how an individualised plan
gets documented so that the exception is visible rather than inferred.

## Scope and safety

This explains why two communication tools are shaped the way they are, for someone already
training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol and not a decision aid, and
it deliberately names no trigger values, because escalation criteria and response times are set
institutionally and are revised. The handover standard and escalation policy where someone works
is the authority; this is not, and it has had no clinical review. Nothing here is for use in an
emergency or for a decision about any person's care. If someone is unwell right now, the local
emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The reasoning is stable: transfers lose information, formats defend against specific losses, and
written criteria transfer between people where judgement does not. What dates is the lettering.
Formats are revised, renamed and extended; trigger values and response ladders are reissued; and
electronic handover tools change what is automatic and what must still be said out loud. The
authority is the card on the reader's own ward, not any textbook's acronym.
