# Three decisions, three different constraints, and one phrase that answers none of them.

The intravenous route looks like one act and is three. **Which conduit** is a question about what
may physically pass and for how long. **How fast** is a question about the gradient between the
effect you want and the harm you can cause. **Which bag** is a question about which body
compartment the contents will end up in. The three are coupled only loosely: each can be right and
the combination still wrong, and the commonest errors in the subject are all errors of collapsing
them into one.

Fluid is a drug. It needs an indication, a volume, a rate and a review, exactly as any other
prescription does, and it is the drug most often given without all four. (**consensus**)

Markers used below. (**mechanism**) means it follows from physics or physiology;
(**definitional**) means it is what the word means; (**consensus**) means mainstream agreement
across major guidance; (**country-dependent**) means the reader's own policy decides. No rate, no
volume, no concentration, no catheter size and no dwell time appears anywhere in this answer, for
the reasons in Scope and safety.

## The line: it governs what may pass, not how fast

Peripheral veins tolerate solutions close to physiological osmolarity and pH. Solutions well
outside that range — concentrated glucose, parenteral nutrition, some drugs — and agents that
injure tissue if they escape the vein, require central venous access with the tip sitting in a
high-flow vessel, so that the infusate is diluted essentially at the moment it arrives.
(**consensus**, **mechanism**) The tip position is therefore part of the prescription and not a
radiological detail, and it is confirmed before certain infusions are started. The confirmation
method and who may authorise use is (**country-dependent**) and institutional.

The flow fact is the one most often got backwards. For laminar flow of a given fluid down a given
pressure gradient, flow rises with the **fourth power of the radius** and falls with the
**length**. (**mechanism**) A short wide peripheral cannula therefore delivers volume faster than
a long narrow central catheter, which is why rapid volume resuscitation is a peripheral act and
why "get central access" is not a synonym for "get fast access". The same relationship explains
why a long peripheral catheter and a short cannula of the same gauge are not interchangeable, and
why narrowing a line with a filter, a long extension or a partially positional tip changes the
rate without anyone having changed the setting.

Duration is the other axis, and it is m038's argument rather than this one's: a device's infection
hazard accrues per device-day, duration is the dominant term, and the decision to insert is made
once while the cost accrues daily. (**consensus**) The practical consequence for this answer is
that the expected duration of therapy is a device-selection input — a short cannula, a longer
peripheral catheter, a centrally inserted or peripherally inserted central catheter, or a
tunnelled or implanted device all exist because the duration question has different answers.

The complications belong to the line even when they are caused by the fluid, which is exactly why
these two decisions get confused:

| Complication | What it is | Where the decision actually went wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Phlebitis** | Inflammation of the vein — mechanical from movement and size, chemical from what is running, infective from organisms | Often the fluid or the site, not the insertion. Standardised phlebitis scoring exists so that the finding is recorded comparably rather than described |
| **Infiltration** | Leakage of a non-vesicant solution into surrounding tissue | The line's position, and the absence of a check before and during |
| **Extravasation** | Leakage of a **vesicant** into tissue — a tissue-injury emergency with a time-critical local management | Usually the line decision: the agent needed access it did not have, or the device was not confirmed before use |
| **Thrombosis** | Clot around or within the catheter | Device-to-vessel size ratio, tip position and dwell — all line decisions |
| **Air embolism and catheter fracture** | Rare and serious | Technique and handling, and the reason the local policy on priming, clamping and removal exists |

## The rate: the same volume, delivered differently, is a different treatment

Rate is where most of the harm lives, and the reason is mechanical. The body's capacity to
accommodate a volume is a rate-limited property: the same total can be absorbed without incident
over hours and cause pulmonary oedema over minutes. (**mechanism**) So circulatory overload is a
rate complication, not a volume complication, and so is the opposite failure — an inadequate rate
in genuine hypovolaemia is not conservative, it is simply slower treatment of shock.

For drugs the same logic is sharper. Some agents have harms that are entirely rate-dependent, and
infusions containing potassium are the standard teaching example: the total is unremarkable and
the rate is the safety-critical variable, which is why pre-mixed bags exist, why adding
concentrated electrolyte at the bedside is restricted or prohibited in many institutions, and why
this area has a long history as a named never-event. (**consensus**; the specific restrictions are
(**country-dependent**).) No rate appears here; the formulary and the local policy are the
authority.

A bolus and an infusion of the same drug are not the same intervention — they produce different
peak concentrations and different durations above whatever threshold matters — and that is m006's
and m044's territory rather than this answer's.

The delivery device is a fourth object that people forget is in the chain. A gravity set's rate
depends on the height of the bag, the position of the limb and the tubing; a volumetric pump
delivers the number entered into it; a syringe driver delivers a small volume accurately and is
the wrong instrument for a large one. Free-flow, wrong-rate entry and the wrong pump programme are
all documented harms, and this is m003's system argument applied to a machine: the defence is
double-checking at the point of programming and a design that makes the dangerous entry hard, not
individual carefulness. (**consensus**)

The indications are genuinely different, and a widely used framework separates resuscitation,
routine maintenance, replacement of ongoing losses, redistribution, and reassessment — a five-R
structure in common use, whose wording differs between countries. (**consensus**;
(**country-dependent**) for the version the reader is held to.) The error that framework exists to
prevent is the one with the worst history: hypotonic maintenance fluid given at a volume suited to
replacement produces hyponatraemia, the harm is neurological, and in children this has been the
subject of national safety alerts in several countries. (**consensus**)

And the measurement side belongs to m005: the fluid balance chart records what was prescribed and
charted rather than what the circulation did, a balanced chart is not a reassuring chart, and
urine output is the circulation signal that the chart happens to contain. This answer is the
prescribing half of that pair's question.

## What goes wrong when the three are made as one

* **"Put up some fluids."** No indication, no rate, no review. The bag gets chosen by what is in
  the cupboard and the rate by what the last one ran at.
* **Treating central access as fast access.** The fourth-power relationship says otherwise, and
  the delay in obtaining it is itself a harm in a bleeding or shocked person.
* **Running a maintenance fluid at a replacement rate, or the reverse.** The first is the
  hyponatraemia story above; the second is under-resuscitation documented as caution.
* **Choosing the line from the drug and then ignoring the rate**, or choosing the rate and
  assuming the line can take it. Extravasation of a vesicant through an unconfirmed device is the
  worst version of this and it is a line decision presenting as a fluid complication.
* **Never stopping.** A maintenance infusion continued after the person started drinking is the
  commonest version of fluid-related harm, and it is m038's unreviewed-device structure in another
  form. The review point is the intervention.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Cannulation hurts, and it hurts more for the people who need it most. The person with difficult
veins — oedematous, dehydrated, previously treated with chemotherapy, a long-term intravenous drug
user, a child — is the person who gets attempted repeatedly, and the number of attempts is rarely
recorded anywhere. People describe repeated attempts as one of the worst parts of an admission,
and some decline treatment rather than face them again. A limit on attempts before escalating to
someone more skilled or a different device is a kindness and also a clinical decision; most
institutions have one and it is worth knowing what the local one is.

A drip also tethers. It makes walking to the toilet a two-object manoeuvre, makes dressing and
washing difficult, wakes people when the pump alarms, and interacts directly with the continence
and falls arguments elsewhere in this specialty: an infusion running overnight is a reason someone
gets up in the dark holding a stand. None of that appears in the prescribing decision and all of
it is real.

For people on long-term intravenous treatment the access device becomes part of their body and
part of their identity, and they usually know more about its care than the person in front of
them. Asking is not a formality.

And the structural point: the commonest reason fluids are prescribed badly is that the three
decisions live in three places — a chart, a pump and a bag — and nobody owns the fourth decision,
which is when to stop. A service that addresses a fluid incident by retraining individuals without
changing where the review is recorded has identified the wrong variable.

## What an examiner digs into next

The three decisions, and an example of each being right while the combination is wrong. Why
osmolarity, pH and vesicant potential drive the access decision and why tip position is part of
the prescription. The fourth-power relationship, and why central access is not fast access. The
difference between infiltration and extravasation and why the second is time-critical. Why
circulatory overload is a rate complication. Why potassium-containing infusions are a rate-safety
problem and what the local restriction is. Where each class of fluid distributes and what that
makes it for. The hypotonic-maintenance hyponatraemia story and why the framework exists. And the
local answers: who may cannulate, who may access a central device, how tip position is confirmed,
what the phlebitis tool is, what the attempt limit is, and who reviews the infusion daily.

## Scope and safety

This explains the structure of an intravenous prescribing decision, at the level of someone
already training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a fluid prescribing guide, not a
cannulation procedure and not a drug administration reference. It states **no volume, no rate, no
concentration, no catheter gauge, no dwell time and no compatibility** — all of which are set by
the reader's formulary, national guidance and local policy, which are the authority. Nothing here
has had clinical review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for any decision about any
person's care; suspected extravasation in particular needs the local policy and the local team
immediately and not a revision answer. If someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number
is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physics does not date: compartment distribution follows tonicity, and flow follows radius and
length. The three-decision structure and the fluid-is-a-drug framing are now standard in national
guidance and are unlikely to reverse. What dates is the detail. The balanced-crystalloid against
saline question is an active trial literature and the balance has moved within the last decade.
Device selection has shifted considerably toward longer peripheral catheters and peripherally
inserted central catheters where the duration justifies them, and that movement is still happening
at different speeds in different countries. The restrictions around concentrated electrolytes, the
dwell and replacement rules for peripheral cannulae — including whether routine replacement is
recommended at all, which has changed — and the phlebitis tools in use are all institutional and
all differ. The reader's national fluid guideline, formulary and local vascular access policy are
the authority throughout.
