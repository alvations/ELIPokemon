# Oxygen has an indication, a dose, a route, a target, and a harm profile at both ends.

Oxygen is the treatment most often given, least often prescribed and least often reviewed. It is
also the one most frequently described as "giving some oxygen", a phrase that would be unusable
about any other drug and that quietly disposes of four of the five decisions above.

The reframe that makes the subject tractable is to insist on the drug analogy completely. There is
an indication, and it is not breathlessness. There is a dose, and it is not the flow. There is a
route, and the route changes the delivered dose by more than the flow does. There is a target, and
it is a range with two edges rather than a floor. And there is a harm profile at both ends of that
range, with different mechanisms at each end.

Markers used below. (**mechanism**) means it follows from physiology; (**definitional**) means it
is what the word means; (**consensus**) means mainstream agreement across major guidance;
(**country-dependent**) means the reader's own guidance decides. Every specific target range,
flow, device setting and threshold is deliberately absent, because all of them are
(**country-dependent**) and the reader's national guidance is the authority.

Writing a target range rather than a fixed flow is the structure most national oxygen guidance now
uses, and it exists because the required setting changes during an illness while the physiological
target does not. (**consensus**; the ranges themselves are (**country-dependent**).)

The upper-edge harm that matters most is the one with the clearest mechanism and the clearest
consequence. In a person with chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure, a high inspired
concentration can produce a rising carbon dioxide and a respiratory acidosis. The mechanism is
multifactorial and is better understood than it used to be: loss of the hypoxic contribution to
ventilatory drive is part of it, but reversal of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction worsening
ventilation–perfusion matching, and the Haldane effect releasing carbon dioxide from haemoglobin,
both contribute and are probably larger terms. (**consensus**, **mechanism**) The practical
consequence is that this group has a *different and lower* target range in most national guidance,
that the group is identified in advance where possible, and that an alert or a card held by the
person is the mechanism by which the information travels. (**consensus**; the ranges and the alert
mechanism are (**country-dependent**).)

The clinically important corollary is also the most counter-intuitive: **the right response to a
rising carbon dioxide is not to switch the oxygen off.** Hypoxia kills faster than hypercapnia, so
the response is a controlled reduction to the correct target with reassessment and escalation to
ventilatory support, not withdrawal. (**consensus**) The specific escalation route is the reader's
local one.

And at the other end: oxygen does nothing for breathlessness in a person who is not hypoxaemic.
The trials comparing oxygen with air in non-hypoxaemic breathlessness do not support a symptomatic
benefit beyond that of air, and the mechanism is unsurprising — there is no deficit to correct.
(**consensus**) What does help is the rest of the management of the cause, and the airflow itself,
which is why a fan is a reasonable intervention and an oxygen mask is not a substitute for one.

## Why the device is a separate decision from the flow

This is the part most often skipped, and it is pure mechanism. (**mechanism**)

The quantity that matters physiologically is the *inspired fraction* of oxygen the person actually
receives. With a **variable-performance** device — nasal cannulae, a simple face mask — the
delivered fraction depends on the set flow *and* on the person's own inspiratory flow, tidal
volume and respiratory rate, because the gap between the set flow and what they draw in is made up
with room air. A person breathing fast and deep entrains more room air and receives a *lower*
fraction from the same flowmeter setting. So the same number on the wall is a different dose in
two people, and a different dose in the same person an hour later.

With a **fixed-performance** device — an air-entrainment or Venturi system — the geometry entrains
a fixed ratio of air to oxygen, so the delivered fraction is approximately independent of
breathing pattern, provided the device's own minimum flow requirement is met. That is why these
are used where the fraction has to be known and controlled rather than merely increased.

## The monitoring does not measure the thing the upper-edge harm lives in

Pulse oximetry is the instrument the target range is written against, and its limits are the
reason a blood gas exists. m102 is the full argument and it is not re-derived here; the parts that
bear directly on oxygen are these. (**consensus**)

Oximetry reports the proportion of available haemoglobin that is carrying oxygen. It therefore
says nothing about how much haemoglobin there is, nothing about cardiac output, and — the point
that matters here — **nothing about carbon dioxide or pH**, which is where the harm from too much
oxygen is measured. A saturation inside the target range is entirely compatible with a rising
carbon dioxide, so the instrument that confirms the oxygen is right cannot detect the commonest
harm from getting it wrong. Only a blood gas reads that. (**mechanism**, **consensus**)

Three further limits are worth holding. The reading is unreliable with poor peripheral perfusion,
movement and some nail coverings. It can be normal or high in carbon monoxide poisoning, because
carboxyhaemoglobin is not distinguished from oxyhaemoglobin by the measurement. (**consensus**)
And accuracy is reduced, with a bias toward over-reading, in people with darker skin pigmentation
— a finding that is now mainstream, is the subject of active regulatory work in several countries,
and matters most precisely in the region where a clinical decision turns on whether the saturation
is inside the range or just below it. (**consensus**; the regulatory position is
(**country-dependent**) and moving.)

## Why it is the commonest unprescribed treatment

The failure is structural rather than careless, and it has the same shape as m038's device
argument. Oxygen is started in an emergency, correctly, by whoever is present — and most national
guidance explicitly permits that, with documentation afterwards. (**country-dependent**) The
decision to start is therefore made once, under pressure, by someone who may never see the person
again. Nothing in the subsequent days asks whether it is still needed.

So the characteristic error is not giving oxygen to someone who does not need it. It is giving
oxygen to someone who needed it on Tuesday, at a setting chosen on Tuesday, read against no stated
target, and still running on Friday. The review point is the intervention. (**mechanism**)

Two operational notes that follow. Oxygen supports combustion and is a fire risk, which is a real
and recurring source of serious harm and is the reason for the local rules about smoking and about
emollients and dressings near a mask. (**consensus**) And it is a finite piped and cylinder
resource with a consumption rate, which matters for transfers and in a surge.

## The human stakes, said plainly

A mask is not a neutral object to wear. It is claustrophobic for many people, it makes speech hard
and lip-reading impossible, it interferes with eating and drinking and with being understood, and
it dries the mouth and nose. People take them off, and the usual conclusion drawn — that they are
non-compliant — describes the prescription rather than the person. A nasal cannula is tolerated
better by most people and is often the difference between a treatment that is received and one
that is merely prescribed.

For someone who is frightened and breathless, the mask also arrives at the worst moment and from
behind. Being able to say what is happening, in short sentences, before putting it on, and
checking back afterwards, is not a courtesy — it is the thing that determines whether the
treatment stays on.

There is a further point that belongs here because it is routinely mishandled. For people with
long-term oxygen at home, the equipment reorganises a household: tubing across floors, a
concentrator that is audible at night, restrictions on who may smoke in the house, and a visible
marker of illness in the living room. Decisions about home oxygen are decisions about a home, and
the people who live in it have a legitimate say that is not a clinical variable.

And the context: the reason oxygen goes unprescribed and unreviewed is not that staff do not know
it should be. It is that the prescribing chart, the observation chart and the device are three
separate objects, often in three places, and the person adjusting the flowmeter frequently has no
documented target to adjust it against. An institution that responds to an oxygen incident by
asking people to be more careful, without changing that, has identified the wrong variable.

## What an examiner digs into next

That the indication is hypoxaemia rather than breathlessness, and what the trial evidence in
non-hypoxaemic breathlessness actually shows. Why a target range is prescribed rather than a flow,
and what the two standing ranges are for locally. The upper-edge mechanisms, with the
ventilation–perfusion and Haldane terms named and not only the hypoxic-drive one. Why the right
response to a rising carbon dioxide is controlled reduction and escalation rather than withdrawal.
The difference between variable-performance and fixed-performance delivery and what each one
controls. Why oximetry cannot detect the upper-edge harm, and what a blood gas adds. The
pigmentation bias, and where in the range it matters. And the local arrangements: who may
prescribe, who may titrate, where the target is written, what the alert mechanism is for people at
risk of hypercapnia, and what the fire rules are.

## Scope and safety

This explains why oxygen is treated as a prescribed drug and what each part of that prescription
is for, at the level of someone already training in or qualified for clinical practice. It is not
an oxygen guideline and not a prescribing aid. It states **no target saturation range, no flow, no
device setting, no inspired fraction and no threshold** of any kind, deliberately, because every
one of those is set by the reader's national guidance and local policy and differs between
countries — and because an oxygen number remembered from a revision answer is exactly the failure
mode this directory should not contribute to. Nothing here has had clinical review. Nothing here
is for use during an emergency or for any decision about any person's care. If someone is unwell
or breathless right now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The physiology is stable: the shape of the dissociation curve, the mechanisms at the upper edge,
and the difference between variable and fixed-performance delivery are not going to move. The
prescription-and-target-range structure is now near-universal in national guidance and is unlikely
to reverse. Everything numerical dates, and dates quickly — the specific target ranges, the
designation of who is at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, and the device recommendations
all differ by country and have all been revised more than once. The conservative-against-liberal
target question in acute illness is an active trial literature whose balance has shifted within
the last decade and may shift again. The pulse oximetry pigmentation question is the
fastest-moving item here: it is under regulatory review in several jurisdictions and the guidance
a reader finds will be newer than this. Their national respiratory society's oxygen guideline and
their own institution's policy are the authority throughout.
