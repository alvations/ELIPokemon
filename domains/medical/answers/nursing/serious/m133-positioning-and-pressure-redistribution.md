# A surface divides the load. Repositioning removes it. Only one of those produces a zero.

m004 is the argument that pressure damage is load multiplied by time rather than a cleanliness
problem, and it owns rather more than the time term: the ischaemic mechanism, the pressure-time
curve, shear and friction and microclimate as the three things that move it, the fact that damage
often starts deep, the anatomical sites, and device-related damage as a category are all argued
there and are **not re-argued here**. This answer is about the other factor in the product.
**Load is not one number.** It has a magnitude, a distribution and a direction; the three are
changed by different interventions and measured by different instruments; and the question this
answer exists to settle is why a support surface and a change of position are not alternatives.

Markers used below: (**mechanism**), (**definitional**), (**consensus**),
(**country-dependent**). No repositioning interval, angle tolerance, pressure value or risk score
appears anywhere here. Every one of those is set by the reader's national guidance and local
policy.

m004 establishes that the deepest damage is frequently not at the skin: load concentrates at the
interface between soft tissue and bone, so deep tissue can be injured while the surface still
looks intact (**consensus**). What belongs here is narrower and is about the instrument — the
surface measurement and the tissue at risk are in different places, so interface pressure is
being used to predict damage that is not where it is measured (**mechanism**).

Two consequences follow, and they are the practically useful ones. First, **a support surface and
repositioning are not alternatives**, because they act on different properties: the surface
reduces magnitude everywhere and the reposition produces a zero somewhere, and no magnitude
reduction is equivalent to a zero (**mechanism**). A surface that permits a longer interval is
still a surface that produces no zeros on its own. Second, **heels are the clearest case in the
whole subject**: there is very little soft tissue between the calcaneus and the skin, so there is
very little to redistribute into, and the only intervention that reliably works is taking the heel
out of the load path altogether (**consensus**).

## Why sitting is the hardest posture, and why raising the head of the bed is not free

Sitting concentrates most of the body's weight onto the ischial tuberosities, over a contact area
far smaller than lying produces — so magnitude per unit area is at its highest in the posture
people are most often encouraged into, and for the longest unbroken periods, because a chair is
where someone is put in order to be left safely (**mechanism**). Chair cushions redistribute;
they do not produce zeros either, and a person who cannot shift their own weight in a chair is
accumulating load throughout.

Raising the head of the bed is the commonest source of shear on a ward, and the mechanism is
worth stating precisely: the skin and the superficial tissue are held in place by friction against
the sheet while the skeleton and deep tissues slide downwards under gravity, which deforms the
tissue in between — at the sacrum above all (**mechanism**). The elevation is frequently
necessary for entirely unrelated reasons, which makes this a genuine trade rather than an error,
and the way the trade is handled is a matter of technique and of local policy rather than of
reasoning from first principles (**country-dependent**).

Sliding rather than lifting during a move does the same thing in a few seconds. This is the point
at which manual handling and pressure area care stop being two separate subjects.

## What the argument about angles is actually about

The familiar instruction to use a tilt rather than a full lateral turn is not about a number. It
is about not transferring the whole load onto the greater trochanter, which is as unforgiving a
prominence as the heel and considerably less often inspected (**mechanism**). The specific
angles, like the specific intervals, sit at the level of consensus and local protocol, and the
evidence for any particular frequency is weak — the trials are hard to do, the comparators differ,
and the surface in use changes the answer (**consensus**; the figures are
(**country-dependent**)).

What is not weak is the principle underneath: the schedule has to be individualised to the
person's skin response, their surface, their posture and their tolerance of being moved, and the
evidence that it is working is the skin itself (**consensus**). A fixed clock applied to a ward
is an administrative artefact, not a clinical plan.

## What the measurement leaves out

Everything above is about the load. What decides whether a given load does damage is the tissue's
tolerance of it, and that is in none of the numbers: perfusion, oxygenation, nutritional state,
age-related changes in the dermis, previous damage at the site, and the microclimate at the skin
all change how much load the tissue will take before it fails (**mechanism**, **consensus**; the
microclimate term is m004's). This is why two people on the same surface for the same time do not
have the same outcome, and why a risk tool's banded output is a prompt to assess rather than a
prediction. m004 and m071 carry the time term and the healing argument respectively; this answer
stops at the mechanics of load, and the terms it has deliberately not touched are the ones m004
already holds.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Repositioning is something done *to* a person, usually several times a day and often at night, and
the subject is almost always taught from the tissue's point of view.

It wakes people up. A schedule that is clinically sound and applied without negotiation produces
fragmented sleep, and fragmented sleep in hospital has consequences of its own. It can hurt — not
only in the obvious cases but in anyone with a painful joint, a recent operation or an injury —
and the question of how that is assessed and managed is deliberately outside this answer's scope
and belongs to local policy and to the person's own clinician. Being turned requires being
touched, often by somebody you met this morning, often in a way that exposes you, and the
difference between care and handling is almost entirely in whether you were told what was about to
happen and asked rather than instructed.

The hardest version is where the clinical schedule and the person's wishes conflict. Someone may
decline to be moved, repeatedly, for reasons that are entirely their own, and an adult who
understands what is at stake is entitled to that. Where capacity is uncertain, where the
preference is persistent and where the consequence is serious, this stops being a bedside
judgement and enters a framework about capacity, consent and best interests that differs
profoundly between countries. It is named here rather than reasoned about here, and the reader's
own legal framework is the only authority on it. The same applies at the end of life, where
comfort can displace prevention entirely, and where the right plan may be one that accepts the
risk — a decision made with the person and their family, recorded, and not a lapse in care.

And the context, because it determines whether any of this happens. Repositioning is
staffing-limited: it usually needs more than one person, it competes with everything else on the
shift, and it is the task most easily postponed because its omission has no immediate visible
consequence. Pressure damage is also unevenly distributed in ways that track frailty, deprivation
and length of stay rather than the quality of anybody's technique. An institution that answers its
pressure ulcer rate by auditing turn charts, without changing establishment or equipment, has
chosen the one variable individuals cannot move.

## What an examiner digs into next

mechanism of shear when the head of the bed is raised, and why sliding rather than lifting does
the same thing in seconds. What the tilt-versus-lateral argument is actually about, and why the
specific angles and intervals are consensus rather than mechanism. Device-related pressure injury
as its own category, with the mechanical reason it is a category. What determines tissue
tolerance, and why that makes a risk score a prompt rather than a prediction. And the local
answers: which surface for whom, how the schedule is individualised and recorded, and who decides
when it is declined.

## Scope and safety

This explains the mechanics of load in pressure ulcer prevention, for someone already training in
or qualified for clinical practice. It is not a repositioning policy, not a risk assessment tool
and not a guide to selecting equipment for anybody. It deliberately states no repositioning
interval, no angle, no interface pressure figure and no risk score, because all of those are set
by the reader's national guidance and institutional policy, which are the authority. It does not
address the assessment or management of pain associated with being moved, which is out of scope
here and belongs to local policy and to the person's own clinician. It is explicitly not a basis
for any decision where someone is declining to be repositioned, which involves capacity, consent
and best interests and belongs to the reader's legal framework. Nothing here has had clinical
review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's care. If
someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The mechanics do not date: force, area and direction are physics, and the observation that deep
tissue fails before skin shows it has been stable for a long time. The classification of pressure
injury and the terminology around it have been revised repeatedly and will be again, and the
international guideline is the place to check which version is current. What is genuinely
unsettled is repositioning frequency — the trial evidence is weak, the comparators are
inconsistent, and the interaction with the support surface is the plausible reason the results do
not converge — so expect the recommendations about intervals to keep moving while the principle of
individualisation stays put. Support surface technology, microclimate management and the use of
subepidermal moisture measurement and other early-detection devices are all moving faster than the
guidance about them. Everything procedural here is local, and the reader's national guidance and
institutional policy are the authority throughout.
