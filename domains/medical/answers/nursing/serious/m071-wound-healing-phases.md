# A wound that is not healing is held in a phase, and the phase names the obstacle.

Repair is a programme, not a rate. It runs in phases, each one producing the conditions the next
one needs, and the useful consequence is that a wound which has not changed in weeks is not a
wound that needs more time. It is a wound that is **arrested somewhere nameable**, and naming
where is the assessment. *This is mechanism rather than guideline, and the phase model itself is
definitional — it is the vocabulary of every wound-care text, not a recommendation.*

## Primary and secondary intention are the same programme with different geometry

A surgically apposed wound heals by **primary intention**: the gap is small, so proliferation has
little to fill and contraction little to do, and the result is a narrow scar. An open wound heals
by **secondary intention**: granulation tissue has to fill a cavity before epithelium can cross
it, so it takes longer and contracts more. A wound closed after a delay is **tertiary** or delayed
primary intention. *Definitional, and the words are international.* The phases are identical in
all three. Only the volume of work differs — which is why a sinus or an undermined edge changes
the timescale even when the surface area does not.

## What "stuck" actually means

The characteristic pattern in a chronic wound is a **prolonged inflammatory phase**: the signals
that should hand over to proliferation keep being re-issued because the stimulus for inflammation
has not been removed. *That the chronic wound is typically arrested in inflammation is consensus;
the molecular detail is an active research area and is not settled.* So the question at every
review is not "is it healing" but "what is holding it here":

**The clearest illustration is compression.** Compression is the central treatment for venous
ulceration and is hazardous in significant arterial insufficiency, and the two can look similar on
the surface. So an assessment of arterial supply precedes compression. *That the assessment
precedes compression is consensus; which test, which index and which cut-off, and who may apply
compression, are country-dependent and institution-dependent — and that is exactly the kind of
threshold this answer will not state.* One act, two diagnoses, opposite sign. That is the whole
argument for assessment-before-product in a single example.

## Why the dressing is downstream, and not a lesser question

A dressing holds a moisture condition and protects a surface — that is m004's subject and the
dressing classes are not repeated here. What it **cannot** do is raise tissue oxygen, remove dead
tissue, reduce oedema, take the load off a heel, or treat the disease underneath. When any of
those is the rate-limiting term, changing the dressing changes the dressing (**mechanism**).

Which gives the practical rule that makes this answer worth having: **a wound that has not changed
over a defined review period is a reason to repeat the assessment, not a reason to try another
product.** *Consensus; the length of that period, and the referral route when it expires, are set
by local policy.* Whether the review is at two weeks, four, or at a point defined by the wound
type is a local decision and is not given here.

## The human stakes, said plainly

A chronic wound is a long thing to live with. It leaks, it can smell, it dictates what someone can
wear and whether they can bathe, and dressing changes can be the part of the week a person dreads.
People with ulcers are frequently given the impression that slow healing is a failure of their own
compliance, and the phase model is an argument against that: the commonest reasons a wound is not
healing are perfusion, load, dead tissue and the underlying disease, none of which is a question
of effort.

Two further things are worth saying without any framing around them. First, healing is not always
the goal. For some wounds — in advanced illness, in a limb that cannot be revascularised — the aim
is comfort, containment of exudate, odour control and fewer disturbances, and choosing that is a
considered plan rather than a surrender. Second, the decision about whether healing is achievable
belongs to an assessment of the person, with them, and not to a model of phases.

## What an examiner digs into next

Which phase a described wound is in, and on what evidence. What holds a wound in inflammation and
how each obstacle is tested for. Why oxygen is the term that makes proliferation fail. Why
compression and arterial assessment are linked, and what the local rule is. What debridement
methods exist, which require specific competence, and which are not used in which circumstances.
Why delayed primary closure exists. And what the local review interval is before a non-progressing
wound is escalated, and to whom.

## Scope and safety

This explains a model of repair and what follows from it, for someone already training in or
qualified for clinical practice. It is not a protocol, not a decision aid and not a wound
assessment tool, and it deliberately names no dressing, no debridement method for a particular
wound, no perfusion index, no cut-off and no review interval. Compression, debridement and the
management of an infected or non-healing wound are governed by local policy and by the competence
of the person doing them; that policy is the authority and this is not, and none of it has had
clinical review. Nothing here is for use in an emergency or for a decision about any person's
wound, including the reader's own. If someone is unwell right now, the local emergency number is
the correct response.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The phase model is old and stable, and the mechanistic claims above — oxygen-limited
proliferation, load as a recurring insult, the inflammatory arrest of chronic wounds — are not in
dispute at the level stated. What moves is the layer underneath: biofilm, the molecular signatures
of non-progressing wounds, and the evidence for individual advanced therapies, all of which are
active research. What moves fastest is the procedural layer — review intervals, referral criteria,
debridement competence frameworks and compression rules — and those differ by country already. The
reader's own policy is the authority for all of it.
