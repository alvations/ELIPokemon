# These few are not the far end of a familiar dial, so reasoning by severity gets them wrong

Most complications of cancer and its treatment sit on a scale. Sickness, fatigue, a falling blood
count, a sore mouth: each is a quantity that can be larger or smaller, each gets worse as the
disease or the dose gets heavier, and the levers against each are the ones already in hand —
reduce, delay, support, treat the symptom. For those, the instinct that serves a clinician well is
*this is worse than usual, so do more of the usual, sooner*.

A small number of complications are not on that scale, and the instinct fails on them in a
specific way. In each case the quantity that is dangerous is **not** the quantity that tracks
disease severity, so the familiar levers are not connected to the thing that will cause harm. They
are emergencies because of their mechanism and not because of their magnitude, and the correct
response is derivable from the mechanism rather than from how bad the person looks.

This answer sorts four of them by the structural reason each is an emergency. The sorting is the
point; the list is only the evidence for it. It also says nothing about what will happen to any
person, because that is not a question a structural argument can answer and not a question this
domain takes.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: (**mechanism**) (derivable
from how the biology or the measurement works), (**definitional**) (true because a classification
says so), (**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**)
(varies by nation, region or institution, and changes).

## What an ordinary complication is, and why severity reasoning works on it

Worth stating explicitly, because the contrast carries the whole argument.

An ordinary complication has a magnitude that is some monotone function of the inputs — the
tumour burden, the dose given, the number of cycles, the organ function to start with
(**mechanism**). Severity and cause move together, so the readout is informative about both; and
because the mechanism is the same one at every severity, a response that works at low severity
works harder at high severity. This specialty's answer on why toxicity is predictable sets out the
reason the list of affected tissues and the timing of their damage both follow from turnover rate,
and the answer on the differences between systemic therapies sets out how each class's toxicity
profile follows from its mechanism. Both describe dials.

Dials also behave well under grading. An ordinal severity scale is useful exactly because the
thing it grades is one thing at different amounts, and a change in grade means a change in amount
(**definitional**). That is what makes toxicity grading the workhorse of trial reporting and of
dose modification.

The four below break that in four different places, and knowing *which* place is broken is what
tells you what to do.

## Shape one: the window, where the determinant is elapsed time

**Metastatic compression of the spinal cord** is the clearest case. The compressing lesion is
usually extradural and usually arises in bone; the cord tolerates compression for a while and then
does not (**mechanism**). The clinically decisive fact is that the neurological state when
treatment begins is the strongest determinant of the neurological state afterwards, and recovery
of function already lost is much less reliable than preservation of function still present
(**consensus**). Pain typically precedes neurological signs, often by a substantial interval,
which is what makes the history the instrument rather than the examination.

Nothing in that is a statement about severity. Someone with early signs and someone with
established signs may be equally unwell; the difference is that one of them is still inside the
window and the other is not, and the current picture does not say how long the compression has
been there. So the response is keyed to the clock: urgent imaging of the whole spine rather than
of the painful level alone, because multiple levels are frequently involved, and a treatment
decision — radiotherapy, surgical decompression, or both, with systemic measures alongside —
made without waiting for the picture to declare itself (**consensus**, with pathway details
strongly (**country-dependent**)).

**Raised pressure inside the skull** from intracranial disease has the same shape with a
mechanical reason behind it. The cranial cavity is a nearly fixed volume, so added volume is
initially absorbed by displacement of cerebrospinal fluid and blood; once that compliance is
exhausted, a small further increase in volume produces a large increase in pressure
(**mechanism**). The consequence is that the relationship between how much disease there is and
how dangerous the situation is turns a corner, and the position of the corner is not visible from
outside.

## Shape two: the missing readout, where the signs are made by what is absent

**Infection during profound neutropenia** is an emergency for a reason that has nothing to do with
how bad the infection is.

Most of what clinicians use to localise and grade an infection is produced by neutrophils:
purulence, the radiographic infiltrate, the local signs of inflammation, much of the visible
reaction at a line site (**mechanism**). Remove the neutrophils and you remove the signal, not the
process. Fever may be the only manifestation, and in some people even fever is absent or blunted
(**consensus**).

Two consequences follow, and both are structural rather than cautionary.

First, **the absence of localising signs carries almost no information** about whether there is a
serious infection (**mechanism**). This is the hardest part to internalise, because in every other
setting a normal chest examination and a clear film are worth something.

Second, **treatment has to precede the information**. Empirical broad-spectrum antibacterial
therapy is started on suspicion, before the source is known and long before an organism is
identified, with cultures taken but not waited for (**consensus**). Services audit the interval
from recognition to the first dose rather than the interval to a diagnosis, which is the
operational expression of the same point (**consensus**, strongly (**country-dependent**) in which
agents and which pathway).

It is worth being precise about what is *not* being claimed. Neutropenia does not make an
infection more severe in some mysterious way; it removes a host response that both contains the
organism and generates the evidence. The emergency is in the epistemics as much as in the biology.

## Shape three: the driver is remote from the bulk

**Hypercalcaemia of malignancy** is the archetype. The mainstream account is that most cases are
humoral — a factor released by the tumour acts on bone and kidney at a distance, with a
parathyroid-hormone-related peptide mechanism the usual one — alongside a local osteolytic
mechanism where bone is directly involved, and a less common route in some lymphomas acting
through the vitamin D pathway (**mechanism**, **consensus**).

The structural consequence is that the dangerous quantity is a circulating concentration, not a
tumour dimension. It is therefore **decoupled from bulk**: the relationship between how much
tumour there is and how high the calcium is is loose, and a small tumour can produce a large
effect. Symptoms are non-specific — thirst, polyuria, constipation, nausea, confusion — so it is
found by considering it rather than by it announcing itself (**consensus**). And the first
intervention is directed at the mechanism and not at the cancer: restoration of circulating
volume, because the renal handling of calcium is volume-dependent, and then antiresorptive
therapy, because the bone resorption is the source (**consensus**). Treating the tumour is the
durable answer and is not the immediate one.

**Obstruction of the superior vena cava** is the same decoupling with geometry instead of
chemistry. What matters is the cross-sectional consequence of a mass in a confined space, how
quickly it developed, and whether collateral venous channels have had time to open — so the
severity of the syndrome tracks the rate of onset rather than the size of the tumour
(**mechanism**). Two further points are worth holding. The historical teaching that this condition
requires treatment before a diagnosis has been substantially revised: where it is safe to do so, a
histological diagnosis first is preferred, because the right treatment depends on the tumour type
and some of the causes are highly treatable (**consensus**). And genuine airway compromise, rather
than facial swelling alone, is what converts it into an immediate-threat situation
(**consensus**).

## Shape four: caused by the treatment working, so it belongs to prevention

**The metabolic syndrome that follows rapid tumour lysis** inverts the usual logic. Intracellular
contents — potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids whose breakdown yields urate — are released
into the circulation when a large number of cells die at once; the resulting disturbance threatens
the kidney and the heart, and renal impairment then worsens the clearance of everything that
caused it (**mechanism**).

Three things follow from the mechanism and not from any guideline.

**It is a complication of success.** It is most likely where the tumour is bulky, dividing fast,
and highly sensitive to the treatment about to be given, which are the same features that predict
the treatment will work (**mechanism**). It can also occur before treatment in a very rapidly
proliferating disease.

**It is predictable before anything is given.** The risk is estimated from the disease, its bulk,
and the person's renal function at baseline — all available in advance (**consensus**). So risk
stratification happens before the first dose, and the risk category is disease-specific rather
than generic.

**It is therefore a prevention problem.** The response is anticipatory: attention to circulating
volume and urine output, urate-lowering treatment chosen by risk category, biochemical monitoring
at an interval set by that category, and the resources to manage a deterioration already arranged
(**consensus**, with agents, thresholds and monitoring intervals (**country-dependent**)). A
syndrome that is diagnosed and then treated has, in this one case, usually already represented a
failure of anticipation.

## Why the classification is the point

Put the four together and the common feature is sharp: in none of them is the dangerous quantity
the same quantity as *how advanced is the cancer*. That is why each has to be recognised as
itself.

The practical payoff is that each shape names its own response (**mechanism**):

* a **window** means the clock is the thing being managed, imaging and treatment are not sequenced
  behind reassurance, and the baseline neurological state is recorded because it is the quantity
  that matters;
* a **missing readout** means a normal examination does not change the plan, and treatment starts
  before the information arrives;
* a **remote driver** means the first act is aimed at the mechanism rather than at the tumour, and
  a small cancer does not argue against a large effect;
* a **treatment-caused, predictable** hazard means the work is done before the first dose, and the
  question is asked of the disease rather than of the patient's appearance.

And one honest limit: real situations are mixtures. Someone can have more than one of these at
once, and several of them share presenting features with ordinary complications — which is the
reason the classification is worth learning rather than the reason to doubt it.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is about the structure of a hazard, which is the right register for learning to
recognise one. It is not the register for being in one.

The person in each of these situations is usually frightened and usually unwell, frequently has
been unwell for a while, and has often already been told something about their cancer that they
are still absorbing. Several of these presentations arrive through a route that was not designed
for them — a call to a helpline, a general emergency department, a routine appointment brought
forward — and the people they meet first may not know the oncological history. That is a system
problem and not a personal failing, and it is why acute oncology services exist where they exist.

Two things follow that matter more than any of the mechanism above. The first is that the history
is often the whole diagnosis, and getting it requires asking rather than looking. Back pain that
has changed in character, weakness that the person has been attributing to tiredness, a rate of
change over days: none of that is on an observation chart. The second is that someone who has been
told to call if something changes needs the call to be answered by a route that can act, or the
instruction was decoration.

Nothing in this answer says anything about what will happen to any individual, and that omission
is deliberate. Nor is anything here a protocol. The reasoning is here so that the response written
down where you work makes sense; the response written down where you work is the one to follow.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about why a handful of complications are emergencies by mechanism,
written for someone already training in or qualified for the field. It has had no clinical review.
**It is not a protocol and must not be used as one**, and no dose, agent choice, threshold,
interval or monitoring schedule appears here, deliberately — those are set locally and nationally,
they differ between countries and between institutions in the same country, and they are revised;
the policy in force where you work is the authority and this is not. It says nothing about what
will happen to any individual. Anyone who is unwell now should use their local emergency number or
their acute oncology contact route rather than reading this, and anyone affected by cancer — their
own diagnosis or someone else's — should be talking to the clinical team looking after that
person, who have the history and the examination, neither of which is here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument will not date: a window, a missing readout, a driver remote from the bulk
and a hazard generated by successful treatment are four different shapes, and the response each
one implies follows from the shape rather than from the current guidance.

What moves is everything operational. Which service holds the pathway, what imaging standard
applies and in what interval, which empirical antibacterial regimen is used, which urate-lowering
agent is chosen for which risk category, and whether a histological diagnosis is obtained before
treating venous obstruction in a given centre are all set locally and nationally and are being
revised. Risk stratification for the syndrome following rapid tumour lysis has been extended as
newer agents with rapid cell kill have entered practice, and that is still moving. The place of
endovascular stenting in venous obstruction has grown and varies substantially between services.
No threshold, agent or interval is quoted here, deliberately: those belong to the current guidance
and the local policy rather than to a revision note.
