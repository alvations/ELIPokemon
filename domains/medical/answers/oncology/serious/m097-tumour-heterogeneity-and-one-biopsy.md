# One place, one moment, and a population that the next treatment will reshape

A cancer is not a clone. It is a **population** of related cells that have diverged from a common
ancestor, are still diverging, and are under selection — by oxygen, by space, by nutrient supply,
by the immune system and, once treatment starts, by the treatment. A biopsy takes a fragment of
that population from one place at one time.

Three separate consequences follow, and they are usually run together.

* A biomarker result can be a **sampling** result. Finding an alteration establishes that it is
  present in what was examined. Not finding it establishes much less than it is read as
  establishing.
* Variation is **structured**, not noise. It has a spatial shape within a lesion, a different
  shape between lesions, and a historical shape — some alterations are in every cell because they
  were in the ancestor, and others are in a branch.
* Treatment is a **filter**. The population after it is not the population before it, and nothing
  about any individual cell had to change for that to be true.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: **mechanism** (derivable
from how the biology or the sampling works), (**definitional**) (true because a classification
says so), (**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**)
(varies by nation, region or institution, and changes).

## Where the structure comes from

Three processes acting together, none of them exotic (**mechanism**).

**Mutation and genomic instability** generate variation continuously. **Selection** acts on it,
and the selective pressures inside a tumour are local: a cell at the hypoxic centre of a nodule is
under different pressure from one at a well-perfused edge, so the two regions are not sampling the
same fitness landscape. **Drift** does the rest, because expanding populations are founded by
small numbers of cells and a founder carries whatever it happened to have.

The result is a **branching** structure rather than a linear sequence of steps, and the branching
has a clinically load-bearing consequence. Alterations present in the common ancestor are in every
cell of the disease; alterations that arose in a branch are in a subset (**definitional**). The
distinction matters because an agent directed at a branch alteration treats part of the disease by
construction, however well the test performed.

Two further features are worth naming. **Convergent evolution** — different regions arriving at
the same pathway through different alterations — means two samples can look genetically unalike
and be functionally alike (**consensus**). And a **metastasis descends from a region**, not from
the average of the primary, which is why discordance between a primary and a metastasis is
expected rather than surprising, with the extent of it disease-specific (**consensus**).

## What one sample can and cannot establish

The asymmetry is the whole of it (**mechanism**):

* **Finding** an alteration establishes that it was present in the material examined, at a level
  the assay could detect.
* **Not finding** one is compatible with absence, with presence below the assay's limit of
  detection, with presence in a region the needle missed, and with a specimen that was inadequate
  for the test.

So a negative biomarker result is only interpretable alongside **what was tested, how much of it
there was, and what the assay can see** — which is why specimen adequacy is part of a molecular
report rather than a preamble to it (**consensus**, with the reporting requirements
**country-dependent**). The limit-of-detection half of that argument is the subject of this
specialty's answer on tumour markers and is not re-derived here; the surface-sampling half is in
the answer on surgical margins.

Repeat sampling improves the evidence and has a cost, which is why multi-region sampling is a
research instrument rather than routine practice, and why the question asked of any repeat biopsy
is whether the result would change management (**consensus**).

## Treatment as a filter

This is the part most often stated as though a drug were mutagenic.

The ordinary account needs only selection. A resistant subpopulation existing at low frequency
before treatment, an agent that removes the sensitive majority, and an expansion of what was left
produces exactly the clinical picture — a deep response followed by progression — with no new
mutation required (**mechanism**). Treatment-induced mutagenesis is a separate claim with separate
and much weaker support, and asserting it where selection suffices is a real error of reasoning.

Three practical consequences (**consensus**):

* **Progression is informative about composition.** What regrew is enriched for whatever survived,
  which is why the mechanism of escape at progression is worth establishing rather than assuming —
  the subject of the previous answer in this specialty.
* **Simultaneous beats sequential**, where the evidence supports it, because a sequence hands the
  surviving subpopulation an uncontested interval to expand in.
* **A pre-treatment result can expire.** A molecular profile obtained before several lines of
  treatment describes a population that has since been filtered, which is the argument for
  re-testing at progression where a current result would change the decision.

## Two instruments, two different blind spots

Tissue and plasma are not ranked, and treating one as a cheaper version of the other is the common
mistake (**mechanism**, **consensus**).

A **tissue biopsy** is spatially specific: it comes from a named lesion, it supports morphological
and immunohistochemical assessment as well as sequencing, and it can therefore establish a change
of histology. Its weakness is that it is one place.

**Circulating tumour DNA** inverts both. It pools shed material from wherever is shedding, so it
can carry alterations no single biopsy would have reached — and it tells you nothing about which
lesion they came from. It depends on shedding, so a low-shedding or low-volume disease can give a
negative result while disease is present. It cannot see morphology at all. And plasma variants are
not all tumour variants: clonal haematopoiesis contributes alterations from blood cells, which is
why plasma results are interpreted with that possibility in mind rather than at face value
(**consensus**).

The honest summary: a negative plasma result is weak evidence of absence, a positive tissue result
is strong evidence of presence somewhere, and neither instrument makes the other redundant. Which
is available, which is funded and which is accepted for a given decision differ markedly between
countries and institutions (**country-dependent**).

## Which lesion, and when

Two questions that follow directly, and are frequently asked badly.

**Which lesion to sample** is decided by what the question is. For establishing a diagnosis, the
most accessible adequate lesion. For a treatment decision at progression, the lesion that is
progressing is usually the informative one, because it is the part of the population that has
escaped (**consensus**). Sampling a controlled site at progression can be an accessible way of
answering a question nobody asked.

**When** is governed by the same test as everything else here: the result has to be capable of
changing what is done. A molecular result that cannot alter management, in a disease where no
matched option exists or is reachable, is a procedure with risk and no decision attached
(**consensus**, strongly (**country-dependent**), since what is reachable is a matter of local
availability).

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is population biology and sampling, which is the right register for
understanding a molecular report. It is not the register for being the person the needle goes
into.

A biopsy is an invasive procedure with a wait before it, a wait after it, and a result that may
not be conclusive. Being asked for another one, having had bad news, is a reasonable thing to
decline and a reasonable thing to accept, and the test that justifies the request is whether the
answer would change what is offered — not whether the information would be interesting. That
reasoning should be shared out loud with the person being asked, because it is the reasoning that
makes the request legitimate.

It is also worth being plain that "the result might be a sampling artefact" is a hard sentence to
hear. People are told a test was negative and reasonably take that as a fact about themselves
rather than a fact about a fragment of tissue. The uncertainty described on this page is real, it
is not a hedge, and conveying it without turning a result into a riddle is a skill rather than a
disclaimer. None of the reasoning here determines what will happen to any individual, and no
figure of any kind appears in this answer for that reason.

## What an examiner digs into next

* A biomarker test is negative. List everything that result is compatible with, in order of how
  often it is the explanation.
* Why is an alteration present in every cell a different kind of target from one present in a
  branch?
* Why is discordance between a primary and a metastasis expected rather than an error?
* A deep response is followed by progression. Explain it using selection only, and say what would
  be needed to justify adding mutagenesis to the account.
* Give two things circulating tumour DNA can establish that one biopsy cannot, and two it cannot
  establish at all.
* Why is clonal haematopoiesis a problem specifically for plasma assays?
* At progression, which lesion should be sampled, and what would make the answer different?

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about what a tumour sample is a sample of, written for someone already
training in the field. It has had no clinical review. **No discordance rate, detection limit,
allele fraction or sampling figure appears here, and none should be inferred** — those are
assay-specific and disease-specific, they differ between laboratories and countries, and they are
revised; the reporting standard and local protocol in force where you work are the authority, and
this is not. It is not a reporting guide, not a testing protocol and not a decision aid, and it
describes no individual's specimen or situation. Anyone affected by cancer — their own diagnosis
or someone else's — should be talking to the clinical team looking after that person, who have the
specimen, the report and the history, none of which are here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The reasoning is structural and will not date. A tumour has always been a population, a biopsy has
always been one sample of it, and the asymmetry between finding something and not finding it
follows from sampling rather than from any current technology.

What moves is everything technological and everything administrative. Assay sensitivity, which
genes are on a panel, whether plasma testing is accepted in place of tissue and for which
decisions, whether multi-region or serial sampling has any routine role, and how a negative result
must be worded in a report are all actively changing and unevenly adopted between countries and
between laboratories in the same country. Clonal haematopoiesis as a confounder is understood as a
mechanism and quantified only study by study. No assay, panel, gene or figure is named here,
deliberately: those belong to the current reporting standard and the current guidance rather than
to a revision note like this one.
