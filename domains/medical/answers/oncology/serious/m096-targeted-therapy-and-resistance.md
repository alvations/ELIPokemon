# A working drug selects for the cells it cannot kill, and the route of escape names the next move

A targeted agent is given because the tumour carries a specific molecular lesion the agent acts
on. Where the lesion is a genuine dependency, the response can be fast and deep. In advanced
disease it almost always ends.

The ending is the part worth understanding, because the usual explanations are wrong. The drug has
not worn off. The dose has not become incorrect. What has changed is the **population being
treated**, and the treatment is part of what changed it. So the useful question at progression is
not *whether* the drug stopped working but *how* — because the escape routes fall into a small
number of classes, and each class implies a different next step. One class is answered by another
drug against the same target. One is answered only by blocking two things at once. One is answered
by abandoning the target altogether. One is not a biology problem at all.

No gene, drug or disease is named in this answer, deliberately. The classes are the teachable
content; the examples date within a year or two, and naming them here would invite a reader to
treat a revision note as a current list.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: **mechanism** (derivable
from how the biology works), **definitional** (true because a classification says so),
**consensus** (widely agreed professional practice), or **country-dependent** (varies by nation,
region or institution, and changes).

## What "targeted" commits you to

A targeted agent's effect is **contingent**, and contingent in a strong sense (**mechanism**). It
is not a treatment that works better when the lesion is present and less well when it is absent.
Where the dependency is absent there is no mechanism for it to act through, so no amount of dose,
duration or repetition substitutes. This is the same point the pharmacology answers in this
repository make about an intervention having no route to its target rather than an insufficient
one, and it is the reason biomarker-negative populations are not simply a lower-response subgroup.

Two consequences follow immediately. First, the agent's whole value rests on a test, which is why
the test and the drug are treated as one object — the subject of its own answer in this specialty.
Second, anything that removes the dependency removes the effect entirely rather than blunting it,
which is why three of the four escape classes below are not dose problems.

## Class one: the target changes

The commonest and best-understood route. Under treatment, cells carrying an additional alteration
**in the target itself** are selected — classically one that preserves the target's function while
preventing the drug from binding, and sometimes instead an increase in the amount of target
present (**consensus**).

This is the class with the most actionable answer, because the problem is chemical. A molecule
that binds the altered target, or binds at a different site on it, restores the block; successive
generations of inhibitors against a single target are the direct product of this logic
(**consensus**). It is also the class most likely to be detectable by sequencing rather than
morphology, which is why it is the one a plasma assay can often find.

The same class has a predictable sequel: a drug designed against the second alteration selects for
a third. Each generation narrows the escape space rather than closing it (**mechanism**).

## Class two: the signal arrives by another way

The pathway the drug blocks is rarely the only route to the same output. Escape here is
**activation of a parallel input** — another receptor, or a node downstream of the block — leaving
the drug's target inhibited and irrelevant (**consensus**).

The logical answer is to block both nodes. The practical constraint is not logic but tolerance:
two agents acting on pathways that are also required by normal tissue produce overlapping toxicity
at the dose each needs, and the combination is limited by whichever organ both agents burden
(**mechanism**). This is why combination development is slow even where the biology is obvious,
and why the useful question about a combination is which toxicities it stacks.

It also argues for giving a combination **up front rather than sequentially** where the evidence
supports it: a sequence allows the resistant subpopulation to expand between treatments, while
simultaneous blockade denies it the single step it needed (**mechanism**, with the actual choice
disease-specific and **consensus**).

## Class three: the cell stops being the kind of cell the drug was chosen for

The least intuitive route and the one most often missed. The tumour changes **lineage or
phenotype** — a shift in histology, or a reversible shift in state that carries no new mutation —
and the dependency the drug exploited disappears with it (**consensus**).

Two things follow, and both are practical. The diagnosis is **morphological**, so a plasma assay
can miss it entirely and a tissue biopsy is what establishes it. And the answer is not another
inhibitor of anything: it is a different class of treatment, usually cytotoxic, chosen for the new
histology rather than for the original target (**consensus**).

Worth separating within this class: a non-genetic, reversible tolerant state is a different thing
from a completed histological transformation, and only the first is expected to reverse when the
pressure is removed (**mechanism**). Conflating them is the commonest error here.

## Class four: the drug never got there

Not resistance at all, and the distinction matters. Viable disease in a compartment the agent
penetrates poorly will progress while disease everywhere else stays controlled
(**mechanism**).

The clue is the **pattern** rather than the fact of progression: one site, or one compartment,
advancing while the rest does not. The answer is a different instrument — local treatment to the
progressing site, or an agent whose penetration into that compartment is a property of the
molecule rather than something a higher dose achieves (**consensus**, **country-dependent** in
what is available).

This class is also why **progression is not one event**. Progression confined to one or a few
sites, with everything else controlled, is treated differently from widespread progression in
several diseases: continuing the systemic agent and treating the progressing site locally is an
established option, and continuing an agent beyond radiological progression has disease-specific
evidence behind it rather than being a general rule (**consensus**, strongly
**country-dependent**).

## Why the classification is the point

Progression is an instruction to find out which class this is, because the four answers are
different and three of them cannot be reached by adjusting the dose.

That is the argument for **repeat tissue sampling at progression where the result would change
management**, and for plasma circulating tumour DNA as a complementary route with a different
blind spot: it samples shed material from many sites at once and cannot see a morphological
change, so a negative plasma result at progression is a reason to consider tissue rather than a
reason to stop asking (**consensus**). The population being sampled, and why one biopsy is a
sample of a population under selection, is the subject of the next answer in this specialty.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is mechanism, and mechanism is the right register for understanding why a drug
stops working. It is not the register for being told that it has.

A deep response to a targeted agent often restores ordinary life quickly and completely enough
that the disease recedes from the foreground. Being told that the scan has changed is therefore
not a gradual disappointment; it is an interruption of something that had started to feel settled,
and it frequently arrives at a routine appointment with no new symptom to explain it. The
mechanistic account on this page — that the population changed, that this was expected, that there
is a next class of answer — is genuinely useful to a clinician and is not comfort.

Repeat biopsy deserves naming too. It is another procedure, with its own risk and its own wait,
offered to someone who has just had bad news, and it is justified by whether the result would
change what is done rather than by completeness. Whether it is the right thing for a particular
person is a conversation, not a protocol, and nothing on this page settles it.

## What an examiner digs into next

* A targeted agent fails after a long response. Name the four classes of explanation and say which
  one a plasma assay is least able to detect.
* Why does a second-generation inhibitor help in one class and not in the other three?
* Why is a combination given simultaneously rather than sequentially, and what limits that?
* Distinguish a reversible tolerant state from a completed lineage change, and say why it matters.
* One site progresses while the rest of the disease is controlled. What does that pattern suggest,
  and what does it not establish?
* Why is "no mechanism against this target" a different statement from "less effective against
  this target"?
* What would make a repeat biopsy at progression not worth doing?

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about why targeted agents fail, written for someone already training in
the field. It has had no clinical review. **No drug, gene, disease, dose, response rate or
interval appears here, and none should be inferred** — the licensed indications, the tests that
define them and the options at progression differ between countries and institutions and are
revised frequently; the label and the local pathway in force where you work are the authority, and
this is not. It is not a prescribing guide and not a decision aid, and it describes no
individual's situation. Anyone affected by cancer — their own diagnosis or someone else's — should
be talking to the clinical team looking after that person, who have the imaging, the molecular
results and the history, none of which are here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The classification is structural and should age well: a contingent drug, a population under
selection, and four ways for the contingency to fail are not facts about any particular era's
drugs. The division between on-target alteration, bypass activation, lineage change and
inadequate penetration has been stable for some time and is how the subject is taught.

Everything attached to it moves. Which escape mechanisms are known in which disease, which of them
have a drug designed against them, whether a combination is given up front, whether plasma
sequencing is accepted in place of tissue, and whether continuing an agent beyond progression is
supported are all disease-specific and are revised on a short cycle, with uneven adoption between
countries. No agent, target or figure is quoted here for exactly that reason: those belong to the
current label and the current guidance rather than to a revision note like this one.
