# Screening is an intervention offered to well people, and it is judged accordingly

A screening programme does not treat anybody. It takes a population of people who feel fine,
applies a test, and changes what happens to some of them. That framing settles what counts as
success: **whether fewer people die, not whether more cancer is found.** Three biases and one
genuine harm all follow from taking the second as evidence for the first, and all three biases
will inflate a screening programme's apparent benefit even if the programme does nothing
whatsoever.

Claims are marked by kind: **mechanism** (derivable from how the measurement or the biology
works), (**definitional**, **consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice),
(**country-dependent**) (varies by nation, region or institution, and changes).

## Lead-time bias: arithmetic, not biology

Survival is conventionally measured from the date of diagnosis. Move the date of diagnosis earlier
and the measured survival gets longer — **even if the date of death is completely unchanged**
(**mechanism**). Nothing about the person or the disease has altered; the clock was started
sooner.

This is why survival measured from diagnosis is close to useless for evaluating a screening
programme, and why programmes are instead judged on mortality in a whole invited population over a
defined period, comparing invited against not invited. The denominator has to be the population,
not the diagnosed, or the bias is built into the design.

## Length bias: the test does not sample the disease

A periodic test can only detect a case while that case is in its detectable-but-asymptomatic
window. The longer the window, the more rounds of screening the case is available to. So slowly
progressing disease is **systematically over-represented** among screen-detected cases, and
rapidly progressing disease is systematically under-represented and tends instead to present
symptomatically between rounds (**mechanism**).

Two consequences, and the second is the one people miss:

* **The screen-detected population is biologically more indolent** than the population that
  presents clinically. Comparing their outcomes therefore compares two different populations, and
  the screen-detected group will look better even if screening is useless.
* **Interval cancers are not primarily a quality failure.** Some are missed lesions and a
  programme should audit for those, but a proportion are a mechanical consequence of sampling
  periodically: fast disease can arise and present entirely between two rounds. A programme with
  no interval cancers would be a programme with an implausibly short interval.

## Overdiagnosis: the limiting case, and not a false positive

**Overdiagnosis is the detection of disease that meets the diagnostic criteria but would never
have caused symptoms or death during that person's life** (**definitional**) — because it does not
progress, because it regresses, or because the person dies of something else first.

The distinction from a false positive is the whole point and is routinely got wrong. A false
positive is a test result that is wrong, resolved by further testing. **An overdiagnosed cancer is
a correct result.** The pathology is real, the criteria are met, a second opinion confirms it, and
the diagnosis cannot be taken back. What is wrong is not the finding but the inference that the
finding needed to be made.

Which means the harm is not reassurance that comes a bit late. It is:

* **Treatment, and all of its consequences, for a condition that was never going to cause harm** —
  surgery, systemic therapy, radiotherapy, and the long-term effects of each.
* **A diagnosis that does not go away**, with its effects on how a person sees themselves, on
  insurance and employment in some systems, and on every subsequent medical encounter.
* **Surveillance**, with its own procedures and its own findings.
* **And the arithmetic is irreducibly impersonal**: overdiagnosis is measurable in populations
  and, for any individual, unknowable. Nobody can be told that they personally were overdiagnosed,
  because for them the course the disease would have taken is not observable. That is not a gap in
  current knowledge; it is a property of what is being claimed.

## Why it is hard to measure, and how it is inferred

You cannot identify overdiagnosed individuals, so it is inferred at population level
(**mechanism**, **consensus**). The signature is an increase in recorded incidence that follows
the introduction or intensification of testing, **without** a corresponding fall in the incidence
of advanced disease and without a fall in mortality. If a test were genuinely moving cases
earlier, late-stage incidence should fall as early-stage incidence rises. When early-stage
incidence rises and late-stage incidence does not fall, the extra cases are additional rather than
earlier.

Two instances are standard teaching examples (**consensus**, and these are the claims in this
answer a reviewer should check first, because they are empirical and specific):

* Screening infants for one particular childhood tumour was introduced in several health systems.
  Recorded incidence rose substantially, deaths from that tumour did not fall, and the programmes
  were discontinued.
* Where neck ultrasound came into very widespread use, recorded thyroid cancer incidence rose
  steeply while mortality remained flat.

In other settings the magnitude of overdiagnosis is **genuinely contested** rather than unknown —
breast screening and prostate-specific antigen testing are the two long-running arguments, and the
disagreement is about how much, not whether. Anyone who presents either as settled is quoting one
side.

## And the other harms, which are not overdiagnosis

Worth separating, because they are different mechanisms (**consensus**):

* **False positives and the cascade** that follows: further imaging, biopsy, and the anxiety that
  attends all of it, in people who turn out not to have the disease.
* **Procedural harm** from the test itself and from the investigations it triggers.
* **Radiation exposure**, where the modality involves it, which is small per examination and not
  zero across a screened population.
* **Inequity.** A programme with socially skewed uptake can widen outcome gaps, because the
  benefit accrues to those who attend while the disease burden sits with those who do not
  (**country-dependent** in extent).
* **Opportunity cost.** Resources are finite and a screening programme is competing with
  everything else.

## The kind of screening that is mechanistically different

This is the best distinction available here, and it is often lost (**mechanism**).

Most screening finds invasive disease earlier. Its benefit therefore depends entirely on earlier
treatment working better, and it is fully exposed to every bias above.

But some programmes find and remove **precursor lesions**, and those change the incidence of
invasive cancer rather than only its timing. Cervical and colorectal programmes are built on this,
and it is a different mechanism with a different signature: if it works, invasive incidence itself
falls. That is a far stronger kind of evidence than a shift in stage distribution, because a fall
in incidence is not producible by any of the three biases in this answer.

So **the right first question about a screening programme is which of the two it is**, before any
question about its performance.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is population reasoning, and it is the correct kind of reasoning for evaluating a
programme. It does not tell any particular person what to do, and it cannot.

Overdiagnosis first, because it is the harm most easily left as an abstraction and should not be.
It means a person who felt well was told they had cancer, was treated for cancer, and carried
everything that follows — surgery and its consequences, systemic therapy or radiotherapy and
theirs, surveillance, and a diagnosis that does not go away and that travels with them through
every later medical encounter and, in some systems, into insurance and employment — when the
disease was never going to trouble them. That is a serious harm done by a well-meant system to
somebody who was fine, and it is the reason a programme is obliged to publish its harms beside its
benefits rather than only its benefits.

For one person deciding whether to take up an invitation, the relevant facts are their own risk,
the specific test, what the programme offers where they live, and their own view of the trade
between a possible earlier diagnosis and a possible unnecessary one. That is a conversation, and
the useful form of it presents the harms alongside the benefits rather than only the benefits. A
population estimate of benefit or of overdiagnosis is an average over a large and varied group
assembled in the past; it does not say what will happen to anybody, because any individual differs
from that average in the ways that actually determine their course. Which is why no figure of that
kind appears in this answer.

And for anyone whose cancer was found by screening: none of the above says anything about their
diagnosis. Overdiagnosis is a statement about a population's excess, never about an identified
person's disease, and it cannot be applied to one.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about the epidemiology of screening, pitched at someone already training
in the field. It has had no clinical review. No screening interval, age range, test performance
figure or eligibility rule appears here, and none should be inferred; screening programmes differ
substantially between countries and regions and they change, and your national programme's own
published information is the authority rather than this. Nothing here describes any individual's
situation, and nothing here is a reason for anybody to accept or decline a screening invitation —
that is a conversation with a clinician who knows the person's circumstances. Anyone affected by
cancer, their own or someone else's, should be talking to the team looking after that person. In
particular, nothing in this answer can be applied to any individual's diagnosis: overdiagnosis is
a population-level excess and is not identifiable in a person.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The three biases and the definition of overdiagnosis are long-standing, formally defined, and not
in dispute; the distinction between finding invasive disease earlier and removing precursor
lesions is likewise stable and is the durable core of this answer. What is unsettled, and actively
argued, is the *magnitude* of overdiagnosis in particular programmes — breast and prostate above
all — and that argument has not converged. What changes fastest is programme design: eligibility,
modality, interval and whether a programme exists at all vary by country and are revised
regularly, often in response to exactly the reasoning above. No interval, age, test characteristic
or survival figure is quoted here, deliberately, and the two historical examples are named by
condition rather than by place or number because they are the claims here that most need checking
against a registry rather than against a revision note like this one.
