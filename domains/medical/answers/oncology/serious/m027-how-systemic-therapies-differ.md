# Four mechanisms, and four toxicity profiles that are consequences of them

The four big classes of systemic anticancer therapy are not four flavours of the same thing. They
differ in what they act on, in what makes them selective at all, in what has to be true before
they can work, and in how long they take. **And the side effects are not a separate list to
memorise — in each class they fall out of the mechanism.** If you can state the mechanism you can
predict most of the toxicity profile, and the places where that prediction fails are themselves
informative.

Claims below are marked by kind: **mechanism** (derivable from how the biology works),
**definitional**, **consensus** (widely agreed professional practice), **country-dependent**
(varies by nation, region or institution, and changes).

## Cytotoxic chemotherapy

**Mechanism.** These agents interfere with making and separating DNA. Alkylating agents and the
platinum compounds form covalent adducts and crosslinks on DNA; antimetabolites masquerade as
nucleotides or block the enzymes that make them; topoisomerase inhibitors trap the enzyme on the
DNA it was supposed to religate; the microtubule agents, which either stabilise or destabilise the
spindle, arrest mitosis itself. Different lesions, one common consequence: a cell attempting
replication or mitosis under that insult is the cell most likely to die.

**Therefore the selectivity is kinetic, not molecular** (**mechanism**). Nothing about an
alkylating agent recognises a cancer cell. What it exploits is that the tumour population contains
a higher proportion of cells attempting division at any moment, and that its repair and checkpoint
responses are often defective. That is a relative advantage over normal tissue, not an absolute
one — which is why the toxicity profile is simply a list of the normal compartments with high
turnover, and why it is **predictable rather than idiosyncratic**.

**Therefore the dosing is in cycles** (**mechanism**). The gap between cycles exists because
normal proliferating compartments recover on a known schedule and the tumour, if the therapy is
working, recovers less completely. Cycling is not a convenience; it is the whole basis of the
therapeutic window for this class.

**And a second, separate kind of toxicity.** Several cytotoxics carry organ-specific damage that
proliferation does not explain at all — cardiac, renal, auditory, peripheral nerve and pulmonary
effects, depending on agent — and some of these relate to the *total lifetime amount received*
rather than to any single cycle (**consensus**). Those are tracked cumulatively, because unlike
myelosuppression they do not recover between cycles. The distinction between a
dose-intensity-limited toxicity and a cumulative-dose-limited one is the most useful single idea
in this paragraph.

**Resistance** arises through efflux, altered drug activation or inactivation, enhanced DNA
repair, defective apoptotic signalling, and — bluntly — cells that were not dividing while the
drug was present (**mechanism**).

## Targeted therapy

**Mechanism.** A targeted agent inhibits a specific molecule on which the malignant cell has
become dependent. Small-molecule inhibitors occupy a binding site on an intracellular kinase;
monoclonal antibodies bind a receptor or its ligand at the cell surface; some agents exploit
synthetic lethality, in which inhibiting one repair pathway is tolerable for a normal cell and
lethal for a cell that has already lost another.

**Therefore the drug does nothing useful if the target is absent** (**mechanism**). This is the
defining practical consequence and the reason predictive biomarker testing is a prerequisite
rather than a refinement (**consensus**): without the dependency, the agent still has its toxicity
and has lost its only route to benefit. A targeted agent given without the target is pure harm,
and that is a mechanistic statement, not a rhetorical one.

**Therefore the toxicity maps onto normal expression of the same target** (**mechanism**). Inhibit
a receptor that normal skin and gut epithelium also use, and you get rash and diarrhoea. Interfere
with the pathway that maintains vascular tone and endothelial integrity, and you get hypertension,
proteinuria and impaired wound healing. Interfere with a receptor the myocardium uses, and cardiac
function is the thing to monitor. None of that is a coincidence, and a student who knows where the
target normally lives can derive most of the list.

**Resistance** is usually a change at or around the target: a secondary mutation in the binding
site, amplification of the target, activation of a parallel pathway that restores the signal, or a
change in the tumour's phenotype so the dependency no longer holds (**consensus**).

## Endocrine therapy

**Mechanism.** For diseases driven through a hormone receptor, the strategy is to interrupt the
signal rather than to poison the cell: block the receptor, degrade it, or reduce the supply of its
ligand by suppressing the gland that makes it or the enzyme that synthesises it. Combinations of
those approaches are used depending on the disease and the physiological setting (**consensus**,
**country-dependent** in regimen choice).

**Therefore the effect is largely cytostatic, and slower** (**mechanism**). The cell is not killed
outright; it is deprived of a growth signal. Clinically that means the effect accrues over longer
periods than cytotoxic therapy, and that treatment in the post-operative setting runs for years
rather than weeks.

**Therefore the toxicity is the physiology of the withdrawn hormone** (**mechanism**). The hormone
being blocked acts systemically, so taking it away is felt systemically: vasomotor symptoms, bone
density loss, musculoskeletal and joint symptoms, effects on sexual function, and metabolic and
cardiovascular effects. Receptor modulators add their own pattern, because a modulator can
antagonise the receptor in one tissue and partially agonise it in another, which is why some have
tissue-specific effects that a pure antagonist does not (**mechanism**).

**And therefore adherence is a first-order determinant of benefit**, in a way it is not for an
infused agent given in a day unit (**consensus**). A drug taken daily for years is only as good as
its continuation, and the symptoms above are the reason continuation is hard.

## Immunotherapy

**Mechanism.** Checkpoint inhibitors are antibodies that block inhibitory signalling on T cells.
They are not cytotoxic. They remove a restraint, and the immune system — a pre-existing or
re-primed T-cell response — does the work. Engineered cell therapies and T-cell-engaging
antibodies take a different route to the same place: they redirect T cells onto a surface antigen.

**Therefore the response kinetics are different** (**mechanism**). Onset can be delayed, and a
response can persist after the drug has stopped, because the effector is a self-sustaining
biological process rather than a drug concentration.

**Therefore the toxicity is autoimmunity, because that is literally what it is** (**mechanism**).
The checkpoints being blocked exist to restrain self-reactive T cells. Release them systemically
and any organ is in scope: colon, liver, lung, thyroid and pituitary and other endocrine organs,
skin, joints, myocardium, peripheral and central nervous system. Three consequences follow from
the mechanism rather than from observation:

* **It is not dose-proportional** in the way cytotoxic toxicity is, because the drug is not the
  thing doing the damage.
* **The latency is variable**, and an event can begin after treatment has finished.
* **Some of it does not resolve when the drug stops** — endocrine organs in particular can be
  permanently affected, needing lifelong replacement (**consensus**).

**Therefore the management is different in kind** (**mechanism**, **country-dependent** in
protocol). The response to an immune-related adverse event is immunosuppression, with permanent
discontinuation in some cases — not the dose reduction that answers a cytotoxic toxicity. Reducing
the dose of a drug that is not doing the damage does not address the problem. Cell therapies and
T-cell engagers add two further mechanism-derived syndromes, from cytokine release and from
neurological effects, each with its own graded management pathway.

## Where the four categories leak

They are a teaching scheme, not a taxonomy of nature (**consensus**).

* **Antibody–drug conjugates** are a targeted delivery system carrying a cytotoxic payload. Their
  toxicity has a target-dependent component and a payload-dependent component, and the payload
  component looks like chemotherapy because it is chemotherapy.
* **Some agents belong to two boxes at once** — an antibody can engage the immune system as part
  of how it works, which blurs targeted and immune mechanisms.
* **The word chemotherapy** is used colloquially for all systemic anticancer treatment, which is
  worth knowing when a conversation seems to be at cross purposes.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Mechanism predicts the *shape* of a toxicity profile. It does not predict what a given person will
experience, in what combination, or how much it will matter to them — and the choice between
classes for one person depends on the disease's biology, on what is available where they are, on
everything else going on in their body, and on what they want. That is a different kind of
reasoning from this answer, carried out by people with the information, and this is not a
substitute for it.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about mechanism, pitched at someone already training in the field. It
has had no clinical review. No dose, regimen, schedule or monitoring interval appears here, and
none should be inferred; the formulary and the local protocol are the authority, and this is not.
Regimen choice, availability and toxicity-management pathways differ by country, region and
institution, and they change. Nothing here describes any individual's situation. Anyone affected
by cancer — their own diagnosis or someone else's — should be talking to the clinical team looking
after that person, who know the disease, the treatment and the history, none of which are here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The four mechanisms, and the derivation of each toxicity profile from its mechanism, are
long-standing and stable; this is the part of the answer that will still be true in ten years. The
contents of each box are not stable: new targets, new conjugate payloads and new cell therapies
arrive continuously, and what is available and funded differs sharply by country. No agent is
named here, no trial is cited, and no number appears, deliberately — those are exactly the facts
that date, and they belong to the prescribing information and the local protocol rather than to a
revision note like this one.
