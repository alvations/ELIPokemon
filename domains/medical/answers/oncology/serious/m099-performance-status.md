# A handful of categories, assigned by eye, carrying more than the measurements it ignores

**Performance status** is a short ordered scale describing what a person can currently do: it runs
from fully active, through restriction of effort, through being up and self-caring but not
working, to dependence for personal care. It is **assigned by an observer**, in seconds, from a
conversation.

It is crude by construction. Two scales are in common use — a short ordinal scale associated with
the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group and the World Health Organization, and the finer-grained
Karnofsky scale — and they are not interchangeable; mappings between them are approximations
(**definitional**, **consensus**).

And it is, nonetheless, one of the most informative single items in the whole assessment of
someone being considered for systemic anticancer treatment, and an eligibility criterion in most
trials. The reasons it works are the reasons it is crude. The ways it misleads are consequences of
the same design. Both halves are the answer.

One thing this answer deliberately does not do: it says nothing about any individual's outlook.
Performance status appears as an input to group-level models, and a group-level model does not
contain the information needed to say what will happen to a person. That boundary is this domain's
and it is kept.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: **mechanism** (derivable
from how the measurement works), (**definitional**) (true because a classification says so),
(**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**) (varies by
nation, region or institution, and changes).

## Why a coarse scale carries more than a precise one

Three mechanisms, and they compound (**mechanism**).

**It integrates.** No single precise measurement captures what the scale captures. A haemoglobin,
an albumin, a tumour measurement on a scan and a walking test each describe one thing; what the
scale describes is the net effect of everything on what this person can actually do. A composite
built from one human judgement over many inputs is doing the same job that the nursing domain's
answer on early warning scores describes a composite doing, and for the same reason.

**It is reproducible enough.** Agreement between observers on a coarse ordinal is moderate rather
than excellent, and it is better than agreement on a finer-grained alternative (**consensus**).
The information a reading carries is bounded by its reproducibility, so extra levels that nobody
assigns the same way add resolution and subtract information. This is the same argument this
specialty's answer on staging makes about why a staging system uses discrete categories for a
continuous extent: a classification used by many people is optimised for agreement, not for
fidelity.

**It is always available.** A measurement that requires equipment, a technician or a wait is not
available at the moment the decision is made. One assigned from the consultation always is, which
is why it is the item that actually gets used (**mechanism**).

And it was built against the outcome it is used for. It predicts **tolerance of treatment**, which
is a different quantity from a measurement of function — and it is a property of the person rather
than of the tumour, which is why it sits on a different axis from stage and grade entirely
(**definitional**).

## Where it misleads, which is the half worth learning

**It is assigned, not measured.** So it carries observer variation, and the variation is not
symmetrical: recorded status is systematically better than the account the person gives of
themselves, and clinicians and patients disagree in that direction often enough that it is a known
property of the instrument rather than an anecdote (**consensus**).

**It conflates causes, and this is the most important point here.** The same category arises from
long-standing unrelated disease, from anaemia, from a drainable effusion, from uncontrolled
symptoms, from deconditioning after a hospital stay, and from the cancer. Some of those are
reversible. A poor performance status with a reversible contributor is **not** a reason to
withhold treatment until the reversible thing has been looked for, and treating the reading as the
finding is a common and consequential error (**mechanism**, **consensus**).

**It is a snapshot.** A value recorded at one visit says nothing about direction, and the
trajectory over weeks carries more information than the level does (**consensus**). A status
carried forward from a letter written a month ago is not a current reading, and is routinely used
as one.

**It misses whole domains.** Cognition, mood, nutrition, polypharmacy, social support and falls
are outside it by construction. That is the explicit rationale for structured geriatric assessment
in older adults, which identifies problems a performance status does not detect and changes
management in a substantial share of the people it is applied to (**consensus**, strongly
(**country-dependent**) in how and whether it is resourced).

**It has a ceiling and a floor.** At the top it cannot distinguish someone exceptionally fit from
someone just inside the top category, which matters because the top category is where intensive
regimens are considered. At the bottom it compresses states that are clinically very different.

**It is not regimen-specific, and it is used as though it were.** "Fit for treatment" is not a
property of a person alone; it is a property of a person and a particular regimen. A status that
is adequate for one schedule may not be adequate for another with different organ toxicity, and
the scale cannot express that (**mechanism**).

**As an eligibility criterion it biases the evidence.** Trials recruit predominantly from the
better categories, so the published evidence is weakest exactly where the decision is hardest — in
people whose status is poor, which is also where the potential for both benefit and harm is
largest (**consensus**). This is a structural feature of the literature and not a criticism of any
trial.

## What good use of it looks like

Short, and all of it follows from the above (**consensus**):

* Record **who** assigned it and **when**. It is an observation, and an observation without a
  source and a time is incomplete — the same point the nursing answer on documentation makes.
* Record the **person's own account** alongside it, because the two disagree in a known direction.
* Ask **why** it is what it is, and look for the reversible contributors, before it is used to
  exclude anything.
* **Reassess** rather than carry forward, and note the direction of travel.
* State the **question** rather than the number alone: fitness for a named regimen, not fitness in
  general.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is about an instrument, which is the right register for understanding what the
instrument can bear. It is not the register for being the person it is assigned to.

A performance status is recorded about somebody, usually without their knowing it has been
recorded, and it can decide whether a treatment is offered or a trial is mentioned. That is a
considerable amount of weight on a judgement made in a few seconds by someone who has met the
person once, often on a day that is not representative. People who are managing a great deal at
home with a great deal of effort are frequently recorded as more independent than they are,
because they describe themselves that way — out of pride, out of fear that treatment will be
withdrawn, or simply because the question was asked in a form that invited a brief answer.

The practical consequence is the one the section above puts last and should probably put first:
ask the person what their day actually looks like, and ask someone who lives with them if they are
there. That takes longer than assigning a category and it is how the category stops being wrong.

Nothing here is a statement about any individual's outlook, and no figure of any kind appears in
this answer. Both omissions are deliberate.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about what a functional assessment scale can and cannot bear, written
for someone already training in the field. It has had no clinical review. **No scale category,
cut-off, agreement statistic or eligibility threshold appears here, and none should be inferred**
— the category wording belongs to the body that publishes the scale, the thresholds at which
treatment is considered are regimen-specific and differ between countries and institutions, and
both are revised; the guidance and policy in force where you work are the authority, and this is
not. It is not an assessment tool and not a decision aid, it says nothing about anybody's outlook,
and it describes no individual's situation. Anyone affected by cancer — their own diagnosis or
someone else's — should be talking to the clinical team looking after that person, who have the
history and the examination, neither of which is here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The reasoning is structural and will not date: the trade between resolution and reproducibility,
the conflation of reversible with irreversible causes, and the gap between a snapshot and a
trajectory are properties of any coarse assigned ordinal and not of the current scales.

What moves is everything around it. Which scale is recorded, the thresholds at which particular
regimens are considered, and whether structured geriatric assessment is routine in older adults
are all set locally and nationally and are being revised, with geriatric assessment in particular
expanding unevenly. Patient-reported functional measures and objective activity data are being
investigated as complements or replacements and have no settled place. No category, cut-off or
figure is quoted here, deliberately: those belong to the publishing body and the current guidance
rather than to a revision note like this one.
