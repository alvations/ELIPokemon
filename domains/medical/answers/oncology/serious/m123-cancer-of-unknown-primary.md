# Nearly every instrument in the specialty is keyed on the organ, and the key is the thing missing

Oncology is organised by site of origin, and more completely than it looks. The staging
classifications are written one per disease site. The guidelines are indexed by site. The trials
recruited by site. Many drug authorisations name a site. The multidisciplinary meetings are
constituted by site, with the pathologist, the radiologist and the surgeon who deal in that organ.
Screening programmes are per site. Even the vocabulary is per site: a grade in one organ and the
same number in another are different statements, as this specialty's answer on staging against
grading sets out.

All of that is lookup machinery keyed on one field. **Cancer of unknown primary** is the situation
where that field is empty: a malignancy confirmed on histology, with evidence of spread, where the
site of origin is not identified after an adequate initial investigation (**definitional**, with
what counts as adequate strongly (**country-dependent**)).

The interesting part is not that this is difficult. It is that the machinery does not announce the
problem. Nothing downstream returns *unknown*. A guideline keyed on a site you do not have does
not refuse to answer; it is simply never opened, and the decision gets made by whatever is left.
So the discipline of this presentation is the discipline of noticing that you are reasoning
without the key, naming what is still readable, bounding the search, and recording that the
decision rested on a default.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: (**mechanism**) (derivable
from the structure of the reasoning), (**definitional**) (true because a classification says so),
(**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**) (varies by
nation, region or institution, and changes).

## What is still readable when the key is absent

The reasoning does not collapse. It changes basis, from the organ to a short list of things that
are measurable without it, and the order of that list is close to the order of importance.

**Lineage, from the morphology and the immunophenotype.** This is the first fork and it is the one
that changes everything. Whether the thing is a carcinoma, a lymphoma, a melanoma, a sarcoma or a
germ-cell tumour determines which treatment family is even relevant, and several of those are
highly treatable in a way the others are not (**mechanism**, **consensus**). A panel of
immunohistochemical stains is the instrument, interpreted as a pattern rather than as a set of
independent yes-or-no results.

**Subtype within carcinoma.** Adenocarcinoma, squamous carcinoma, neuroendocrine neoplasm and
poorly differentiated carcinoma behave differently and attract different treatment, and the
distinction is available from the specimen (**definitional**, **consensus**).

**The distribution of disease.** This is where the specialty's existing machinery still works.
This specialty's answer on how a cancer spreads derives organ-specific patterns from venous and
lymphatic drainage, and the argument runs backwards: a pattern of involvement constrains the set
of plausible origins, because whatever is first downstream of a site is where its cells arrive
(**mechanism**). Squamous carcinoma in upper cervical nodes, adenocarcinoma confined to one
axilla, disease confined to the peritoneum: each is a distribution before it is anything else.

**Grade, and the rate of change.** Both readable, both relevant to what is offered, neither keyed
on a site.

**Fitness.** Performance status sits on a different axis from the tumour entirely, which is
precisely this specialty's answer on performance status, and it is unaffected by the missing
field.

**Specific molecular alterations, where present.** This specialty's answer on biomarker-driven
treatment selection describes the small set of indications defined by an alteration irrespective
of the tissue of origin, and that is the one place where the missing key costs nothing at all
(**consensus**, strongly (**country-dependent**) in what is available and reimbursed).

Two further pieces of information are worth naming because they are easy to forget. **The specimen
itself is a limited sample**, with the limitations this specialty's answer on tumour heterogeneity
and the single biopsy sets out, and a panel run on too little material returns an equivocal
pattern rather than a wrong one. And **the clinical history** — previous excisions, a lesion
removed years ago and not followed up, previous imaging — occasionally supplies the field
directly, which is why it is asked for before the panel is extended (**consensus**).

## Why the workup is bounded rather than exhaustive

The instinct with a missing key is to keep searching, and it is the wrong instinct past a point.
The bound is not a resource argument. It is the same argument the rest of the specialty makes
about any test.

**Further investigation is justified only where its result would change management**
(**mechanism**). If the treatment offered would be the same whichever of three candidate origins
is correct, establishing which one costs time and buys nothing. The emergency-medicine answer on
imaging as a test with a harm of its own makes the general version of this point, and the
general-practice and oncology answers on overdiagnosis make the version about what additional
looking generates.

**The search has its own harms, and they are not hypothetical.** Repeated cross-sectional imaging
and endoscopy carry procedural risk and radiation exposure; they generate incidental findings that
then demand their own investigation; and all of it takes time during which no treatment is
happening (**mechanism**, **consensus**).

**So the recognised framing is a defined initial workup, after which the diagnosis is made.**
Cancer of unknown primary is a diagnosis reached after an adequate investigation rather than a
label for an incomplete one, and that distinction is the whole clinical content of the definition
(**definitional**). What constitutes adequate differs between countries and is set out in national
guidance — typically histology with an immunohistochemical panel, cross-sectional imaging, and
investigation directed by the pattern and by the lineage, with further tests added only where a
specific hypothesis makes them worth doing (**consensus**, **country-dependent**).

The practical discipline follows: decide in advance what each proposed test would change. A test
ordered because the primary has not been found yet, rather than because a named hypothesis
predicts its result, is the characteristic error of this presentation.

## The favourable subsets, and why they are the clinically important part

A minority of these presentations are not undifferentiated problems at all. They are recognisable
patterns that behave like a known disease, and are treated as that disease despite the absent
field (**consensus**). This is the part of the topic most worth learning, because the management
and the outlook differ substantially from the general case.

The mainstream list, as a current textbook would give it, is a list of **distributions plus
lineages**:

* squamous carcinoma confined to cervical nodes, managed along the lines of a head and neck
  primary;
* adenocarcinoma confined to axillary nodes in a woman, managed along the lines of a breast
  primary;
* adenocarcinoma confined to the peritoneum in a woman, managed along the lines of a tubal or
  ovarian primary;
* poorly differentiated carcinoma with a midline distribution in a younger person, where a
  germ-cell origin is considered and treatment follows that family;
* neuroendocrine neoplasms, which have their own treatment family independent of site;
* adenocarcinoma with bone metastases in a man, where a prostatic origin is considered and the
  relevant immunohistochemical and serum markers are checked;
* a single site of disease that is resectable or treatable with local therapy, where the local
  treatment is offered on its own merits.

Notice the structure. Every entry is *a pattern of spread combined with a lineage*, which is
exactly the two readable things from the section above. The subsets are not exceptions to the
reasoning; they are what the reasoning produces when the distribution is distinctive enough to
substitute for the missing field (**mechanism**).

Outside those subsets, treatment is empirical and chosen on lineage and fitness, and this is the
part of the topic where practice varies most and where the evidence is weakest (**consensus**,
**country-dependent**).

## The default that does not announce itself

This is the argument the rest of the answer exists to support.

When the organ field is absent, the instruments downstream do not return an error. A guideline is
not opened. A staging classification is not applied — the anatomical schemes require a primary to
measure, so what gets recorded is a description rather than a stage. A site-specific
multidisciplinary meeting is not the one that discusses it. And the record that results is a
perfectly ordinary-looking sentence: a histological description, a pattern of spread, and a
treatment.

**Nothing in that sentence distinguishes a decision made with the key from a decision made without
it** (**mechanism**). Which means three failure modes, all of them documented in practice:

* **An assumed origin hardens into a stated one.** A plausible hypothesis recorded once as a
  possibility reappears in the next letter without its hedge, and three letters later it is the
  diagnosis. This is the clinical-record argument the nursing answer on documentation makes, in
  its most consequential form.
* **The favourable subsets are missed**, because recognising one requires somebody to ask whether
  this distribution plus this lineage is a pattern, and nothing in the pathway prompts that
  question unless a person does.
* **The limits of the workup are not carried forward.** A later clinician cannot tell from the
  record whether the primary was looked for thoroughly and not found, or looked for briefly, and
  those are different situations with different implications for repeating anything.

The remedies are unglamorous and all consist of writing things down: record that the primary is
unidentified rather than naming a presumed one; record what was done to look for it; record the
immunohistochemical panel and what it did and did not support; and record whether the presentation
was assessed against the favourable subsets (**consensus**).

## Where tissue-of-origin profiling has and has not changed this

Honest statement of an unsettled area, because asserting either side would be wrong.

Molecular classifiers — gene-expression and methylation profiling — can assign a predicted tissue
of origin from a specimen, and they are used in some services (**consensus**,
(**country-dependent**)). Whether directing treatment according to that prediction improves
outcomes compared with empirical treatment chosen on lineage has been tested, and the results have
not settled the question; practice therefore differs substantially between countries and between
centres, and this is a live area rather than a resolved one (**consensus**,
**country-dependent**).

What has changed the reasoning more is the other route. Where a treatment is indicated by a
molecular alteration irrespective of the tissue it arose in, the missing field stops mattering for
that decision entirely — the inversion this specialty's answer on biomarker-driven treatment
selection describes. That is a genuine structural change: for a subset of these presentations,
broad molecular profiling is not an attempt to recover the key but a route that does not need it
(**consensus**, strongly (**country-dependent**)).

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is about reasoning with a missing field, which is the right register for learning
to do it. It is not the register for being the person it is about.

Being told that you have cancer and that nobody can say where it started is a specific and
unusually hard thing to be told. Almost everything a person has ever heard about cancer is
organised by organ: the word people are given is the handle for everything else — what to read,
which charity, which support group, which question to ask. Without it people are left holding a
diagnosis they cannot look up, and the commonest reaction is to assume that the not-knowing is a
failure of effort, either theirs or the team's. It is usually neither.

Two things help, and they are both about language. The first is saying explicitly that this is a
recognised situation with a name and a pathway, rather than an unfinished investigation — people
frequently believe tests are still pending when the diagnosis has in fact been reached. The second
is saying what the search was, what it ruled out, and why it stopped, because *we stopped looking*
sounds like giving up and *further tests would not change what we would offer* does not, and the
second is the accurate one when it is true.

The repeated biopsies and repeated scans are their own burden, and so is the number of different
clinicians a person meets when no single site-specific team owns the problem. Where an acute
oncology or unknown-primary service exists, having one named team is the main thing it provides,
and it matters more than its diagnostic yield.

Nothing in this answer says anything about what will happen to any individual, and no figure of
any kind appears in it. Both omissions are deliberate.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about diagnostic reasoning when the organising fact is missing, written
for someone already training in or qualified for the field. It has had no clinical review. **It is
not a diagnostic algorithm and must not be used as one**, and no immunohistochemical panel,
marker, threshold, imaging protocol or treatment regimen appears here, deliberately — the panels
belong to the pathology standard, the adequate workup is defined nationally and differs between
countries, and both are revised; the guideline and pathway in force where you work are the
authority and this is not. It says nothing about what will happen to any individual and describes
no individual's situation. Anyone affected by cancer — their own diagnosis or someone else's —
should be talking to the clinical team looking after that person, who have the histology, the
imaging and the history, none of which is here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument will not date: a specialty organised by one field behaves differently when
that field is empty, the instruments downstream fail silently rather than loudly, a pattern of
spread plus a lineage is what remains readable, and a test that cannot change management is not
worth doing. Those follow from the shape of the reasoning.

Two things are moving. The adequate initial workup is being revised as imaging and pathology
change, and national definitions of it have never agreed with each other. And the place of
molecular profiling is unsettled in both of its forms — as a route to a predicted tissue of
origin, where the evidence has not settled the question, and as a route to a treatment indicated
irrespective of tissue, which is expanding and is strongly dependent on what is reimbursed where
you are. The favourable subsets are the stable part of the topic and the empirical treatment of
everything else is the part most likely to look different in a few years. No panel, marker or
regimen is quoted here, deliberately: those belong to the current guidance rather than to a
revision note.
