# A decision process with a declared membership, whose output is a recommendation and not a decision

A cancer multidisciplinary meeting — a tumour board, depending on where you work — is not a
seminar and not a second opinion. It is a **decision process**: a defined membership, a prepared
list, the source material rather than reports of it, and an output that is recorded.

The commonest misunderstanding is that its value lies in the clinical reasoning. It does not. Most
of the reasoning in most cases could be done by any one of the people in the room. The value is in
the **structure**, and the structure is the answer to a list of specific, repeatable failures. The
useful way to learn it is backwards: name each failure, then look at which feature of the meeting
exists because of it.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: **mechanism** (derivable
from how the process works), (**definitional**) (true because a classification or standard says
so), (**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**) (varies by
nation, region or institution, and changes).

## Failure one: the option set shaped by the observer

The strongest single argument for the meeting. A clinician's assessment of what is possible is
conditioned by what they can do, and the effect is neither dishonesty nor incompetence — it is
what expertise does to a problem (**mechanism**).

Declaring the membership in advance is the control. It means the option set presented includes
surgery, systemic therapy, radiation therapy and, where relevant, the option of treating neither
the tumour nor a symptom yet — regardless of which specialty referred the case. In several
countries the required membership is specified in national standards rather than left to local
custom (**country-dependent**, (**consensus**) in principle).

## Failure two: deciding on a summary

A radiology report and a pathology report are summaries, written at a point in time, to answer the
question that was asked. The meeting reviews the **primary material**: the images on a screen with
a radiologist driving, the slides with a pathologist describing them.

The justification is empirical rather than theoretical. Specialist re-review of imaging and of
histology changes the assessment often enough — and changes staging and diagnosis often enough —
that it is done as a matter of course, which is a statement about measured discrepancy rates and
not about anybody's competence (**consensus**; the measured rates are disease-specific and are not
quoted here). It is also why a case referred from elsewhere with its reports but without its
images and blocks is frequently not reviewable.

## Failure three: deciding before the data exist

A meeting under time pressure, with a long list, is under structural pressure to produce something
for every case. The countermeasures are procedural: a list prepared in advance so the missing
results are known to be missing, and an explicit culture that **deferring is a legitimate output**
(**consensus**).

"Insufficient information to recommend; proceed to the following investigations and return" is a
complete and useful outcome. A guess recorded in the same field as a recommendation is not
distinguishable from a recommendation by anybody reading it later.

## Failure four: reasoning that is not written down

The record is the durable product. Who was present, what was recommended, what alternatives were
considered, what was uncertain, and what would change the recommendation — because the people who
act on it were mostly not in the room, and the person it concerns will be asking about it
(**mechanism**).

Two features of a good record are worth naming. It states the **basis**, so a later reader can
tell a recommendation that follows a guideline from one that departs from it. And it records when
a recommendation was **not** followed and why, which is the only way the meeting can learn
anything about itself (**consensus**).

## Failure five: people falling out of the pathway

Underrated, and arguably the function with the largest aggregate effect. The list is a register.
A named coordinator tracking which cases have been discussed, which are awaiting results, which
have been referred onward and which have not yet been given their recommendation turns the
meeting into a tracking instrument as well as a decision process (**consensus**, strongly
(**country-dependent**) in how it is resourced and recorded).

The same mechanism supports **trial screening**: a meeting that sees every new case in a disease
is the natural place for open studies to be checked against, which is one reason trial accrual
correlates with how the meeting is run rather than only with what is available (**consensus**).

## The contribution that only exists inside the meeting

Worth isolating, because it is the part most often treated as courtesy rather than function. Some
of what the meeting produces has no value anywhere else, and no value at all if it arrives late
(**mechanism**).

The radiologist's re-read, the pathologist's description of the slide, the specialist nurse's
account of what the person said at the last visit, the pharmacist's check on interactions with
existing medicines, the coordinator's knowledge that a result is outstanding: each of those
changes the recommendation if it is heard **before** the recommendation is formed, and changes
nothing if it is offered afterwards. They are also contributions that cannot be made by
correspondence, because they depend on being able to respond to what somebody else has just said.

Two practical consequences (**consensus**). A meeting that runs so fast that only the presenting
clinician speaks has lost these contributions without losing a single attendee, which is why
attendance and participation are measured separately where they are measured at all. And an input
that arrives after the recommendation is recorded has to be handled as a reason to revisit it,
which needs a route — a meeting with no mechanism for reopening a case is a meeting whose late
information is discarded.

## What the meeting cannot do, and what follows

The meeting has the disease. It frequently does not have the person (**mechanism**).

Fitness, other conditions, what matters to the individual, what they would accept and what they
have already declined are often absent or second-hand. A performance status recorded weeks ago by
someone else is a weak input and is routinely treated as a strong one — the subject of the next
answer in this specialty.

Three things follow, and they are the practical core of this topic (**consensus**):

* The output is a **recommendation**, to be taken to the person by someone who can discuss it, and
  the decision is made there.
* A recommendation can be declined, and a documented decision not to follow it is a normal
  outcome rather than a deviation to be explained away.
* Where the recommendation depends on something the meeting does not know, it should say so
  conditionally — recommending one thing if the person is fit for it and another if not is a more
  honest output than picking one and hoping.

## The meeting's own failure modes

Stated plainly, because an uncritical account of this topic is not useful (**consensus**):

* **Volume.** Time per case falls as the list grows, and a long list of brief discussions can be
  a worse instrument than a short list of real ones.
* **Dominance.** A chaired meeting where one voice or one discipline sets the frame has the
  structure of a multidisciplinary meeting without the function of one.
* **Rubber-stamping.** Confirming the referrer's plan is the default failure, and it is invisible
  in the record, because the record of a rubber-stamp and the record of a review look identical.
* **Absent disciplines.** A recommendation made without radiology or pathology present is a
  different kind of output and should be recorded as one.
* **Everything-by-default.** Reviewing every case with equal depth means the straightforward cases
  consume the time the difficult ones needed; some services triage the list for this reason, which
  has its own risks.
* **The evidence base.** That multidisciplinary review improves outcomes rests largely on
  observational and audit evidence, with the mechanisms above as the reasoning. It is a widely
  accepted model of care rather than a randomised finding, and saying so is more useful than
  overstating it.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is process, and process is the right register for understanding why the meeting
is built the way it is. It is not the register for being on the list.

Being discussed at a meeting one is not present at, by people one has mostly not met, is a strange
thing to be told about and a stranger thing to learn about afterwards. People are sometimes told
"your case is going to the MDT" with no account of what that is, and then wait — often over a
weekend — for a recommendation whose timing nobody has explained. Two things follow that cost
nothing: saying what the meeting is and when it sits, and saying who will make contact afterwards
and roughly when.

And the recommendation-versus-decision distinction is not administrative. It is the difference
between being told what has been decided about you and being offered what has been recommended for
you, and the second is the only one of those that leaves room for a person to say that they want
something else, or nothing yet, or more time. Where a recommendation is presented as settled, that
room disappears without anybody intending it to.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Name three failures the meeting exists to prevent, and the structural feature that answers each.
* Why does the meeting review images and slides rather than the reports?
* What is the difference between a recommendation and a decision, and where is each one made?
* "Insufficient information to recommend." Why is that a complete output rather than a failure?
* How would you tell, from the record, whether a case was reviewed or rubber-stamped?
* Why is the list also a tracking instrument, and what does that prevent?
* What kind of evidence supports multidisciplinary review, and what kind does not exist?

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about how a decision process is constructed, written for someone already
training in the field. It has had no clinical review. **No membership requirement, quorum,
discrepancy rate, timescale or target appears here, and none should be inferred** — those are set
nationally and locally, they differ between countries and institutions, and they are revised; the
standard and the terms of reference in force where you work are the authority, and this is not. It
is not a governance document and not a decision aid, and it describes no individual's case. Anyone
affected by cancer — their own diagnosis or someone else's — should be talking to the clinical
team looking after that person, who have the images, the histology and the history, none of which
are here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The reasoning is structural and should age well. A declared membership, review of source material,
a written record, a register that tracks people between steps, and an output that is a
recommendation rather than a decision are answers to failures that do not go away.

What moves is the regulation and the format. Required membership, quorum rules, which cases must
be discussed, what must be recorded and how quickly a recommendation must reach the person are set
nationally and are revised. The format is also changing: remote and hybrid meetings are now
ordinary in many services, triage of the list so that straightforward cases are not discussed in
full is being adopted unevenly, and the use of automated preparation and summarisation of the list
is an active area with no settled position. No requirement, interval or figure is quoted here,
deliberately: those belong to the current standard rather than to a revision note like this one.
