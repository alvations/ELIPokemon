# An anatomical map presupposes a located primary, and a disease of the marrow does not have one

A solid tumour oncologist and a haematologist describe their patients with words that sound like
each other and are not. *Stage.* *Response.* *Complete.* *Residual.* Each of those words is
defined by an instrument, the instruments are different in kind, and a term carried across without
its instrument becomes a confident statement about nothing in particular.

The root of it is one structural fact. The anatomical staging scheme — extent of a primary,
involvement of regional nodes, presence of distant deposits — requires there to be a primary with
a location and a boundary, a regional nodal field downstream of it, and a meaningful distinction
between *here* and *elsewhere*. This specialty's answer on staging against grading sets out what
the three categories encode and why they are recorded separately rather than summed.

For a malignancy arising in the bone marrow, none of those three has a referent. The marrow is one
organ distributed through the whole skeleton, so there is no *here*. The malignant cells are in
the circulation by nature rather than by escape, so *distant spread* is not an event that may or
may not have happened. And the regional nodal field of the marrow is not a concept. The same
argument applies with less force to a disease of the lymphatic system, which is a network rather
than a site.

So the vocabulary is not transferable, and the interesting question is what replaces it. The
answer is that each disease family builds its classification from whichever variables actually
carry information about it — and that the response vocabulary diverges even further than the
staging vocabulary does, because the haematological diseases have an instrument solid tumour
oncology does not have.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: (**mechanism**) (derivable
from what the measurement can reach), (**definitional**) (true because a classification says so),
(**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**) (varies by
nation, region or institution, and changes).

## Why an anatomical map has nothing to measure here

Worth doing properly, because "they just use different systems" is the answer that fails under
questioning.

The anatomical scheme is a **communication protocol over an extent** (**definitional**). It works
because extent is a real, ordered, partly observable quantity for a tumour that started in one
place: you can ask how far it has got, and the answer constrains what can be offered. Every piece
of the notation is doing that job — the prefix recording which measurement it was, the edition
recording which version of the stage groups applies.

Three things have to be true for the scheme to mean anything (**mechanism**):

* a **site of origin** with a boundary, so that extent is measurable at all;
* a **downstream nodal field**, so that regional involvement is a distinguishable state;
* a **distinction between local and distant**, so that the third category is a different
  biological statement from the second.

For a leukaemia, all three fail at once, and they fail for mechanistic reasons rather than
notational ones. The disease arises in a compartment that is everywhere the skeleton is; the
abnormal cells enter the blood as a consequence of what they are rather than as a step in a
cascade; and an organ which every vessel passes through has no *elsewhere*.

For a lymphoma the failure is partial, which is why lymphoma classification looks closer to
anatomical staging than leukaemia classification does. There are discrete involved sites, they can
be imaged and measured, and their number and distribution carry information. What does not
transfer is the ordering: a lymphoma at multiple distant sites is not in the same clinical
position as a carcinoma at multiple distant sites, and treating the anatomical category as if it
carried the same implication is the characteristic error (**mechanism**, **consensus**).

The consequence of a partial failure is worth stating on its own, because it is the position most
clinicians actually work in. For the lymphomas **both vocabularies apply at once**, and which one
carries a given decision is settled by what is in front of you rather than by a general rule. A
nodal mass is measurable in the way a carcinoma deposit is measurable, so the imaging-based
response categories are well formed for it. Marrow involvement in the same person is not
measurable that way, and the compartment vocabulary is the one that fits. Neither displaces the
other, both can be recorded for one person at one time, and an answer that picks one and applies
it to the whole disease will be wrong about part of it (**mechanism**, **consensus**).

## What the haematological classifications are built from instead

Each family built its own, from the variables that carry information about it. The pattern worth
learning is **which variable each one chose**, because the choice is the biology.

**Lymphomas** use a distribution scheme — the number and grouping of involved regions and their
relation to the diaphragm, with extranodal involvement recorded separately. The historical
framework is the Ann Arbor scheme and the version in current use is a modification of it; the
modifications and the role of metabolic imaging within them have changed and continue to
(**definitional**, **consensus**, (**country-dependent**)). Crucially, prognostic indices built
from clinical and laboratory variables sit alongside the stage and often carry more weight than it
does, which is a structural statement about how much information a distribution contains.

**The myelomas** are classified by markers of disease burden and of biology rather than by
anatomical extent, in frameworks maintained by an international working group and revised as the
evidence changes (**definitional**, **consensus**). The disease has a quantitative marker in the
blood or urine, which is unusual and consequential — this specialty's answer on tumour markers
sets out what such a molecule can and cannot do, and myeloma is one of the places where it does a
great deal.

**The chronic lymphocytic leukaemias** use frameworks built from blood counts, nodal and organ
involvement and marrow failure. Two long-standing schemes are in use and they are not identical,
which is itself the point: a classification is a protocol and two protocols over the same disease
can both be valid (**definitional**).

**The acute leukaemias are not staged at all**, and this is the sharpest fact in the topic. There
is no stage because there is nothing for a stage to order. What replaces it is classification by
lineage, morphology, immunophenotype, cytogenetics and molecular genetics, feeding a **risk
stratification** — which is a different kind of object from a stage: it is a grouping by expected
behaviour and by what treatment is indicated, built from the biology of the clone rather than from
where it has reached (**definitional**, **consensus**). Somebody who asks for the stage of an
acute leukaemia has asked a question with no referent, and the honest answer is to say so rather
than to supply an approximation.

## Response against remission: two words, two instruments

Here the divergence is larger than in the staging vocabulary, and it is almost entirely about what
each instrument can reach.

**Solid tumour response is a change in the size of named lesions on an imaging test.** This
specialty's answer on response assessment and surrogates sets out the structured criteria, what
they buy, and where information is lost at each link. The relevant feature for this answer is the
floor: complete response means *no visible lesion on an imaging test*, and an imaging test has a
resolution limit. The category is defined by the instrument's blind spot
(**definitional**, **mechanism**).

**Haematological remission is a state of a sampled compartment.** Complete remission in an acute
leukaemia is defined on a marrow sample — a proportion of blasts below a specified level, with
recovery of peripheral counts and absence of disease outside the marrow (**definitional**, with
the exact criteria set by the current classification and therefore (**country-dependent**) in
which version is in force). Note what kind of statement that is. It is not *nothing can be seen*;
it is *this sample meets this specification*.

Three consequences follow from the difference, and all three are mechanical.

**The words denote different depths of evidence.** "Complete" in one setting means below the
resolution of an imaging test applied to a lesion; in the other it means within a specification
applied to a sample. Those are not comparable amounts of residual disease, in either direction,
and treating them as the same word is how a reader comes away with a false equivalence
(**mechanism**).

**The sampling properties are opposite.** This specialty's answer on tumour heterogeneity and the
single biopsy sets out why one piece of one solid lesion is a sample of a population under
selection and is not representative. A marrow aspirate is a sample of a compartment whose contents
circulate and mix, which makes it *more* representative of the whole than a solid tumour biopsy is
— not perfectly, since marrow disease can be patchy and a trephine adds architectural
information an aspirate cannot, but the direction of the difference is the point (**mechanism**,
**consensus**).

**The schedules are different in kind.** A solid tumour is measured when it is imaged, and this
specialty's answer on response assessment makes the point that a measurement made on a schedule
inherits that schedule. A blood count is available whenever blood is taken, so for several
haematological diseases the burden can be tracked nearly continuously. That changes what the word
*progression* can mean (**mechanism**).

## The depth axis below complete, and why there is no solid tumour equivalent

The most important vocabulary in modern haematological malignancy has no counterpart on the solid
tumour side at all.

**Measurable or minimal residual disease** is disease detected below the level at which morphology
can see it, by applying a more sensitive instrument — multiparameter flow cytometry, or a
molecular assay for a disease-specific target — to the same sample. It is reported against a
stated sensitivity, because a negative result means *not detected at this sensitivity* and nothing
stronger (**definitional**, **mechanism**). In several diseases it directs treatment: it is used
to decide whether to intensify, to proceed to transplantation, or to stop (**consensus**, strongly
(**country-dependent**) in which assay, which threshold and which decision).

Two things are worth being precise about.

**It is a depth axis, not a better version of response.** Complete remission and residual-disease
status are two different readings of one sample by two instruments with different floors. The
coarser one is not wrong; it is bounded.

**Solid tumour oncology has no equivalent, and the reason is structural rather than
technological.** Residual-disease assessment needs a compartment you can sample representatively
and an assay with a floor below the coarse instrument's. For a solid tumour the compartment is the
body: there is no sample that stands for the whole of it, which is why a complete response on
imaging cannot be refined by a more sensitive look at the same specimen — there is no specimen
(**mechanism**).

What solid tumour oncology has instead is a different and newer attempt at the same axis, and it
is addressed in the next section rather than here because it is not settled.

## Where the two vocabularies are converging

Three places, and all three are live rather than resolved.

**Circulating tumour DNA as a residual-disease instrument for solid tumours.** Assays detecting
disease-derived DNA in blood after curative-intent treatment are an attempt to build the depth
axis that solid tumour oncology has never had, using the circulation as the samplable compartment.
Whether acting on such a result improves outcomes is under investigation and the place of these
assays in practice is not settled (**consensus**, **country-dependent**). If it settles, it would
import a haematological vocabulary into solid tumour oncology wholesale, which is worth stating
plainly as the direction of travel rather than as an established fact.

**Metabolic imaging in the lymphomas**, which brought an imaging-based response vocabulary into a
disease classified by distribution, and interim response assessment with it (**consensus**,
(**country-dependent**)).

**Molecular classification generally.** This specialty's answer on biomarker-driven treatment
selection describes indications defined by an alteration irrespective of the tissue of origin, and
the acute leukaemias were doing genomically-defined classification long before solid tumour
oncology was. The traffic in that direction has mostly been from haematology outward
(**consensus**).

What is not converging is the staging vocabulary. A disease of a distributed compartment will not
acquire a primary site, and the anatomical scheme will not acquire a way to describe one.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is about vocabulary and instruments, which is the right register for learning not
to carry a word across a boundary. It is not the register for being on the receiving end of the
words.

The words themselves do work in people's lives, and two of them do more harm than any of the
classification systems. **Remission** is heard by almost everybody as a synonym for *cured*, and
it is not one — it is a statement about a sample meeting a specification. The gap between what the
word means to the person using it and what it means to the person hearing it is one of the widest
in medicine, and closing it is a matter of saying what was measured rather than of choosing a
softer word. **Residual disease not detected** is heard as *gone*, and it means *not detected at
this sensitivity*. People are entitled to that qualifier, and they generally cope with it better
than clinicians expect.

The other human fact in this topic is that the two specialties feel different to be treated in.
Haematological treatment is frequently intensive, inpatient, prolonged and count-dependent, which
means long periods of being unwell in hospital away from home, isolation precautions, and a
rhythm of blood tests that organises the whole of life. Solid tumour treatment is more often
cyclical and outpatient. Neither is harder in general, and the comparison is not useful to anybody
going through either; what is useful is that the shape of the burden is different, and somebody
moving between the two — or supporting somebody in one while having experience of the other — is
not comparing like with like.

Nothing in this answer says anything about what will happen to any individual, and no figure of
any kind appears in it. Both omissions are deliberate.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about classification and response vocabulary, written for someone
already training in or qualified for the field. It has had no clinical review. **No classification
category, blast proportion, count threshold, assay sensitivity or response criterion appears here,
and none should be inferred** — those belong to the working group that maintains each set of
criteria, they differ between diseases and between the versions in force in different countries,
and they are revised; the classification and criteria in force where you work are the authority
and this is not. It is not a staging manual, not a reporting standard and not a decision aid, it
says nothing about what will happen to any individual, and it describes no individual's situation.
Anyone affected by cancer or by a blood disorder — their own diagnosis or someone else's — should
be talking to the clinical team looking after that person, who have the samples and the results,
neither of which is here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structural argument will not date. An anatomical scheme needs a located primary; a distributed
compartment does not have one; a sample that stands for its compartment permits a depth axis and a
sample that does not cannot; and a word is defined by the instrument that reports it.

Everything built on top of that is being revised, and some of it quickly. The classification of
haematolymphoid tumours is in active revision and the groupings have moved between recent
editions, with more than one competing current framework in circulation — which is itself a good
illustration of a classification being a protocol rather than a description. The response criteria
for each disease family are periodically updated. Residual-disease assessment is the
fastest-moving part: which assay, at what sensitivity, at which timepoint and what the result
triggers are all under revision and differ sharply between countries. And the attempt to build an
equivalent axis for solid tumours out of circulating tumour DNA is the thing most likely to make
this answer's central asymmetry look dated, which is worth saying rather than leaving for a reader
to discover. No category, threshold or criterion is quoted here, deliberately.
