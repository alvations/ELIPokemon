# Two axes, and the habit of reading one off the other

**Stage** describes how far a cancer has spread. **Grade** describes how abnormal its cells look
down a microscope. They are independent descriptions of the same disease, they are produced by
different people from different material, and the commonest error at this level is treating one as
a proxy for the other. A high-grade tumour can be anatomically early. A low-grade tumour can be
widely disseminated. Neither sentence is a paradox.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: **mechanism** (derivable
from how the biology or the system works), **definitional** (true because a classification says
so), **consensus** (widely agreed professional practice), or **country-dependent** (varies by
nation, region or institution, and changes).

## What stage actually encodes

The dominant anatomical framework describes the primary tumour, the regional lymph nodes and
distant spread as three separate categories, which are then combined into a stage group by rules
specific to each disease site (**definitional**). The three categories are not interchangeable:
nodal involvement and distant spread are different biological statements, which is exactly why
they are recorded separately rather than summed into one number.

Two features of the notation matter more than students expect (**definitional**):

* **A prefix says where the information came from.** Assessment before surgery, assessment from
  the resected specimen, and assessment after pre-operative systemic therapy are distinguished in
  the notation, because they are not the same measurement and must not be pooled.
* **The edition must be stated.** The classification is revised; stage groups move between
  revisions. A stage without its edition is an incomplete statement.

Several diseases are not staged anatomically at all. Lymphomas, the myelomas and the chronic
lymphocytic leukaemias use frameworks built around different variables, because an anatomical map
of a disease distributed through the marrow or the lymphatic system does not carry the information
the anatomical scheme was designed to carry (**consensus**).

## What grade actually encodes

Grading is an assessment of how far the tissue has departed from the normal tissue it arose from
(**mechanism**): architecture, nuclear appearance, and how much of it is visibly dividing.
Different organs have their own grading systems with their own inputs, and the systems are not
comparable across organs — a grade in one disease and the same number in another are different
statements (**definitional**). Prostate, breast, central nervous system tumours and soft-tissue
sarcomas each use a distinct scheme maintained by a different body (**consensus**).

Grade is a statement about *behaviour tendency*, assessed from appearance. That is its strength
and its limit: it is reproducible between pathologists to a useful degree, and it remains a proxy
for biology inferred from morphology.

## Why a staging system is a communication protocol

This is the part usually taught as an afterthought, and it is actually the point.

A staging system is **optimised for agreement, not for biological fidelity** (**mechanism**). Its
job is that two clinicians who have never met, in two countries, describing two different people,
mean the same thing by the same label — so that a multidisciplinary meeting can function, so that
a referral carries information, and so that two series are comparable at all. Every design choice
follows from that:

* **Categories are discrete** even though extent is continuous, because discrete categories can be
  agreed between observers and continuous ones cannot.
* **The inputs are things that can actually be observed** at the time of assessment, which is why
  the scheme is anatomical in origin rather than molecular.
* **It is versioned**, and the version travels with the label or the label means nothing.

The protocol has a known failure mode. When the *investigation* improves — a more sensitive
imaging modality, a more thorough nodal examination — people are reassigned to higher stage groups
without anything about their disease changing. Both the group they left and the group they joined
then look better than before, because the group they left has lost its most advanced members and
the group they joined has gained its least advanced ones (**mechanism**). This is a measurement
artefact with a long literature behind it, and it means stage-for-stage comparisons across an era
of changing imaging are comparing different things under the same labels.

## Where the two axes are deliberately being merged

Because anatomy alone under-predicts behaviour, some current schemes now fold grade and
tissue-based biomarkers into the stage group itself for particular diseases (**consensus**, with
**country-dependent** uptake). That is a reasonable response to a real limitation, and it does
blur the clean orthogonality this answer opened with. The honest position: the axes are
conceptually orthogonal, keeping them separate is what makes each one reproducible, and a scheme
that combines them is a third thing — a prognostic grouping, neither a stage nor a grade, and it
should be named as such when it is reported.

## What a population label does not tell an individual

A stage group and a grade are descriptions of a disease. They are not a forecast about a person. A
group statistic is an average over a large, varied population assembled at some point in the past,
under the treatments and the imaging of that time. It does not contain the information needed to
say what will happen to any individual within it, and any individual differs from the group
average in ways the group average has no way to express. That is a statement about what the
arithmetic can and cannot do, and it is why figures of that kind are not reproduced anywhere in
this answer.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Give an example of a high-grade, anatomically confined tumour, and one of a low-grade, widely
  spread one. If both are hard to produce, the two axes are still fused.
* Why are nodal and distant spread recorded separately rather than as one number?
* What breaks when the imaging improves but the staging system does not change?
* Why do the lymphomas not use the anatomical scheme?
* What is lost by merging grade into a stage group, as well as what is gained?

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about how two classification axes are constructed, written for someone
already training in the field. It has had no clinical review. It is not a staging manual, it is
not a decision aid, and it describes no individual's situation. Staging and grading systems differ
between countries and institutions and are revised; the edition in force where you work is the
authority, and this is not. Anyone affected by cancer — their own diagnosis or someone else's —
should be talking to the clinical team looking after that person, who have the specimen, the
images and the history, none of which are here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The structure described here — anatomical extent recorded separately from cellular appearance,
combined into stage groups by site-specific rules, versioned, with prefixes marking the source of
the information — is stable and has been for decades. What moves is the content: editions are
revised, stage groups are redrawn, and the merging of grade and biomarkers into prognostic groups
is an active direction of travel in several diseases rather than a settled one. No edition number,
threshold or disease-specific rule is quoted here, deliberately: those are exactly the facts that
go out of date, and they must be read from the classification currently in force rather than from
a revision note like this one.
