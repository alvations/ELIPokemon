# The same assay can be excellent for monitoring and useless for screening, unchanged

A **tumour marker** is a substance measurable in blood or another fluid that is produced by a
tumour, or by the host in response to one, and used as a proxy for whether disease is present or
how much of it there is.

The central fact about markers is that **usefulness is not a property of the assay alone**. It is
a joint property of the assay, the population it is applied to, and the decision it feeds. Change
the population from *one person known to have a marker-producing cancer* to *a large number of
well people*, and the same assay, with the same characteristics, unchanged in every respect, goes
from informative to actively harmful. Nothing about the molecule or the machine has to change for
that to happen.

Three separate arguments do the work, and they are independent of one another.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: **mechanism** (derivable
from the arithmetic or the biology), **definitional** (true because a convention says so),
**consensus** (widely agreed professional practice), or **country-dependent** (varies by nation,
region or institution, and changes).

## Reason one: the arithmetic of rarity

The proportion of positive results that are true positives depends on the prevalence of the
condition in the tested population as much as on the test's sensitivity and specificity
(**mechanism**). In a population where the disease is rare, the false positives drawn from the
very large well group outnumber the true positives drawn from the very small affected group, even
when the false-positive fraction is low.

This is the dominant reason markers fail as screening tests and it is worth being precise about
what kind of claim it is: **it is arithmetic, not a defect of any particular assay.** A better
machine does not fix it. A tighter threshold trades it for lost sensitivity. The only things that
genuinely move it are raising the prevalence in the tested group — which is what restricting
screening to a higher-risk population does — or following a positive with a second, independent
test whose errors are uncorrelated with the first.

And every false positive has consequences: further imaging, invasive investigation, and a period
of being treated as possibly having cancer. Those are the harms a screening programme is obliged
to count (**consensus**), and they are discussed at greater length in the screening answer in this
specialty.

## Reason two: the signal reflects bulk

Marker concentration generally rises with the amount of marker-producing tissue present
(**mechanism**). The consequence is uncomfortable and unavoidable: **sensitivity is lowest for
small-volume disease**, which is precisely the disease a screening programme exists to find. A
marker that reliably detects advanced disease and unreliably detects early disease has its
sensitivity in the wrong place for screening and exactly the right place for monitoring.

Two further properties follow from the same fact:

* **A marker that is produced by only some tumours of a given type cannot be used to exclude.** A
  normal result in that setting means either no disease or a non-producing tumour, and the two are
  not distinguishable (**mechanism**).
* **"Undetectable" is a statement about the assay's lower limit, not about the absence of the
  molecule.** Every assay has a floor, and a result at the floor is the floor's result
  (**definitional**). This matters most where a marker is used to detect recurrence after
  treatment intended to be curative.

## Reason three: specificity is biological

Most markers are neither cancer-specific nor organ-specific (**consensus**). They are raised by
benign disease of the same organ, by inflammation anywhere, by smoking, by pregnancy, by impaired
renal or hepatic handling, and sometimes by nothing that is ever identified. A raised result is
therefore a reason to ask a different question, not an answer.

There are genuine exceptions, and the pattern in them is instructive: they are markers that are
nearly disease-specific **in a defined context** (**consensus**).

* **Germ-cell tumours.** The markers are built into the staging and risk classification rather
  than bolted on, and are used diagnostically.
* **Thyroglobulin after total thyroidectomy.** The context is what makes it specific: the tissue
  that normally makes it has been removed.
* **The paraprotein in plasma cell disorders**, which is a product of the clone itself and is
  measured as such.

And one case that is genuinely contested rather than settled: **prostate-specific antigen** is
organ-specific and not cancer-specific, and the long argument about it is not about whether it
detects disease but about overdiagnosis and what follows a positive. Policy differs markedly
between countries (**consensus** that it is contested, firmly **country-dependent** in what is
offered).

## What markers are actually for

Monitoring, and the reason it works is that every one of the three arguments above is reversed
(**consensus**):

* **The population is one person already known to have marker-producing disease.** The prior is
  entirely different and the base-rate problem largely disappears.
* **The comparison is to that person's own earlier value**, not to a population threshold, so
  between-person variability stops mattering.
* **Changing bulk is exactly what is being asked about**, so the bulk dependence is the signal
  rather than the limitation.

Which brings in the practicalities that distinguish competent use from superstition:

* **A baseline before treatment is required**, or there is nothing to compare against.
* **Trends must be on the same assay and platform.** Different assays for nominally the same
  marker are not interchangeable, and a change of laboratory can look exactly like a change in
  disease (**consensus**).
* **The rate of fall after treatment carries information**, because the marker has a
  characteristic clearance; a slower fall than the molecule's own kinetics predict means
  something.
* **A change smaller than the biological and analytical variation is not a change.** Markers vary
  between measurements in a stable person, and a single value crossing a threshold is weaker
  evidence than a trend.
* **Interference exists.** Very high concentrations can read falsely low on some immunoassay
  formats, and heterophile antibodies can produce spurious results; an implausible number is a
  reason to speak to the laboratory (**consensus**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is arithmetic and assay behaviour, and that is the right register for deciding
what a test is for. It is not the register for what being tested is like.

Someone having a marker measured to monitor a known cancer is waiting for a number that they have
learned to read as a verdict, repeatedly, often every few weeks, for years. The waiting is its own
burden, it has a name in the literature and in common use, and it is not reduced by the number
being reassuring on any particular occasion. A rise that turns out to be variation still costs the
person the weeks in which they did not know that.

A false positive in a screening context is not a neutral event either. It is a period in which
somebody who was well is investigated for cancer, with everything that goes with that, and in some
people the anxiety does not fully resolve even after the result is clear. Counting that as a harm
is not a technicality; it is why a programme that finds more disease is not automatically doing
more good.

And no threshold or trend in any of this says what will happen to any individual. A marker is a
measurement of a molecule, not a statement about a person's future, and no figure of any kind
appears in this answer for that reason.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Why does the same assay, unchanged, perform well for monitoring and badly for screening? Answer
  without using the words sensitivity or specificity.
* What are the only two things that genuinely improve the proportion of positives that are true
  positives?
* Why is a marker's sensitivity being best in advanced disease exactly wrong for screening and
  exactly right for monitoring?
* Why can a marker produced by only some tumours of a type not be used to exclude disease?
* What does "undetectable" actually assert?
* Why must a trend be on one assay, and what does changing laboratory do to a series?
* Name a marker that genuinely is useful diagnostically, and say what makes it the exception.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about what a marker measures and what a result can support, written for
someone already training in the field. It has had no clinical review. **No reference interval,
threshold, detection limit, half-life, sensitivity, specificity or predictive value appears here,
and none should be inferred** — those are properties of a particular assay in a particular
laboratory, they are not transferable, and the laboratory handbook is the authority. This is not a
guide to interpreting anybody's result, and it describes no individual's situation. Screening
policy differs by country and is revised; yours governs. Anyone affected by cancer — their own
diagnosis or someone else's — should be talking to the clinical team looking after that person,
who have the results, the history and the context, none of which are here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The arithmetic is permanent. The dependence of predictive value on prevalence, the bulk dependence
of marker concentration, and the non-specificity of most markers are structural and will not
change, and they are the reason the general position — markers for monitoring, not for population
screening — has been stable for a long time (**consensus**).

What is moving is the technology rather than the logic. Multi-cancer early detection assays based
on circulating tumour DNA and related analytes are under active evaluation, and the arguments
above apply to them unchanged: in a low-prevalence population, predictive value is governed by
prevalence, and detecting something earlier is not the same as doing more good. Whether any of
them earns a place in a screening programme is an open question as of this date and is being
answered differently in different countries. No performance figure for any such assay is quoted
here, deliberately. Those figures are the facts most likely to date, they are contested, and they
belong to the primary literature and the programme documentation rather than to a revision note
like this one.
