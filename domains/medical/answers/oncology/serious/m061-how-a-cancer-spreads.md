# A short list of routes, and a destination decided by plumbing first and permissiveness second

A cancer spreads by a small number of routes — direct extension, lymphatics, the bloodstream,
across a body cavity, and along nerves — and the destinations it reaches are strikingly
reproducible for a given primary site. Explaining that reproducibility takes **two separate
arguments**, and the commonest error at this level is offering one of them and stopping.

The first is mechanical: a cell leaving a tumour goes where the drainage goes, and arrives at the
first filter downstream. The second is biological: arriving somewhere is not the same as being
able to live there. Neither argument alone matches what is observed. Together they do.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: **mechanism** (derivable
from how the biology or the anatomy works), (**definitional**) (true because a classification says
so), (**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**) (varies by
nation, region or institution, and changes).

## The mechanical argument: whatever is first downstream

Follow the venous drainage and a large part of the observed pattern falls out without any biology
(**mechanism**).

Blood leaving the gastrointestinal tract and the other portal-drained viscera goes to the **portal
vein**, so the **liver** is the first capillary bed it meets. Blood leaving almost everywhere else
goes into the systemic veins, through the right heart, and the **lung** is the first bed it meets.
Past that bed the outflow is arterial and goes everywhere, which is why the lung and the liver are
so often the first site involved and why involvement beyond them tends to follow rather than
precede. The standard mechanical account of spread to the axial skeleton invokes the valveless
venous plexuses around the vertebral column, which offer a route that bypasses the usual sequence
(**consensus** on the anatomy, (**mechanism**) for the inference).

Lymphatic spread is the same kind of argument in a different vessel. Each site drains to a
particular group of nodes, in a particular order, and that is why a **regional node field** is a
defined anatomical object rather than a vague one, why involvement tends to be sequential rather
than scattered, and why sentinel node mapping works at all: it is an attempt to find the first
node in that site's own drainage and let it stand for the field (**consensus**).

## Where the mechanical argument runs out

Blood flow alone over-predicts some organs and under-predicts others, and the mismatch is the
reason the second argument exists.

**Skeletal muscle receives a large share of cardiac output and is an uncommon site of metastasis;
the spleen is likewise uncommon** (**consensus**). Conversely, several cancers deposit
overwhelmingly in organs that are not first downstream of them — the strong preference of uveal
melanoma for the liver is the usual teaching example, and the preference of breast and prostate
cancer for bone is the commonest (**consensus**).

So the standing account has two parts, and the second is the **seed-and-soil** argument: a cell
that arrives must adhere, survive, extravasate, and then find a local environment that supports
it. That environment is organ-specific, and the molecular vocabulary for it includes adhesion
molecules, chemokine receptor and ligand pairing, the local stromal and immune context, and the
behaviour of resident cells of the target tissue (**mechanism**, with the specifics an active
research area rather than settled). The honest summary: flow decides what arrives, the target
tissue decides what stays, and both are needed.

## The cascade is lossy at every step

Metastasis is rare per cell and common per cancer, and those two facts are reconciled by the
number of steps (**mechanism**).

A cell must breach the basement membrane and local stroma, enter a vessel, survive in circulation
against shear and loss of adhesion and immune surveillance, arrest somewhere, leave the vessel,
survive in a foreign tissue, and then start dividing again. Each step removes most of what reached
it, so the joint probability of all of them is the product of several small numbers and is tiny.
Two consequences follow with no further observation:

* **Cells in the circulation are far commoner than metastases.** Detecting them is therefore not
  the same as detecting metastatic disease, which is the central difficulty in interpreting any
  circulating-cell or circulating-DNA assay (**consensus**).
* **Dormancy and late recurrence are expected, not anomalous.** The last step — resuming
  proliferation in a new tissue — can be delayed, and in some diseases is delayed by many years.
  That is why follow-up in those diseases is long, and why a disease-free interval is not proof
  that the cascade was never completed.

## What the pattern buys clinically

* **Staging investigation is directed rather than exhaustive.** What is imaged, and how, is chosen
  from the known pattern for that primary site rather than by looking everywhere (**consensus**,
  with (**country-dependent**) protocols).
* **The nodal field is an anatomical object and is handled as one** — sampled, mapped or treated
  as a field, by site-specific rules.
* **A cancer of unknown primary is worked up from the pattern of spread**, because the pattern
  carries information about origin (**consensus**).
* **A limited-volume metastatic pattern is managed differently from a widespread one in several
  diseases**, and whether that is offered is strongly (**country-dependent**).

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is anatomy and probability, and that is the right register for understanding how
spread happens. It is a poor register for what finding it means to anybody.

A report describing spread beyond the primary site is, for the person it is about, one of the
hardest things they will be told, and it changes the whole shape of what follows. Nothing in a
mechanistic account speaks to that. Being able to predict which organ a particular cancer tends to
reach is a different skill from being any use to someone who has just learned that it has, and the
second is harder and matters more.

Nor does a pattern of spread settle what will happen to an individual. The distribution of sites
is a population regularity; one person's course depends on their disease, their treatment, what is
available where they live, everything else going on in their body, and their own choices. That is
why no figure of any kind appears in this answer, and the absence is deliberate.

## What an examiner digs into next

* Why is the liver the commonest first site for colorectal cancer and the lung for a limb sarcoma?
  Answer from the venous drainage, not from a list.
* Skeletal muscle has a large blood supply and few metastases. What does that tell you about the
  sufficiency of a flow-based explanation?
* Why does sentinel node mapping work, and what anatomical assumption does it rest on?
* Why are circulating tumour cells much commoner than metastases, and what does that do to the
  interpretation of an assay that detects them?
* Why is late recurrence predicted by the cascade model rather than being an exception to it?
* Which routes of spread does an anatomical nodal field fail to describe at all?

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about routes and patterns of spread, written for someone already
training in the field. It has had no clinical review. It is not a staging protocol, it is not a
diagnostic aid, and it describes no individual's situation. No threshold, interval, proportion or
survival figure appears here and none should be inferred. Which investigations are done for which
primary site differs by country, region and institution and is revised; the protocol in force
where you work is the authority, and this is not. Anyone affected by cancer — their own diagnosis
or someone else's — should be talking to the clinical team looking after that person, who have the
imaging, the pathology and the history, none of which are here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The anatomical half of this answer is as stable as anatomy: the routes, the drainage, the
first-bed-downstream argument and the regional node fields have been described the same way for
many decades and will not move. The seed-and-soil half is stable as a principle and active as a
research programme — which adhesion molecules, which chemokine pairs, which features of a target
niche matter, and how much any of them can be intervened on, is genuinely unsettled, and no
specific molecule is asserted here as established for that reason.

What moves fastest is the clinical periphery: what circulating tumour DNA is used for, how
limited-volume metastatic disease is defined and treated, and which staging investigations are
protocolised for which site. Those are (**country-dependent**) and under revision, and they belong
to the current local protocol rather than to a revision note like this one.
