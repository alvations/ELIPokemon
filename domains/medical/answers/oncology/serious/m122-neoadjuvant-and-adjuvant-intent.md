# Before surgery the treatment is also a measurement; after surgery it is only a wager

Take one drug, one dose, one schedule. Give it before the operation and it is **neoadjuvant**.
Give it after, and it is **adjuvant**. The pharmacology is identical. The toxicity profile is
identical. And the two placements answer different questions, are justified by different
arguments, produce different information, and fail in different ways.

The reason is not pharmacological. It is informational, and it is almost entirely about what is
present to be measured at the moment the drug acts. Before the operation there is a tumour in the
body: something with a size, a position, and a response that can be observed. After the operation
there is not. What the adjuvant drug is aimed at is disease that is **presumed** rather than
demonstrated — cells that may or may not have left before the primary came out — and the defining
feature of that target is that nobody can see whether it was there or whether it has gone.

So the same intervention is, in one placement, simultaneously a treatment and a test. In the
other it is a treatment with no test attached. That asymmetry generates everything else, including
the parts that look like trivia: why the staging notation records which it was, why one of them
has a per-patient readout and the other has only a population argument, and why the trial
literatures are shaped differently.

Each load-bearing claim below is marked with the kind of thing it is: (**mechanism**) (derivable
from the structure of the problem), (**definitional**) (true because a classification says so),
(**consensus**) (widely agreed professional practice), or (**country-dependent**) (varies by
nation, region or institution, and changes).

## Adjuvant: treating a probability, with no readout available

Adjuvant treatment rests on one biological claim: by the time a primary tumour is large enough to
be found, cells may already have left it, and some of those cells may be capable of founding a
metastasis later (**mechanism**). This specialty's answer on how a cancer spreads sets out why the
cascade is lossy at every step, which is the reason the word is *may* in both halves of that
sentence.

Three consequences follow, and all three are structural.

**There is no target you can point at.** The disease being treated is below the detection limit
of every available instrument, or it is not there at all. Nobody can tell which, for any
individual, at the time of the decision (**mechanism**).

**So there is no per-patient readout, ever.** Nothing shrinks. The only observable is whether the
cancer returns, and a single person's outcome is one draw: a person who takes adjuvant treatment
and does not recur may have been cured by the operation alone, and a person who recurs may have
had their recurrence delayed. Neither case is interpretable. This is the same structure as the
general-practice argument about an intervention that prevents many cases across a population while
offering almost nobody a benefit they could notice (**mechanism**).

**Therefore the justification can only be a randomised comparison.** Adjuvant therapy is one of
the places in medicine where a randomised trial is not the best available evidence but the only
possible evidence, because the counterfactual is unobservable in principle and not merely in
practice (**consensus**). This specialty's answer on randomisation against registries sets out why
no amount of adjustment substitutes, and the argument applies here with unusual force.

The clinical shape that falls out of this is familiar and is worth deriving rather than
remembering. Benefit is expressed as a reduction in the risk of recurrence across a population,
and the absolute size of that reduction depends on the baseline risk — so the same relative
benefit is worth a great deal to someone at high risk and very little to someone at low risk
(**mechanism**). That is why so much adjuvant decision-making consists of estimating baseline risk
from stage, grade and biomarkers, and why tools that refine that estimate change practice.

And the cost is paid up front, with certainty, by everybody treated. Toxicity is not probabilistic
in the way the benefit is. This specialty's answer on why toxicity is predictable sets out which
tissues and when, and its late-effect counterpart sets out the consequences that arrive years
later. All of that is incurred by people who were, in a proportion that nobody can identify,
already cured.

## Neoadjuvant: the same drug, and an answer you cannot otherwise get

Move the same treatment in front of the operation and four things become available.

**Downstaging, and an operation that was not previously offerable.** Where the limiting problem is
local extent — involvement of a structure that cannot be resected, or a margin that cannot be
obtained — shrinking the tumour can convert an inoperable situation into an operable one
(**consensus**). This is the oldest and least controversial rationale.

**A smaller operation for the same result.** Where surgery is possible but extensive, response can
permit a less destructive procedure: breast conservation instead of mastectomy, sphincter
preservation, a smaller nodal dissection in some settings (**consensus**, with the specific
indications strongly (**country-dependent**)).

**An in-vivo test of sensitivity.** This is the informational payoff and it has no adjuvant
equivalent. The tumour is exposed to the drug while it can still be observed, so the degree of
response is measurable — on imaging during treatment, and definitively in the resected specimen
afterwards (**definitional**). A tumour that has largely or entirely disappeared from the specimen
and one that is unchanged are two different pieces of information about the same person, obtained
by the same treatment, and neither is obtainable after the primary has gone.

**A decision that can use it.** The reason the test matters is that something downstream depends
on it. In several diseases the pathological response now determines what is offered after surgery,
with further or different systemic treatment given where the response was poor (**consensus**,
and this is one of the fastest-moving areas in the specialty, so it is strongly
(**country-dependent**) and dated). A test with no action attached would be interesting and not
useful — the same argument this specialty's answer on biomarker-driven treatment selection makes
about an assay and the drug it selects for.

There is also an argument about guaranteed delivery that cuts the other way from the usual
intuition. Treatment given before surgery is given to somebody who has not yet had an operation,
so it is more likely to be delivered at full intensity than treatment offered to somebody
recovering from major surgery (**consensus**). Adjuvant treatment is frequently delayed, reduced
or declined for reasons that have nothing to do with the cancer.

## What the measurement costs

A test that changes the thing being tested is not free, and three costs are real.

**Time passes with the primary in place.** Most tumours treated this way respond or remain stable.
A minority progress during treatment, and in a situation where an operation was available at the
start, progression can remove it (**mechanism**, **consensus**). That risk is the central argument
against the approach in any disease where the operation alone would have been curative in most
people.

**The specimen is no longer the specimen you would have examined.** Pathological assessment after
systemic therapy is assessment of treated tissue. Extent is altered, nodes may have been cleared,
and the tissue may contain fibrosis where tumour was. This is exactly why the staging notation
distinguishes assessment before treatment, assessment of the resected specimen, and assessment of
a specimen resected after pre-operative systemic therapy — this specialty's answer on staging
against grading makes the point that a prefix says where the information came from and that the
three must not be pooled (**definitional**). A writer who pools them is comparing populations that
were measured by different instruments.

**The pre-treatment information has to be gathered more carefully, and sometimes it cannot be.**
Because the operation will no longer establish the baseline, everything that the untreated
specimen would have shown must be obtained from biopsy and imaging first — which means a smaller
sample, interpreted with the limitations this specialty's answer on tumour heterogeneity and the
single biopsy describes, standing in for a whole resection (**mechanism**).

## Where the two placements are genuinely equivalent, and where they are not

The honest position is that this depends on the disease and the question, and asserting a general
answer would be wrong.

In several diseases, trials comparing the two placements of the same regimen have found similar
long-term outcomes, with the neoadjuvant placement offering surgical and informational advantages
rather than a survival advantage (**consensus**). In those settings the choice is made on the
surgical question and on the value of the response information, which is a legitimate basis for a
decision and not a fudge.

In others the two are not interchangeable at all. Where the standard regimen differs before and
after surgery, where the operation itself is curative in most people, or where the disease
responds poorly enough that the waiting is a real hazard, the argument is specific to the disease
and not derivable from first principles (**consensus**, **country-dependent**).

What does generalise is the asymmetry of information, and that is the thing worth carrying between
diseases.

## What this does to the trial literature

Two effects, and both are worth knowing because they shape what the evidence can tell you.

**Adjuvant trials are long and large.** The endpoint is recurrence or survival in a population
most of whom will not recur, so the trial must be big and must be followed for years. The
consequence is that an adjuvant question takes a long time to answer and the answer arrives about
a treatment landscape that has since changed (**mechanism**, **consensus**).

**Neoadjuvant trials offer a much earlier endpoint, and it is a surrogate.** Pathological response
is available at the time of surgery rather than years later, which makes it enormously attractive
as a trial endpoint. It is also a surrogate, and this specialty's answer on response assessment
and surrogates sets out the general problem: a treatment effect on a surrogate does not transfer
to the clinical endpoint automatically, and the strength of the relationship is itself an
empirical question that has to be established separately in each disease (**consensus**). The
degree to which pathological response predicts long-term outcome differs substantially between
diseases and between subtypes of one disease, and that is a live area rather than a settled one.

## The human stakes, said plainly

Everything above is about information and sequencing, which is the right register for
understanding why two identical treatments are not the same treatment. It is not the register for
being the person in the chair.

Somebody being offered adjuvant treatment is being asked to accept certain harm now for a benefit
nobody will ever be able to confirm they received. That is a genuinely difficult thing to be told,
and it is frequently told badly — in relative terms that sound larger than the absolute change, or
in a form that implies the treatment is a continuation of the operation rather than a separate
decision with its own balance. People are entitled to the absolute numbers for their own
situation, and they are entitled to decline. Someone who declines adjuvant treatment has not made
a mistake; they have weighted a trade differently.

Somebody being offered neoadjuvant treatment is being asked to wait, with a cancer in their body
that they know is there, while treatment that may or may not be working happens first. The waiting
is the hard part, and it is not made easier by the explanation that it is informationally
efficient. The question people actually ask — *what if it grows while we wait* — is the right
question, is not neurotic, and deserves a direct answer about what is monitored and what would
happen if it did.

Neither of these conversations is improved by precision about mechanism. They are improved by
saying what is known, what is not known, what the person's own numbers are, and that the decision
is theirs. Nothing in this answer says anything about what will happen to any individual, and no
figure of any kind appears in it. Both omissions are deliberate.

## Scope and safety

This is revision material about treatment intent and sequencing, written for someone already
training in or qualified for the field. It has had no clinical review. **No regimen, dose,
interval, response threshold or sequencing recommendation for any disease appears here, and none
should be inferred** — the sequencing question is answered disease by disease, the answers differ
between countries and between institutions, and they are being revised quickly; the guideline and
protocol in force where you work are the authority and this is not. It is not a decision aid, it
says nothing about what will happen to any individual, and it describes no individual's situation.
Anyone affected by cancer — their own diagnosis or someone else's — should be talking to the
clinical team looking after that person, who have the history, the pathology and the imaging, none
of which is here.

## Where this stands, October 2026

The informational argument will not date. A treatment given while the tumour is present produces a
measurement and a treatment given afterwards does not; a target that cannot be seen can only be
studied at population level; a test that alters the specimen is not free. Those are properties of
the structure of the problem.

Almost everything else here is moving, and in one direction: towards the neoadjuvant placement in
more diseases, and towards response-adapted treatment after surgery. What a poor pathological
response triggers has changed substantially in several diseases within the past few years and will
change again. The use of circulating tumour markers of residual disease to give adjuvant treatment
a readout it has never had is an active area with no settled place in practice, and if it becomes
routine it would alter the central asymmetry this answer is built on — which is worth saying
plainly rather than leaving for a reader to discover. No regimen, threshold or indication is
quoted here, deliberately: those belong to the current guideline rather than to a revision note.
